2.1 — All Formulas & Quantitative Relations (with symbols & units)

1. Shaft Design (Torsion & Combined Loading)

  • Torsional Shear Stress (Solid Shaft):
    $${\boxed{\tau = \frac{16 \cdot T}{\pi \cdot d^3} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$
  • $T$: applied twisting moment / torque $\text{N}\cdot\text{mm}$

  • $d$: solid shaft diameter $\text{mm}$

  • Torsional Shear Stress (Hollow Shaft):

    $${\boxed{\tau = \frac{16 \cdot T}{\pi \cdot d_o^3 \cdot (1 - k^4)} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$

  • $d_o$: outer diameter $\text{mm}$
  • $d_i$: inner diameter $\text{mm}$

  • $k$: ratio of diameters $k = \frac{d_i}{d_o}$

  • Equivalent Twisting Moment $T_e$:

    $${\boxed{T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2} \quad (\text{N}\cdot\text{mm})}}$$

  • $M$: bending moment $\text{N}\cdot\text{mm}$

  • $T$: torsional moment $\text{N}\cdot\text{mm}$

  • Equivalent Bending Moment $M_e$:

    $${\boxed{M_e = \frac{1}{2} \left[ M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2} \right] \quad (\text{N}\cdot\text{mm})}}$$

  • Maximum Shear Stress under Combined Loading $\tau_{\text{max}}$:

    $${\boxed{\tau_{\text{max}} = \frac{16 \cdot T_e}{\pi \cdot d^3} = \frac{16}{\pi \cdot d^3} \sqrt{M^2 + T^2} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$

2. Square Key Design & Stress Relations

  • Torque Transmission (Shear Failure):
    $${\boxed{T = \frac{d}{2} \cdot (l \cdot w \cdot \tau) \implies \tau = \frac{8 \cdot T}{d \cdot l \cdot w} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$
  • $w$: key width ($\text{mm}$, standard $w = \frac{d}{4}$)

  • $l$: key length ($\text{mm}$, standard $l \ge 1.5d$)

  • Torque Transmission (Crushing Failure):

    $${\boxed{T = \frac{d}{2} \cdot \left(l \cdot \frac{t}{2} \cdot \sigma_c\right) \implies \sigma_c = \frac{8 \cdot T}{d \cdot l \cdot t} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$

  • $t$: key thickness ($\text{mm}$, standard $t = \frac{d}{4}$)

  • Square Key Stress Relationship:

    $${\boxed{\sigma_c = 2 \cdot \tau}}$$

  • Crushing stress is EXACTLY TWICE the shear stress for a square key $w = t$.

3. Cotter Joint Proportions

  • Thickness of Cotter $t$:
    $${\boxed{t \approx 0.3 \cdot d \text{ to } 0.4 \cdot d \approx 0.31 \cdot d \quad (\text{mm})}}$$

  • $d$: diameter of co-axial rod $\text{mm}$

  • Width of Cotter $b$:

    $${\boxed{b \approx 1.3 \cdot d \text{ to } 1.5 \cdot d \quad (\text{mm})}}$$

  • Cotter Taper:

    $${\boxed{\text{Taper} = \frac{1}{24} \text{ to } \frac{1}{48} \quad (\text{provided on ONE SIDE only})}}$$

4. Couplings & Misalignment Selection

  • Muff / Sleeve Coupling Design:
    $${\boxed{\tau_{\text{sleeve}} = \frac{16 \cdot T}{\pi \cdot D^3 \cdot (1 - k^4)} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$
  • Outer diameter $D \approx 2d + 13 \text{ mm}$, inner diameter $d$. Sleeve designed as a hollow shaft.

2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Hollow Shaft Superiority: A hollow shaft has a higher polar section modulus $Z_p$ than a solid shaft of the same mass and material, making it stronger in torsion and lighter in weight.
  • Equivalent Twisting Moment $T_e$: The single fictitious torque which, acting alone, produces the same maximum shear stress as the combined bending moment $M$ and torque $T$.
  • Equivalent Bending Moment $M_e$: The single fictitious bending moment which, acting alone, produces the same maximum normal (bending) stress as the combined bending moment $M$ and torque $T$.
  • Woodruff Key: A semi-circular key fitted into a matching semi-circular keyway in a tapered shaft. It is self-aligning and accommodates shaft taper automatically.
  • Feather Key: A parallel key fixed to either the shaft or hub that permits axial sliding of the hub along the shaft while transmitting torque.
  • Kennedy Key: A heavy-duty keying system comprising two square keys placed at $90^\circ$ to each other, fitted along diagonals.
  • Saddle Key: A key fitted into a keyway on the hub only, resting flat or curved against the un-keywayed shaft surface; transmits torque purely by friction.
  • Cotter Joint: A rigid temporary fastening used to connect two co-axial rods subjected to axial tensile or compressive forces.
  • Oldham's Coupling: A flexible coupling consisting of two flanges and a central floating disc with perpendicular tongue projections, used to connect parallel shafts with a small offset/distance between their axes.
  • Hooke's (Universal) Joint: A flexible joint featuring a central four-armed spider/cross that connects two shafts intersecting at a large variable angle (e.g. automobile driveshafts).

2.3 — Standard Values & Physical Constants

  • Square Key Stress Ratio: $\sigma_c / \tau = 2.0$.
  • Standard Key Width & Thickness: $w = d/4$, $t = d/4$ (square key).
  • Cotter Taper Range: $1/24$ to $1/48$ (one side).
  • Cotter Thickness: $t \approx 0.31d$.
  • Kennedy Key Angle: $90^\circ$ between keys.

SECTION 3: MAIN BODY

3.1 Shaft Design (Solid, Hollow & Combined Loading)

1. Concept Pointers
  • Solid vs Hollow Shafts:
  • For the same mass and material, a hollow shaft transmits significantly MORE TORQUE than a solid shaft.
  • Reason: Material in a hollow shaft is concentrated farther from the neutral axis, maximizing polar moment of inertia $J$ and polar section modulus $Z_p = J/r$.
  • Applications: Aircraft propeller shafts, automobile drive shafts, ship power transmission.
  • Combined Bending & Torsion:
  • When a shaft experiences both bending moment $M$ and torque $T$:
    • Maximum Shear Stress Theory (Guest/Tresca):
      $${T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2} \implies \tau_{\text{max}} = \frac{16 T_e}{\pi d^3}}$$
    • Maximum Normal Stress Theory (Rankine):
      $${M_e = \frac{1}{2}\left[M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}\right] \implies \sigma_{\text{max}} = \frac{32 M_e}{\pi d^3}}$$
  • Mechanical Fuse Role:
  • In a shaft-key-hub assembly, the KEY is designed to be the weakest member so it shears first during unexpected torque overloads, protecting expensive shafts and gears from damage.
💡 Key Concept: Hollow Shaft Efficiency: A hollow shaft is structurally superior to a solid shaft of equal weight because its higher polar section modulus $Z_p$ provides higher torsional strength and stiffness per unit mass.

[!TRAP] Equivalent Moment Formulas: - Equivalent Twisting Moment $T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}$. - Equivalent Bending Moment $M_e = \frac{1}{2}[M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}]$. Do not mix up $T_e$ and $M_e$ when calculating shear vs normal stresses!

2. Decision Tree
Shaft Strength & Design Check?
├─ Is question asking for shear stress under Combined M & T? → Calculate Te = sqrt(M^2 + T^2)
├─ Is question asking for bending stress under Combined M & T? → Calculate Me = 0.5 * [M + sqrt(M^2 + T^2)]
├─ Is mass equal for Solid vs Hollow shaft? → HOLLOW SHAFT IS STRONGER & LIGHTER
└─ Which member acts as a safety fuse in shaft-key-hub assembly? → KEY
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $\tau = \frac{16T}{\pi d^3}$ (solid).
  • [ ] $\tau = \frac{16T}{\pi d_o^3 (1-k^4)}$ (hollow).
  • [ ] $T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}$.
  • [ ] $M_e = \frac{1}{2}[M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}]$.
  • [ ] Key is weakest component.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A solid shaft is subjected to a bending moment $M = 3.46 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$ and a twisting moment $T = 11.5 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Calculate the equivalent twisting moment $T_e$ and equivalent bending moment $M_e$. - Solution: - Calculate equivalent twisting moment $T_e$:

$${T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2} = \sqrt{(3.46)^2 + (11.5)^2} = \sqrt{11.9716 + 132.25} = \sqrt{144.2216} \approx 12.0 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}}$$
- Calculate equivalent bending moment $M_e$:
$${M_e = \frac{1}{2} \left[ M + T_e \right] = \frac{1}{2} [3.46 + 12.0] = \frac{15.46}{2} = 7.73 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}}$$
- Key Insight: $T_e = 12.0 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$, $M_e = 7.73 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$.

Worked Example 2 - Problem: A hollow shaft transmits $45 \text{ kW}$ power at $500 \text{ rpm}$. The ratio of inner to outer diameter is $k = d_i/d_o = 0.6$. If the maximum allowable shear stress is $\tau = 84 \text{ MPa}$, calculate the outer diameter $d_o$ and inner diameter $d_i$. - Solution: - Calculate torque $T$:

$${T = \frac{P \times 60}{2\pi N} = \frac{45000 \times 60}{2\pi \times 500} = \frac{2700000}{3141.59} \approx 859.44 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 859440 \text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}}$$
- Apply hollow shaft torsion formula:
$${\tau = \frac{16 T}{\pi d_o^3 (1 - k^4)} \implies 84 = \frac{16 \times 859440}{\pi d_o^3 (1 - 0.6^4)}}$$
$${1 - 0.6^4 = 1 - 0.1296 = 0.8704}$$
$${84 = \frac{13751040}{3.14159 \times 0.8704 \times d_o^3} = \frac{13751040}{2.7344 \times d_o^3} \implies 84 = \frac{5028905}{d_o^3}}$$
$${d_o^3 = \frac{5028905}{84} \approx 59867.9 \implies d_o \approx \sqrt[3]{59867.9} \approx 39.12 \text{ mm}}$$
- Calculate inner diameter $d_i$:
$${d_i = 0.6 \times d_o = 0.6 \times 39.12 \approx 23.47 \text{ mm}}$$
- Key Insight: $d_o \approx 39.12 \text{ mm}$, $d_i \approx 23.47 \text{ mm}$. Include $(1-k^4)$ in the hollow shaft torsion denominator.

3.2 Key Design, Stress Relations & Applications

1. Concept Pointers
  • Square Key Stress Ratio:
  • For a square key $w = t = d/4$:
    • Shear stress: $\tau = \frac{8T}{d \cdot l \cdot w}$.
    • Crushing stress: $\sigma_c = \frac{8T}{d \cdot l \cdot t}$.
    • Since $w = t \implies \sigma_c = 2\tau$.
    • Crushing stress is EXACTLY TWICE the shear stress!
  • Special Key Types & Applications:
  • Woodruff Key: Semi-circular disc shape. Self-aligning; used on tapered shafts for low torque at high speeds.
  • Feather Key: Fixed to one member, permits axial sliding of hub relative to shaft while transmitting torque.
  • Kennedy Key: Two square keys placed at $90^\circ$ to each other, fitted along diagonals for heavy-duty torque.
  • Saddle Key: No keyway cut in shaft. Transmits power purely by friction between flat/curved key bottom and shaft. Used for light loads.
  • Tangent Key: Fitted in pairs at right angles; handles heavy impact torque in both directions.
💡 Key Concept: Square Key Stress Ratio: For a square key $w=t$, crushing stress is always twice the shear stress $\sigma_c = 2\tau$. This relation forms the basis for equal strength key design.

[!TRAP] Key Application Matching: - Tapered shaft $\implies$ Woodruff key (self-aligning). - Axial sliding $\implies$ Feather key. - Heavy duty $\implies$ Kennedy key $90^\circ$. - Friction drive (no shaft keyway) $\implies$ Saddle key.

2. Decision Tree
Key Selection & Stress Check?
├─ Is ratio of crushing to shear stress asked for Square Key? → sigma_c = 2 * tau
├─ Is shaft Tapered? → WOODRUFF KEY (self-aligning)
├─ Is Axial Sliding of hub required? → FEATHER KEY
├─ Is loading Heavy Duty? → KENNEDY KEY (two keys at 90 deg)
└─ Is there NO keyway on the shaft? → SADDLE KEY (friction transmission)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Square key: $w = t = d/4$.
  • [ ] Square key relation: $\sigma_c = 2\tau$.
  • [ ] Woodruff: tapered shaft, self-aligning.
  • [ ] Feather: axial sliding.
  • [ ] Kennedy: heavy duty, 2 keys at $90^\circ$.
  • [ ] Saddle: light duty, friction only.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A square key of width $w = 10 \text{ mm}$ and length $l = 60 \text{ mm}$ is mounted on a shaft of diameter $d = 40 \text{ mm}$. If the allowable shear stress of the key material is $\tau = 50 \text{ MPa}$, calculate the allowable crushing stress $\sigma_c$ for equal strength in shear and crushing. - Solution: - For a square key $w = t$, equal strength in shear and crushing dictates:

$${\sigma_c = 2\tau = 2 \times 50 = 100 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Key Insight: $\sigma_c = 100 \text{ MPa}$. Crushing stress is double the shear stress.

Worked Example 2 - Problem: A shaft of diameter $d = 35 \text{ mm}$ transmits a torque $T = 395 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$. A square key of length $l = 60 \text{ mm}$ is used. If the allowable shear stress is $\tau = 55 \text{ MPa}$, determine the required key width $w$ and thickness $t$. - Solution: - Apply shear torque formula:

$${T = \frac{d}{2} \cdot (l \cdot w \cdot \tau) \implies 395000 = \frac{35}{2} \times (60 \times w \times 55)}$$
$${395000 = 17.5 \times 3300 \times w = 57750 \cdot w \implies w = \frac{395000}{57750} \approx 6.84 \text{ mm}}$$
- Since it is a square key: $w = t \approx 6.84 \text{ mm}$. - Key Insight: Key dimensions $w = t \approx 6.84 \text{ mm}$.

3.3 Cotter Joints & Engine Applications

1. Concept Pointers
  • Definition & Load Capacity:
  • A cotter joint is a rigid temporary fastening used to connect two co-axial rods subjected to axial tensile or compressive loads.
  • It does NOT permit angular motion (unlike a knuckle joint).
  • Geometry & Taper:
  • Cotter is a flat wedge-shaped rectangular plate.
  • Taper is provided on ONE SIDE ONLY (typically $1/24$ to $1/48$) to make the joint self-locking and facilitate assembly/disassembly.
  • Standard Proportions:
  • Cotter thickness: $t \approx 0.31 d$ (or $0.3d - 0.4d$).
  • Cotter width: $b \approx 1.3d - 1.5d$.
  • Engine Applications:
  • Joint connecting piston rod to crosshead of a steam engine.
  • Joint connecting piston rod to tail rod / pump rod.
  • Joint connecting slide spindle to fork in valve gear mechanisms.
  • Foundation anchor bolts.
  • Failure Modes:
  • Shear failure of cotter (double shear).
  • Tearing of spigot across slot / Socket tearing.
  • Crushing of spigot / socket collars.
💡 Key Concept: Cotter Joint Application Rule: Cotter joints connect CO-AXIAL RODS under AXIAL TENSION/COMPRESSION (e.g. piston rod to crosshead). Taper is provided on ONE SIDE ONLY ($1/24$ to $1/48$).

[!TRAP] Cotter Joint vs Knuckle Joint: - Cotter Joint $\implies$ Rigid connection between CO-AXIAL rods (no angular movement). - Knuckle Joint $\implies$ Flexible connection between INTERSECTING/ANGULAR rods (permits angular movement).

2. Decision Tree
Rod Joint Selection Check?
├─ Are rods Co-axial under Pure Axial Tension/Compression? → COTTER JOINT
├─ Are rods requiring Angular Flexibility / Pin Joint? → KNUCKLE JOINT
├─ Which engine part connects Piston Rod to Crosshead? → COTTER JOINT
├─ On how many sides is Taper provided on a cotter? → ONE SIDE ONLY (1 in 24 to 1 in 48)
└─ Standard cotter thickness formula? → t approx 0.31 * d
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Connects co-axial rods under axial load.
  • [ ] Rigid joint (no angular motion).
  • [ ] Taper on one side only ($1/24$ to $1/48$).
  • [ ] Piston rod to crosshead joint.
  • [ ] Cotter thickness $t \approx 0.31d$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Specify the recommended thickness of a steel cotter used to connect two co-axial rods of diameter $d = 40 \text{ mm}$. - Solution: - Standard empirical proportion for cotter thickness:

$${t = 0.31 \cdot d = 0.31 \times 40 = 12.4 \text{ mm}}$$
- Key Insight: $t \approx 12.4 \text{ mm}$ (typically $0.3d$ to $0.4d$).

3.4 Shaft Couplings (Rigid, Flexible, Oldham's & Universal)

1. Concept Pointers
  • Rigid Couplings:
  • Used strictly when shafts are PERFECTLY ALIGNED. Cannot tolerate any misalignment.
  • Muff / Sleeve Coupling: Hollow cylinder fitted over shaft ends with a key. Sleeve is designed as a hollow shaft.
  • Flange Coupling: Two flanged hubs keyed to shaft ends and bolted together. Bolt holes are reamed to ensure uniform shear load sharing among bolts.
  • Flexible Couplings:
  • Used when shafts have SLIGHT MISALIGNMENT (parallel or angular) or shock loads.
  • Bushed Pin Flexible Coupling: Replaces rigid bolts with flexible pins cushioned by rubber/leather bushes. Bushes absorb shock and accommodate small misalignment. Pins experience bearing, bending, and shear stresses.
  • Oldham's Coupling: Used to connect PARALLEL SHAFTS WITH A SMALL PARALLEL OFFSET/DISTANCE. Contains a floating central disc with perpendicular tongues.
  • Hooke's / Universal Joint: Used to connect SHAFTS INTERSECTING AT A LARGE VARIABLE ANGLE. Common in automobile drive/propeller shafts.
💡 Key Concept: Coupling Selection by Misalignment: - Perfectly Aligned $\implies$ Rigid Coupling (Muff/Flange). - Slight Misalignment + Shock Absorption $\implies$ Bushed Pin Flexible Coupling. - Parallel Offset Shafts (Small Distance) $\implies$ Oldham's Coupling. - Angular Misalignment (Intersecting Shafts) $\implies$ Universal / Hooke's Joint.

[!TRAP] Oldham's vs Universal Joint: - Parallel shafts with offset $\implies$ Oldham's Coupling. - Intersecting shafts at an angle $\implies$ Universal (Hooke's) Joint. Do not mix up parallel offset with angular misalignment!

2. Decision Tree
Shaft Coupling Selection Check?
├─ Shafts are Perfectly Aligned? → RIGID COUPLING (Flange / Muff)
├─ Shafts have Slight Misalignment & Shock Loads? → BUSHED PIN FLEXIBLE COUPLING
├─ Shafts are Parallel with a Small Distance/Offset? → OLDHAM'S COUPLING
├─ Shafts Intersect at a Large Variable Angle? → HOOKE'S / UNIVERSAL JOINT
└─ Muff coupling sleeve is designed as? → HOLLOW SHAFT
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Perfectly aligned $\implies$ Rigid (Muff/Flange).
  • [ ] Muff sleeve designed as hollow shaft.
  • [ ] Flange bolt holes reamed for equal load sharing.
  • [ ] Slight misalignment $\implies$ Bushed pin flexible coupling.
  • [ ] Parallel offset $\implies$ Oldham's coupling.
  • [ ] Large angular misalignment $\implies$ Universal (Hooke's) joint.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Select the appropriate coupling for connecting the propeller shaft of an automobile to the rear axle differential gear shaft, where the shafts intersect at a varying angle during vehicle motion. - Solution: - Requirement: Shafts intersect at a large, varying angle during operation. - Recommended coupling: Hooke's (Universal) Joint. - Key Insight: Universal joints accommodate dynamic angular misalignment between intersecting shafts.

Worked Example 2 - Problem: Two parallel shafts have their axes offset by a small distance of $5 \text{ mm}$. Which coupling should be selected to transmit rotation between them? - Solution: - Requirement: Parallel shafts with a small parallel offset. - Recommended coupling: Oldham's Coupling. - Key Insight: Oldham's coupling handles parallel lateral offset via its sliding intermediate disc.

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (Core Conceptual)

Q1: Why is a hollow shaft stronger in torsion than a solid shaft of equal mass and material?
A: Because its material is located farther from the neutral axis, resulting in a higher polar section modulus $Z_p = J/r$.

Q2: Write the expression for Equivalent Twisting Moment $T_e$ under combined bending $M$ and torsion $T$.
A: $T_e = \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}$.

Q3: Write the expression for Equivalent Bending Moment $M_e$ under combined bending $M$ and torsion $T$.
A: $M_e = \frac{1}{2}\left[M + \sqrt{M^2 + T^2}\right]$.

Q4: Which component in a shaft-key-hub assembly is designed as the weakest safety member?
A: The Key (acts as a mechanical fuse).

Q5: What is the relationship between crushing stress $\sigma_c$ and shear stress $\tau$ for a square key?
A: Crushing stress is exactly twice the shear stress $\sigma_c = 2\tau$.

Q6: Which key is self-aligning and best suited for tapered shafts?
A: Woodruff key (semi-circular disc shape).

Q7: Which key permits axial sliding of a hub along a shaft while transmitting torque?
A: Feather key.

Q8: Describe the layout of a Kennedy keying system.
A: Consists of two square keys fitted along diagonals at $90^\circ$ to each other for heavy-duty torque transmission.

Q9: What type of load is a Cotter Joint designed to transmit?
A: Axial tensile or compressive load between co-axial rods.

Q10: On how many sides is taper provided on a cotter, and what is its typical range?
A: On ONE side only, with a taper of $1/24$ to $1/48$.

Q11: Give two practical engine applications of a cotter joint.
A: 1. Connecting piston rod to crosshead in a steam engine, 2. Connecting piston rod to pump/tail rod.

Q12: What type of coupling is used when shafts are perfectly aligned?
A: Rigid Coupling (such as Muff or Flange coupling).

Q13: How is the sleeve of a muff coupling designed?
A: As a hollow shaft $\tau = \frac{16T}{\pi D^3(1-k^4)}$.

Q14: Which coupling is specifically designed to connect parallel shafts with a small lateral offset?
A: Oldham's Coupling.

Q15: Which joint is used to connect shafts intersecting at a large variable angle (e.g. automobile propeller shafts)?
A: Hooke's (Universal) Joint.
