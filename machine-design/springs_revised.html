Design of Mechanical Springs

Helical · Torsion · Leaf · Surge & Fatigue  ·  APGENCO AE | SSC JE Mechanical

1. Helical Compression & Tension Springs — Stress & Deflection

A helical compression spring stores elastic strain energy when compressed. The wire of the spring is subjected primarily to torsion when an axial load is applied — the axial force creates a twisting moment about the wire axis. Two secondary effects are superimposed: a direct shear stress from the transverse shear of the axial load, and a stress concentration on the inner surface of the coil due to the curvature of the wire.

Spring Index

$$\boxed{C = \frac{D}{d}}$$
$D$ — mean coil diameter, mm $d$ — wire diameter, mm Preferred manufacturing range: $4 \leq C \leq 12$; typical $C \approx 6$–$8$ Low $C$ (tightly wound): high stress concentration; difficult to coil High $C$ (loosely wound): prone to buckling; tangling in service

Direct Shear Factor $K_s$

$K_s$ accounts for the direct shear stress from the axial load, which adds to the torsional shear stress:

$$\boxed{K_s = 1 + \frac{1}{2C} = \frac{2C+1}{2C}}$$

Wahl Stress Factor $K_w$

$K_w$ accounts for both direct shear and curvature stress concentration. It is the complete correction factor used in detailed stress calculations:

$$\boxed{K_w = \frac{4C-1}{4C-4} + \frac{0.615}{C}}$$
$K_w > K_s$ always; the additional term accounts for coil curvature effect on inner surface

Maximum Torsional Shear Stress

Maximum stress occurs on the inner surface of the coil wire where torsion and curvature concentration add together:

$$\boxed{\tau_\text{max} = K_w \cdot \frac{8PD}{\pi d^3} = K_w \cdot \frac{8PC}{\pi d^2}}$$
$P$ — axial load on spring, N $\tau_\text{max}$ at inner surface of coil (smallest radius of curvature)

Axial Deflection

$$\boxed{\delta = \frac{8PD^3n}{Gd^4} = \frac{8PC^3n}{Gd}}$$
$n$ — number of active coils $G$ — modulus of rigidity (shear modulus), MPa For steel: $G \approx 80{,}000$ MPa; for bronze: $G \approx 40{,}000$ MPa

Spring Stiffness (Spring Rate)

$$\boxed{k = \frac{P}{\delta} = \frac{Gd^4}{8D^3n} = \frac{Gd}{8C^3n} \quad \text{N/mm}}$$

2. Spring Geometry & End Conditions

The total number of coils $n_t$ differs from the active coils $n$ depending on how the spring ends are formed. Inactive (dead) coils at each end do not contribute to deflection but do contribute to solid length.

End typeActive coils $n$Solid length $L_s$
Plain ends$n = n_t$$n_t \cdot d$
Plain & ground ends$n = n_t - 1$$n_t \cdot d$
Squared (closed) ends$n = n_t - 2$$(n_t + 1)\cdot d$
Squared & ground ends$n = n_t - 2$$n_t \cdot d$

Squared and ground ends are the standard for precision springs — they seat flat on their support surfaces, ensuring the load is applied coaxially with the spring axis. This prevents eccentric loading and buckling.

Free Length, Solid Length & Pitch

$$L_s = n_t \cdot d \qquad (\text{squared \& ground ends})$$
$$\boxed{L_f = L_s + \delta_\text{max} + \delta_\text{clash}}$$
$\delta_\text{clash}$ — clash allowance $\approx 0.15\,\delta_\text{max}$ (prevents coils closing under overload)
$$\boxed{p = \frac{L_f - L_s}{n} + d \quad \text{mm}}$$
$p$ — coil pitch at free length

3. Spring Combinations, Cutting & Strain Energy

Series and Parallel

$$\text{Series: } \frac{1}{k_\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} \qquad \text{Parallel: } k_\text{eq} = k_1 + k_2$$
Series: same load, deflections add — softer combined spring Parallel: same deflection, loads add — stiffer combined spring Concentric (nested) springs are always in parallel

Cutting a Spring

If a spring of stiffness $k$ and $n$ active coils is cut into two equal halves, each half has $n/2$ coils. Since $k \propto 1/n$, each half has stiffness $2k$. If both halves are then placed in parallel, the equivalent stiffness is $4k$.

$$\text{Original: } k \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Cut into 2 halves: } 2k \text{ each} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Both halves in parallel: } 4k$$

Strain Energy & Resilience

$$\boxed{U = \frac{1}{2}P\delta = \frac{P^2}{2k} = \frac{\tau^2}{4G}\cdot V}$$
$V = \frac{\pi^2}{4}d^2 D n$ — volume of wire in spring, mm³
$$\text{Modulus of Resilience} = \frac{U}{V} = \frac{\tau^2}{4G}$$

Concentric / Co-axial Springs

Two helical springs nested together are always in parallel and share the load. For the springs to be independent (neither one locks the other when deflected), adjacent coils must be wound in opposite hands — one right-hand helix and one left-hand helix. Without this, the coils of one spring would hook into the other's coils under heavy loading.

For equal stress design of co-axial springs (to fully utilise material in both springs simultaneously):

$$\frac{P_1}{D_1 d_1^2} = \frac{P_2}{D_2 d_2^2}$$

4. Helical Torsion Spring

A torsion spring is loaded by a torque or bending moment applied to its ends, causing it to wind or unwind. Unlike a compression or tension spring where the wire is in torsion, in a torsion spring the wire is subjected to bending. The wire bends in its plane as the coil winds up.

Wahl Factor for Torsion Spring

$$\boxed{K_b = \frac{4C-1}{4C-4}}$$
Only the first term of the Wahl factor (no direct shear term, since the loading is bending)

Bending Stress in Wire

$$\boxed{\sigma = K_b \cdot \frac{32\,M}{\pi\,d^3}}$$
$M$ — applied bending moment, N·mm $\sigma$ — bending stress in wire, MPa

Angular Deflection

$$\boxed{\theta = \frac{64\,M\,D\,n}{E\,d^4} \quad \text{radians}}$$
$E$ — Young's modulus (bending uses $E$, not $G$), MPa For steel: $E \approx 200{,}000$ MPa

Angular Stiffness

$$k_\theta = \frac{M}{\theta} = \frac{E\,d^4}{64\,D\,n} \quad \text{N·mm/rad}$$

5. Leaf Springs (Semi-Elliptic Laminated Springs)

A semi-elliptic leaf spring consists of a master leaf (full-length) and a stack of progressively shorter graduated leaves, all clamped at the centre by a U-bolt and pin and supported at the ends. The graduated leaves form an equivalent beam of uniform strength — constant bending stress throughout the length — achieved by the triangular taper in width.

Bending Stress

$$\boxed{\sigma = \frac{3\,P\,L}{2\,n\,b\,t^2}}$$
$P$ — total central load, N $L$ — half-span (centre to eye), mm $n$ — total number of leaves $b$ — width of each leaf, mm $t$ — thickness of each leaf, mm

Central Deflection

$$\boxed{\delta = \frac{3\,P\,L^3}{8\,E\,n\,b\,t^3}}$$

Load Sharing Between Full-Length and Graduated Leaves

When a semi-elliptic spring has both full-length master leaves and shorter graduated leaves acting together without pre-stressing (nipping), the load is shared unequally. The graduated leaves deflect less (being shorter), so the full-length leaves carry a disproportionately higher share:

$$P_g = \frac{2}{3}P \qquad P_f = \frac{1}{3}P$$
$P_g$ — load carried by graduated leaves (2/3 of total) $P_f$ — load carried by full-length (master) leaves (1/3 of total)

This unequal sharing means the full-length leaves reach a bending stress $1.5\times$ higher than the graduated leaves for the same number of leaves:

$$\sigma_f = 1.5\,\sigma_g \quad \text{(without nipping)}$$

Nipping

Nipping is the practice of pre-stressing the leaves by inserting a spacer (a pre-formed initial curvature difference) between the master and graduated leaves before assembly. The pre-stress introduces compressive stress in the full-length leaves and tensile stress in the graduated leaves. Under service load, the tensile service stress in the master leaves subtracts from the initial compressive pre-stress, effectively reducing the net stress. The goal of nipping is to equalise the stress in all leaves:

$$\sigma_f = \sigma_g \quad \text{(with nipping — equal stress condition)}$$

6. Surge, Buckling & Fatigue of Helical Springs

Spring Surge

A helical compression spring has a natural frequency of longitudinal vibration, called the surge frequency. If the frequency of the applied load approaches the spring's natural frequency, resonance occurs — standing waves travel through the spring coil-by-coil. This causes coil-to-coil impacts (surge), severe stress amplification, and rapid fatigue failure. The surge frequency is:

$$\boxed{f_n = \frac{d}{2\pi D^2 n}\sqrt{\frac{G\,g}{2\rho}} \quad \text{Hz}}$$
$\rho$ — density of wire material, kg/mm³ (steel: $\approx 7.86 \times 10^{-6}$ kg/mm³) $g$ — gravitational acceleration, mm/s²

To avoid resonance, the operating frequency must remain below $0.5\,f_n$ or above $2\,f_n$. Variable-pitch springs are used in high-speed valve springs to eliminate a single dominant natural frequency — the varying pitch means different coils have different natural frequencies, preventing standing wave formation.

Buckling of Compression Springs

A long helical compression spring can buckle like a column if its free length is too large relative to its coil diameter. The tendency to buckle is characterised by the slenderness ratio $L_f/D$. Springs with $L_f/D > 4$ (for fixed ends) or $L_f/D > 2.6$ (for pivoted ends) are prone to buckling. Prevention methods: guide posts or guide sleeves, or design the spring with lower slenderness ratio.

Fatigue Loading of Springs

Springs subjected to fluctuating loads (valve springs, suspension springs) experience fully reversed or partially reversed shear stresses. The stress parameters for the Goodman or Soderberg criterion are:

$$\tau_m = K_w\,\frac{8P_m D}{\pi d^3} \qquad \tau_a = K_w\,\frac{8P_a D}{\pi d^3}$$
$P_m = (P_\text{max}+P_\text{min})/2$ — mean load $P_a = (P_\text{max}-P_\text{min})/2$ — amplitude load
$$\text{Soderberg: } \frac{\tau_a}{\tau_e} + \frac{\tau_m}{\tau_y} = \frac{1}{N} \qquad \text{Goodman: } \frac{\tau_a}{\tau_e} + \frac{\tau_m}{\tau_u} = \frac{1}{N}$$
$\tau_e$ — endurance limit in shear $\tau_y$ — yield strength in shear $\tau_u$ — ultimate strength in shear
