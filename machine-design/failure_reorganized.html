2.1 — All Formulas & Quantitative Relations (with symbols & units)

1. Stress Concentration Factor

  • Theoretical Stress Concentration Factor $K_t$:
    $${\boxed{K_t = \frac{\sigma_{\text{max}}}{\sigma_{\text{nom}}}}}$$
  • $\sigma_{\text{max}}$: maximum localized stress at discontinuity $\text{MPa}$

  • $\sigma_{\text{nom}}$: nominal stress calculated on net cross-section $\text{MPa}$

  • Circular Hole in Infinite Plate (Uniaxial Tension):

    $${\boxed{K_t = 3.0}}$$

  • Maximum boundary stress: $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 3 \cdot \sigma_{\text{nom}}$.

2. Damping & Endurance Limit Modifiers

  • Modified Endurance Limit $S_e$:
    $${\boxed{S_e = k_a \cdot k_b \cdot k_c \cdot k_d \cdot k_e \cdot S_e' \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$
  • $S_e'$: endurance limit of standard polished test specimen $\text{MPa}$
  • For steel: $\boxed{S_e' \approx 0.5 \cdot S_{ut}}$
  • $k_a$: surface finish factor
  • $k_b$: size factor (decreases as size increases: larger volume $\implies$ more defects)
  • $k_d$: loading factor ($k_d = 1.0$ bending, $0.85$ axial, $0.59$ torsion)
  • Load factor for reversed axial load with intermediate bending: $\boxed{k_d \approx 0.90}$

3. Fluctuating Stress Components

  • Mean Stress $\sigma_m$:

    $${\boxed{\sigma_m = \frac{\sigma_{\text{max}} + \sigma_{\text{min}}}{2} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$

  • Alternating Stress $\sigma_a$:

    $${\boxed{\sigma_a = \frac{\sigma_{\text{max}} - \sigma_{\text{min}}}{2} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$

  • $\sigma_{\text{max}}, \sigma_{\text{min}}$: maximum and minimum stress values in the cycle $\text{MPa}$

4. Design Criteria for Fluctuating Loads

  • Soderberg Line (Most Conservative):
    $${\boxed{\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{yt}} = \frac{1}{N}}}$$
  • $S_{yt}$: yield strength of material $\text{MPa}$

  • $N$: factor of safety (FOS)

  • Goodman Line:

    $${\boxed{\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{N}}}$$

  • $S_{ut}$: ultimate tensile strength of material $\text{MPa}$

  • Gerber Parabola:

    $${\boxed{\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \left(\frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N}}}$$

  • Completely Reversed Loading $\sigma_m = 0$:

    $${\boxed{\sigma_a = \frac{S_e}{N}}}$$

5. Notch Sensitivity & Fatigue Stress Concentration

  • Notch Sensitivity $q$:
    $${\boxed{q = \frac{K_f - 1}{K_t - 1}}}$$
  • $q$: notch sensitivity factor $0 \le q \le 1$

  • $K_f$: fatigue stress concentration factor $1 \le K_f \le K_t$

  • Fatigue Stress Concentration Factor $K_f$:

    $${\boxed{K_f = 1 + q \cdot (K_t - 1)}}$$

  • If $q = 0 \implies \mathbf{K_f = 1.0}$ (material is insensitive to notch).
  • If $q = 1 \implies \mathbf{K_f = K_t}$ (material is fully sensitive to notch).

6. Griffith Brittle Fracture Theory

  • Griffith Crack Fracture Strength $\sigma_f$:
    $${\boxed{\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot E \cdot \gamma}{\pi \cdot c}} \quad (\text{MPa})}}$$
  • $E$: Young's modulus $\text{MPa}$
  • $\gamma$: surface energy per unit area $\text{J/m}^2$
  • $2c$: internal crack length $\text{m}$

2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Stress Concentration: High local stress levels near geometry discontinuities (holes, fillets, keyways, cracks) compared to average stress.
  • Endurance Limit $S_e$: The maximum amplitude of completely reversed bending stress that a material can withstand for an infinite number of cycles ($N \ge 10^6$ for steel) without fatigue failure.
  • Fatigue Strength: The stress level at which failure occurs after a specific number of loading cycles $N$.
  • Low-Cycle Fatigue: Fatigue failure occurring at high stress levels with plastic deformation, typically under $10^3$ cycles.
  • High-Cycle Fatigue: Fatigue failure occurring at low stress levels within elastic range, typically above $10^3$ cycles.
  • Haigh Diagram: A plot of alternating stress $\sigma_a$ on the vertical axis against mean stress $\sigma_m$ on the horizontal axis, displaying safety boundaries.
  • Shot Peening: A cold working process of blasting small steel shot pellets onto a component surface to create a layer of beneficial compressive residual stresses, significantly increasing fatigue life.
  • Notch Sensitivity $q$: The measure of how close the actual fatigue strength reduction matches the theoretical stress concentration factor.

2.3 — Standard Values & Physical Constants

  • Steel Asymptote Cycles: $N = 10^6$ cycles (transition to horizontal endurance line).
  • Infinite Plate Circular Hole $K_t$: $3.0$.
  • Reversed Axial Load Modifier: $k_d \approx 0.90$.
  • Polished Specimen Endurance Ratio: $S_e' / S_{ut} \approx 0.50$ (steel).
  • Notch Sensitivity Range: $0.0 \le q \le 1.0$.

SECTION 3: MAIN BODY

3.1 Theoretical Stress Concentration in Static Loading

1. Concept Pointers
  • Source of Stress Concentration: Abrupt changes in cross-section geometry (holes, notches, fillets, keyways, threads) that disrupt smooth stress flow lines, raising localized stress peak.
  • Ductile vs Brittle Behavior:
  • Brittle Materials: Stress concentration is EXCEEDINGLY DANGEROUS under static loads! There is no yielding capability to redistribute high local stress, leading to sudden fracture.
  • Ductile Materials: Yielding occurs locally at the peak stress location, blunting the notch and redistributing stress across the section. Thus, stress concentration is often ignored in ductile materials under static loading.
  • Uniaxial Tension Circular Hole:
  • For a central circular hole in an infinite plate under uniaxial tensile stress $f$, the theoretical stress concentration factor is:
    $${K_t = 3.0 \implies \sigma_{\text{max}} = 3f}$$
  • Non-Causes: Stress concentration is NOT caused by load variations or simple material property changes alone; it is fundamentally a geometric discontinuity effect.
💡 Key Concept: Static Loading Stress Concentration: Stress concentration is dangerous ONLY for brittle materials under static load. Ductile materials experience local plastic deformation that redistributes the stress, rendering the concentration factor harmless under static loads.

[!TRAP] Kirsch Hole Bound Trap: For a circular hole in a plate under tension, the maximum boundary stress is $3f$ (using $K_t = 3$). Do not confuse with $2f$ or $4f$ in MCQs!

2. Decision Tree
Static Loading Stress Concentration Check?
├─ Is the material Ductile? → Local yielding blunts notch (Ignore Kt under static load)
├─ Is the material Brittle? → Highly dangerous (Must apply full Kt factor)
├─ Is it a circular hole in a plate under tension? → Kt = 3.0
└─ Is the stress peak due to load variation? → NO (Stress concentration is purely geometric)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $K_t = \sigma_{\text{max}}/\sigma_{\text{nom}}$.
  • [ ] Brittle: dangerous under static load.
  • [ ] Ductile: ignored under static load.
  • [ ] Circular hole: $K_t = 3.0$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A wide flat plate made of structural steel has a central circular hole. The plate is subjected to a static uniaxial tensile stress of $150 \text{ MPa}$. Determine the maximum localized stress at the edge of the hole. - Solution: - Identify geometry: central circular hole in infinite plate under uniaxial tension. - Constant stress concentration factor: $K_t = 3.0$. - Calculate maximum stress:

$${\sigma_{\text{max}} = K_t \cdot \sigma_{\text{nom}} = 3.0 \times 150 = 450 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Key Insight: $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 450 \text{ MPa}$. Kirsch solution gives $K_t = 3$.

3.2 S-N Diagram & Fatigue Strength Limits

1. Concept Pointers
  • The S-N Curve (Wöhler Curve):
  • Plots stress amplitude ($\sigma_a$ or $S$) against the number of cycles to failure $N$ on log-log or semi-log scales.
  • Endurance Limit Asymptote $S_e$:
  • Ferrous alloys (steels) exhibit a distinct horizontal line (endurance limit asymptote) indicating infinite life $N \to \infty$ at a specific stress level.
  • This flattening occurs at approximately $10^6$ cycles for steel.
  • Non-ferrous alloys (aluminum, copper, magnesium) do not exhibit a true endurance limit; their S-N curves keep decreasing continuously.
  • Low-Cycle vs High-Cycle Fatigue:
  • Low-Cycle Fatigue: $N < 10^3$ cycles, governed by cyclic plastic strain.
  • High-Cycle Fatigue: $N \ge 10^3$ cycles, governed by cyclic elastic stress.
  • Crack Initiation Point:
  • Fatigue cracks almost always initiate on the component surface due to peak stresses, surface roughness, machining marks, and exposure.
💡 Key Concept: Ferrous vs Non-Ferrous S-N Curves: Ferrous metals (steels) exhibit a distinct endurance limit where the S-N curve flattens out near $10^6$ cycles. Non-ferrous metals continue to lose fatigue strength indefinitely and have no horizontal endurance limit.

[!TRAP] Endurance Cycles Threshold: The S-N curve for steel becomes asymptotic at $10^6$ cycles. Do not select $10^3$ (low-cycle threshold) or $10^8$ for steels!

2. Decision Tree
S-N Diagram / Fatigue Life Check?
├─ Is the material Steel/Ferrous? → Curve becomes horizontal (asymptotic) at 10^6 cycles
├─ Is the material Aluminum/Non-ferrous? → No true endurance limit (continues to decline)
├─ Is the cycle count N < 10^3? → LOW-CYCLE FATIGUE (plastic strain governed)
├─ Is the cycle count N >= 10^3? → HIGH-CYCLE FATIGUE (elastic stress governed)
└─ Where does a fatigue crack initiate? → SURFACE (due to maximum stress/defects)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Steel S-N flattens at $10^6$ cycles.
  • [ ] Non-ferrous: no endurance limit.
  • [ ] Low-cycle limit: $10^3$ cycles.
  • [ ] Fatigue cracks initiate at the surface.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Explain why a steel axle designed for a fatigue life of $5 \times 10^5$ cycles behaves differently from one designed for $5 \times 10^6$ cycles under identical fluctuating stresses. - Solution: - For steel, $5 \times 10^5$ cycles falls in the finite life region of the S-N curve. - $5 \times 10^6$ cycles is beyond the asymptote point of $10^6$ cycles. - Axle designed for $5 \times 10^6$ cycles operates below the endurance limit and has infinite theoretical life. - Key Insight: Operating past $10^6$ cycles means the component must be designed below the endurance limit to achieve infinite life.

3.3 Fluctuating-Load Criteria: Soderberg, Goodman & Gerber

1. Concept Pointers
  • Fluctuating Load Parameters:
  • Stresses fluctuate between $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{min}}$.
  • Mean stress: $\sigma_m = (\sigma_{\text{max}} + \sigma_{\text{min}})/2$.
  • Alternating stress: $\sigma_a = (\sigma_{\text{max}} - \sigma_{\text{min}})/2$.
  • Design Criteria Models:
  • Soderberg Line (Most Conservative): Uses yield strength $S_{yt}$ on the mean stress axis.
    $${\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{yt}} = \frac{1}{N}}$$
  • Goodman Line: Uses ultimate strength $S_{ut}$ on the mean stress axis.
    $${\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{N}}$$
  • Gerber Parabola: Parabolic relation using $S_{ut}$ on the mean stress axis.
    $${\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \left(\frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N}}$$
  • Haigh Diagram Comparison:
  • Soderberg line lies entirely inside/below the Goodman and Gerber lines, making it the safest and most conservative design criterion.
  • Reversed Loading:
  • When $\sigma_m = 0$ (completely reversed load), all three theories converge to the same condition: $\sigma_a = S_e / N$.
💡 Key Concept: Soderberg Conservatism: Soderberg is the most conservative fluctuating-load theory because it uses yield strength $S_{yt}$ instead of ultimate strength $S_{ut}$ on the mean stress axis, preventing any yield failure during operations.

[!TRAP] Haigh Axis Coordinates: Alternating stress $\sigma_a$ is plotted on the vertical y-axis, and mean stress $\sigma_m$ is plotted on the horizontal x-axis. Do not invert the axes!

2. Decision Tree
Fluctuating-Load Theory Check?
├─ Is ultimate strength Sut used with a straight line? → GOODMAN LINE
├─ Is yield strength Syt used with a straight line? → SODERBERG LINE (Most conservative)
├─ Is ultimate strength Sut used with a parabolic line? → GERBER PARABOLA
├─ Is mean stress sigma_m = 0? → ALL converge to sigma_a = Se / N
└─ Which line is the safest/most conservative? → SODERBERG LINE
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Soderberg: $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{yt}} = \frac{1}{N}$.
  • [ ] Goodman: $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{N}$.
  • [ ] Gerber: $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \left(\frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N}$.
  • [ ] Soderberg is the most conservative.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A steel component has an ultimate tensile strength $S_{ut} = 1000 \text{ MPa}$ and an endurance limit $S_e = 500 \text{ MPa}$. Under operation, it experiences fluctuating stresses with a mean stress $\sigma_m = 200 \text{ MPa}$ and an alternating stress $\sigma_a = 300 \text{ MPa}$. Calculate the factor of safety $N$ using the Goodman criterion. - Solution: - Apply Goodman equation:

$${\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{N}}$$
- Substitute values:
$${\frac{300}{500} + \frac{200}{1000} = \frac{1}{N} \implies 0.6 + 0.2 = \frac{1}{N} \implies 0.8 = \frac{1}{N} \implies N = \frac{1}{0.8} = 1.25}$$
- Key Insight: $N = 1.25$. Goodman uses $S_{ut}$ in the denominator of the mean stress term.

Worked Example 2 - Problem: A machine plate experiences fluctuating flexural loading varying between $+300 \text{ MPa}$ and $-150 \text{ MPa}$. The ultimate tensile strength is $S_{ut}$, the endurance limit is $0.5 S_{ut}$, and the factor of safety is $2.0$. Calculate the required minimum $S_{ut}$ using the Goodman criterion. - Solution: - Calculate mean and alternating stresses:

$${\sigma_m = \frac{300 + (-150)}{2} = 75 \text{ MPa}}$$
$${\sigma_a = \frac{300 - (-150)}{2} = 225 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Substitute $S_e = 0.5 S_{ut}$ and $N = 2.0$ into Goodman equation:
$${\frac{225}{0.5 S_{ut}} + \frac{75}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{2.0}}$$
$${\frac{450}{S_{ut}} + \frac{75}{S_{ut}} = 0.5 \implies \frac{525}{S_{ut}} = 0.5 \implies S_{ut} = \frac{525}{0.5} = 1050 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Key Insight: $S_{ut} = 1050 \text{ MPa}$. Calculate $\sigma_m$ and $\sigma_a$ first, then substitute.

3.4 Notch Sensitivity & Fatigue Stress Concentration Factor

1. Concept Pointers
  • Notch Sensitivity $q$:
  • Measures how much the fatigue strength of a material is reduced by a geometric notch:
    $${q = \frac{K_f - 1}{K_t - 1} \quad (0 \le q \le 1)}$$
  • Fatigue Stress Concentration Factor $K_f$:
  • The actual fatigue reduction factor: $K_f = 1 + q(K_t - 1)$.
  • $K_f$ is ALWAYS less than or equal to $K_t$ $K_f \le K_t$.
  • Extreme Damping Cases:
  • If $q = 0 \implies K_f = 1.0$ (material is completely insensitive; notch has no fatigue effect).
  • If $q = 1 \implies K_f = K_t$ (material has full notch sensitivity).
  • Influencing Parameters:
  • $q$ increases as the notch radius becomes smaller (sharper notch) and ultimate strength $S_{ut}$ increases (harder, higher-strength steels are more notch-sensitive).
💡 Key Concept: Notch Sensitivity and Fatigue Limits: Fatigue stress concentration factor $K_f$ is bounded by $1.0$ and $K_t$. Harder, high-strength steels are more sensitive to notches $q \to 1.0 \implies K_f \to K_t$.

[!TRAP] Notch Sensitivity Equation Trap: The notch sensitivity formula is $q = \frac{K_f - 1}{K_t - 1}$. Do not invert the numerator and denominator!

2. Decision Tree
Notch Sensitivity & Fatigue check?
├─ Is notch sensitivity q = 0? → Kf = 1.0 (No notch fatigue effect)
├─ Is notch sensitivity q = 1? → Kf = Kt (Full theoretical fatigue concentration)
├─ Does ultimate strength Sut increase? → Notch sensitivity q increases (Kf gets closer to Kt)
└─ Does notch radius decrease? → Notch sensitivity q increases
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $q = (K_f - 1)/(K_t - 1)$.
  • [ ] $K_f = 1 + q(K_t - 1)$.
  • [ ] $K_f \le K_t$.
  • [ ] Harder materials have higher $q$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A steel shaft with a notch has a theoretical stress concentration factor $K_t = 1.5$. The notch sensitivity of the material is $q = 0.6$. Calculate the actual fatigue stress concentration factor $K_f$. - Solution: - Apply the fatigue concentration factor formula:

$${K_f = 1 + q \cdot (K_t - 1) = 1 + 0.6 \times (1.5 - 1) = 1 + 0.6 \times 0.5 = 1 + 0.3 = 1.3}$$
- Key Insight: $K_f = 1.3$. Notice that $K_f < K_t$.

3.5 Endurance Modifiers & Fatigue Life Improvement

1. Concept Pointers
  • Real Component vs Specimen:
  • The endurance limit of a real mechanical part $S_e$ is significantly lower than that of a polished specimen $S_e'$ tested in a lab:
    $${S_e = k_a k_b k_c k_d k_e S_e'}$$
  • Key Modifying Factors:
  • Surface Finish Factor $k_a$: Component surface roughness reduces fatigue strength. Polished surfaces have higher $k_a$ values.
  • Size Factor $k_b$: As component size increases, endurance limit decreases because a larger volume under stress increases the statistical probability of material defects.
  • Load Factor $k_d$: Adjusts for loading mode. Bending $k_d = 1.0$, axial load $k_d = 0.85$, torsion $k_d = 0.59$.
  • Load factor for reversed axial load with intermediate bending: $k_d \approx 0.90$ (used in some standard codes).
  • Fatigue Life Improvement Methods:
  • Shot Peening: Blasting tiny steel shot onto the surface creates a cold-worked layer of compressive residual stress that delays crack initiation.
  • Surface Polishing & Filleting: Smooth transitions lower $K_t$ and eliminate surface notch defects.
💡 Key Concept: Size Effect on Fatigue: Larger components have lower endurance limits $k_b < 1.0$ due to a higher likelihood of structural flaws within the larger stressed volume.

[!TRAP] Fatigue Life Improvement Trap: Shot peening improves fatigue life by introducing COMPRESSIVE surface stresses, NOT tensile surface stresses! Tensile surface stresses accelerate fatigue cracking.

2. Decision Tree
Fatigue Life Modifiers / Improvement Check?
├─ Does component size increase? → Size factor kb decreases (Endurance limit Se drops)
├─ How does Shot Peening work? → Introduces compressive residual surface stress (Increases Se)
├─ What loading mode has highest Kd? → Bending (Kd = 1.0)
└─ Does cost affect endurance limit? → NO (Endurance limit is strictly mechanical)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $S_e = k_a k_b k_c k_d k_e S_e'$.
  • [ ] Size increases $\implies$ endurance decreases.
  • [ ] Bending $k_d = 1.0$; Axial $k_d \approx 0.85$; Torsion $k_d \approx 0.59$.
  • [ ] Shot peening: introduces compressive residual stress.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A connecting rod is designed for completely reversed axial loading of $50 \text{ kN}$. The ultimate strength is $S_{ut} = 1000 \text{ MPa}$ $S_e' = 500 \text{ MPa}$. Given $k_a = 0.82$, $k_b = 0.9$, loading factor $k_e = 0.85$, $K_f = 1.3$, and factor of safety $N = 2.0$, determine the minimum diameter of the rod. - Solution: - Calculate modified endurance limit $S_e$ (using $K_f$ to reduce endurance limit):

$${S_e = \frac{k_a \cdot k_b \cdot k_e \cdot S_e'}{K_f} = \frac{0.82 \times 0.9 \times 0.85 \times 500}{1.3} = \frac{313.65}{1.3} \approx 241.27 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Completely reversed loading $\sigma_m = 0$ design:
$${\sigma_a = \frac{S_e}{N} \implies \sigma_a = \frac{241.27}{2.0} = 120.63 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Calculate area and diameter:
$${\sigma_a = \frac{P_a}{A} \implies 120.63 = \frac{50000}{\frac{\pi}{4}d^2} \implies d^2 = \frac{50000 \times 4}{120.63 \times \pi} \approx 527.7 \implies d \approx 22.97 \text{ mm}}$$
- Round to the nearest standard size: $23 \text{ mm}$. - Key Insight: $d \approx 23 \text{ mm}$. Apply modifiers and $K_f$ to adjust specimen endurance.

3.6 Griffith Brittle Fracture Theory

1. Concept Pointers
  • Griffith Theory of Brittle Fracture:
  • Explains that the theoretical cohesive strength of materials is much higher than observed fracture strength due to micro-cracks.
  • Predicts crack propagation occurs when the strain energy release rate exceeds the rate of surface energy increase.
  • Fracture Strength Equation:
    $${\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot E \cdot \gamma}{\pi \cdot c}}}$$
  • Fracture stress $\sigma_f$ is inversely proportional to the square root of half-crack length $c$ $\sigma_f \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}$.
💡 Key Concept: Griffith Fracture Relation: Fracture strength $\sigma_f$ is governed by the crack dimension. Larger flaws drastically lower the stress required to propagate a crack $\sigma_f \propto c^{-1/2}$.
2. Decision Tree
Griffith Brittle Fracture Check?
├─ Material classification? → BRITTLE
├─ Internal crack length is 2c? → Use crack half-length 'c' in formula
└─ Fracture strength relation to crack size? → sigma_f is proportional to 1 / sqrt(c)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $\sigma_f = \sqrt{2E\gamma / \pi c}$.
  • [ ] Applies to brittle fracture.
  • [ ] Fracture stress $\sigma_f \propto c^{-1/2}$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A glass plate has a modulus of elasticity $E = 70 \text{ GPa}$, surface energy $\gamma = 1.0 \text{ J/m}^2$, and contains an internal crack of length $2c = 4 \mu\text{m}$. Calculate the critical fracture stress $\sigma_f$ using Griffith's theory. - Solution: - Internal crack length $= 2c = 4 \mu\text{m} \implies c = 2 \mu\text{m} = 2 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}$. - Substitute values into Griffith equation:

$${\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot E \cdot \gamma}{\pi \cdot c}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times (70 \times 10^9) \times 1.0}{\pi \times (2 \times 10^{-6})}} = \sqrt{\frac{140 \times 10^9}{6.283 \times 10^{-6}}} = \sqrt{2.228 \times 10^{16}} \approx 149.26 \text{ MPa}}$$
- Key Insight: $\sigma_f \approx 149.26 \text{ MPa}$. Use crack half-length $c = 2 \mu\text{m}$ (not the full crack length $2c$).

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (Core Conceptual)

Q1: Define stress concentration. How is it calculated?
A: Stress concentration is the high local stress near cross-section geometry changes. Calculated as $K_t = \sigma_{\text{max}}/\sigma_{\text{nom}}$.

Q2: Contrast static stress concentration behavior in ductile vs brittle materials.
A: In brittle materials, stress concentration is dangerous and can lead to sudden fracture. In ductile materials, local yielding redistributes stress, making it harmless under static load.

Q3: What is the theoretical stress concentration factor $K_t$ for a circular hole in an infinite plate under tension?
A: $K_t = 3.0$.

Q4: Define endurance limit $S_e$ in fatigue.
A: The maximum amplitude of completely reversed bending stress that a material can withstand for infinite cycles ($N \ge 10^6$ for steels) without failure.

Q5: At approximately how many cycles does the S-N curve for steel become asymptotic?
A: Near $10^6$ cycles.

Q6: Differentiate between low-cycle and high-cycle fatigue.
A: Low-cycle fatigue occurs at high stresses with plastic strain under $10^3$ cycles. High-cycle fatigue occurs at lower stresses in the elastic range above $10^3$ cycles.

Q7: Write the Soderberg straight-line equation for fluctuating loads.
A: $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{yt}} = \frac{1}{N}$.

Q8: Write the Goodman straight-line equation for fluctuating loads.
A: $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{N}$.

Q9: Why is the Soderberg criterion considered the most conservative?
A: Because it uses yield strength $S_{yt}$ on the mean stress axis, which is lower than ultimate strength $S_{ut}$, thereby preventing any yielding.

Q10: Define notch sensitivity $q$ and state its mathematical formula.
A: A measure of the sensitivity of a material to notches under fatigue: $q = \frac{K_f - 1}{K_t - 1}$.

Q11: Under what condition does $K_f = 1.0$? What does it mean?
A: When $q = 0$. It means the material is insensitive to notches, and the notch has no reducing effect on fatigue strength.

Q12: Why does the size of a component reduce its endurance limit?
A: Because larger components contain a larger volume of material, increasing the statistical probability of micro-defects.

Q13: How does shot peening improve the fatigue life of a component?
A: By bombarding the surface with small steel shot, which cold-works the surface layer and introduces beneficial compressive residual stresses that delay crack initiation.

Q14: Where do fatigue cracks usually initiate in a machine element?
A: At the surface, where cyclic stresses are highest and surface defects exist.

Q15: State Griffith's critical brittle fracture stress equation.
A: $\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma}{\pi c}}$, where $c$ is the crack half-length.

SECTION 5: CONSOLIDATION NOTES & HIGH-YIELD EXAM TRAPS

  • Stress Concentration: $K_t = \sigma_{\text{max}}/\sigma_{\text{nom}}$.
  • Danger: Brittle (dangerous under static), Ductile (ignored under static).
  • Central Hole Plate: $K_t = 3.0 \implies \sigma_{\text{max}} = 3f$.
  • S-N Diagram: Steel flattens at $10^6$ cycles. Non-ferrous has no flat asymptote.
  • Crack initiation always occurs at the surface.
  • Fluctuating-Load Criteria:
  • Soderberg (conservative, yield-based): $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{yt}} = \frac{1}{N}$.
  • Goodman (ultimate-based): $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}} = \frac{1}{N}$.
  • Gerber (parabolic, ultimate-based): $\frac{\sigma_a}{S_e} + \left(\frac{\sigma_m}{S_{ut}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N}$.
  • If $\sigma_m = 0 \implies \sigma_a = S_e / N$.
  • Notch Sensitivity: $q = \frac{K_f - 1}{K_t - 1}$. $K_f = 1 + q(K_t - 1)$. $K_f \le K_t$.
  • Harder materials have higher $q$ (closer to $K_t$).
  • Endurance Limit Modifiers: $S_e = k_a k_b k_c k_d k_e S_e'$.
  • Size effect: larger component $\implies$ lower endurance limit.
  • Loading: Bending $k_d=1.0$, Axial $k_d \approx 0.85$, Torsion $k_d \approx 0.59$.
  • Axial load + bending modifier: $k_d \approx 0.90$.
  • Fatigue Life Improvement: Shot peening (most effective, creates compressive surface stresses). Surface polishing increases $k_a$.
  • Griffith Theory: $\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma}{\pi c}}$ (for brittle fracture, uses crack half-length $c$).
