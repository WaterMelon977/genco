Design of Bearings, Brakes & Clutches

Rolling Contact · Journal Bearings · Friction Brakes · Friction Clutches  ·  APGENCO AE | SSC JE Mechanical

1. Rolling Contact Bearings

Rolling contact bearings (ball and roller bearings) support rotating shafts with much lower friction than sliding bearings by interposing rolling elements between the inner and outer races. They are rated by their basic dynamic load rating $C$ — the constant radial load that $90\%$ of a group of identical bearings can withstand for $10^6$ revolutions without fatigue flaking.

Basic Rating Life $L_{10}$

$$\boxed{L_{10} = \left(\frac{C}{P_e}\right)^k \times 10^6 \quad \text{revolutions}}$$
$C$ — basic dynamic load rating, N $P_e$ — equivalent radial dynamic load, N $k = 3.0$ — ball bearings $k = 10/3 = 3.33$ — roller bearings

Load–Life Proportionality (Ball Bearings, $k = 3$)

$$\boxed{L_1 P_1^3 = L_2 P_2^3 \implies L \propto \frac{1}{P^3}}$$

Doubling the load ($P \to 2P$): $L \to L/8$ (life drops to one-eighth). Halving the load ($P \to P/2$): $L \to 8L$ (life increases eight-fold).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Ball bearing life scales with the cube of load ($k=3$), whereas roller bearing life uses $k = 10/3 \approx 3.33$. If a question asks for life change under doubled load for a ball bearing, the new life is $L/8$.

Life in Hours

$$L_{10h} = \frac{L_{10}}{60\,N} \quad \text{hours}$$
$N$ — shaft speed, rpm

Equivalent Radial Load

$$\boxed{P_e = X\,F_r + Y\,F_a}$$
$F_r$ — radial load, N $F_a$ — axial (thrust) load, N $X, Y$ — radial and axial load factors (from bearing catalogue, depend on $F_a/F_r$)

Bearing Designation System

Standard rolling bearing designation: a four-digit number. The first one or two digits denote the series (light, medium, heavy); the last two digits give the bore diameter code.

$$\text{Bore diameter} = \text{last two digits} \times 5 \quad \text{mm}$$

Example: Bearing 6308 — series 63 (medium), bore $= 08 \times 5 = 40$ mm.

2. Journal (Sliding Contact) Bearings

A journal bearing supports a rotating shaft (the journal) on a film of lubricant. In hydrodynamic lubrication, the rotation of the shaft drags lubricant into a converging wedge-shaped film, generating pressure that lifts the shaft away from the bearing surface entirely — there is no metal-to-metal contact under steady operation.

Petroff's Equation for Coefficient of Friction

$$\boxed{f = 2\pi^2\,\frac{\mu\,N}{P}\cdot\frac{r}{c}}$$
$\mu$ — dynamic viscosity, Pa·s $N$ — shaft speed, rev/s $P = W/(d\,\ell)$ — bearing pressure on projected area, Pa $r$ — shaft radius, m $c$ — radial clearance, m
⚠️ Exam Trap: Bearing pressure $P$ is calculated on the projected area $d \cdot \ell$, NOT the circular cross-sectional area $\frac{\pi}{4}d^2$!

Bearing Characteristic Number (Sommerfeld parameter)

$$\boxed{\text{Bearing Characteristic Number} = \frac{Z\,N}{P}}$$
$Z$ — viscosity in centipoise (cP) $N$ — speed in rpm $P$ — bearing pressure in MPa Above critical $(ZN/P)_\text{crit}$: hydrodynamic lubrication (full film, low friction) Below critical: boundary lubrication (metal contact, high friction, wear)

3. Brakes

A brake converts kinetic energy of a rotating member into heat by applying a friction force between a stationary shoe (or band) and a rotating drum. The braking torque is the product of the normal (clamping) force, the coefficient of friction, and the drum radius.

Short Shoe Brake ($2\theta < 60^\circ$)

When the total contact angle is small ($2\theta < 60^\circ$), pressure is assumed uniform across the shoe, and the actual coefficient of friction $\mu$ is used directly.

$$T = \mu\,R_n\,R$$
$R_n$ — normal force between shoe and drum, N; $R$ — drum radius, m

Long Shoe Brake ($2\theta > 60^\circ$)

For large contact angles ($2\theta > 60^\circ$), the normal pressure distribution is non-uniform (sinusoidal). An equivalent friction coefficient $\mu_\text{eq}$ replaces actual $\mu$ to account for the varying moment arm:

$$\boxed{\mu_\text{eq} = \frac{4\,\mu\,\sin\theta}{2\theta + \sin 2\theta}}$$
$\theta$ — half angle of contact, radians (in the denominator!) $2\theta > 60^\circ$ for long shoe
$$\boxed{T = F_r \times R = \mu_\text{eq}\,R_n\,R}$$
⚠️ Exam Trap: In the formula for $\mu_\text{eq}$, the denominator term $2\theta$ must be in radians, not degrees!

Shoe Proportions & Types

$$\text{Shoe width } b = (0.25\text{ to }0.50)\,D$$

Internal expanding shoe brakes (used in motor car drum brakes) have shoes that press outward against the inner surface of the drum. The friction force on the leading shoe tends to increase the normal force (self-energising), while on the trailing shoe it decreases it. Parking brakes operate mechanically for failsafe reliability.

4. Friction Clutches

A friction clutch engages and disengages the drive train by applying a clamping force between friction disc surfaces. Two theories predict torque capacity depending on the state of the friction lining.

Uniform Pressure Theory — New Clutch

When a clutch is new, the axial clamping pressure is uniform across the disc face. This gives a higher predicted torque capacity but applies only to a brand-new, unworn clutch.

$$\boxed{T = \frac{2}{3}\,\mu\,W\,\frac{r_1^3 - r_2^3}{r_1^2 - r_2^2} \cdot n}$$
$r_1$ — outer radius of friction disc, m $r_2$ — inner radius of friction disc, m $W$ — total axial clamping force, N $n$ — number of active contact pairs

Uniform Wear Theory — Service (Worn) Clutch [Safe Design]

After running in, the condition $p \cdot r = \text{const}$ (pressure inversely proportional to radius) produces uniform wear across the disc. This is the condition used for safe design of clutches in service:

$$\boxed{T = \mu\,W\,R_m \cdot n} \qquad \boxed{R_m = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}}$$
$R_m$ — mean radius (arithmetic mean for uniform wear), m Uniform wear gives LOWER torque than uniform pressure — conservative, used for design
⚠️ Exam Trap: Unless explicitly stated as a "new clutch", always use Uniform Wear Theory ($R_m = \frac{r_1+r_2}{2}$) for clutch design questions. Uniform wear gives a lower, safer torque capacity ($T_{\text{wear}} < T_{\text{pressure}}$).

Multi-Plate Clutch — Active Contact Pairs

$$\boxed{n = n_1 + n_2 - 1}$$
$n_1$ — number of discs on driving shaft $n_2$ — number of discs on driven shaft

Centrifugal Clutch

Centrifugal clutch shoes are flung outward by centrifugal force as speed increases. Engagement begins when centrifugal force equals spring force ($F_{\text{cf}} = F_{\text{spring}}$). At running speed, the net engaging force is the difference:

$$\text{Engagement begins: } F_\text{cf} = F_\text{spring} \quad (N_1)$$ $$\text{Running net force: } F_\text{net} = F_\text{cf} - F_\text{spring} \quad (N_2 > N_1)$$

📌 Quick-Fire Exam Checklist
  • $L_{10} = (C/P_e)^k \times 10^6$ rev; $k=3$ (ball), $k=10/3$ (roller).
  • Ball bearing: $L \propto 1/P^3$; double load $\Rightarrow$ life = $L/8$.
  • Bearing bore code: last two digits $\times 5$ mm (e.g., 08 $\rightarrow$ 40 mm).
  • $P_e = XF_r + YF_a$; $X$, $Y$ from catalogue.
  • Petroff: $f = 2\pi^2(\mu N/P)(r/c)$; valid for lightly loaded journal bearings.
  • $ZN/P$ above critical $\Rightarrow$ hydrodynamic; below $\Rightarrow$ boundary lubrication.
  • $c/r \approx 0.001$ ($1\,\mu$m per mm radius); $L/D = 1$–$2$.
  • Short shoe ($2\theta < 60^\circ$): use actual $\mu$. Long shoe ($2\theta > 60^\circ$): use $\mu_\text{eq} = \frac{4\mu\sin\theta}{2\theta+\sin 2\theta}$.
  • Braking torque: $T = F_r \times R = \mu_\text{eq} R_n R$.
  • Shoe width: $b = 0.25$–$0.50\,D$.
  • Uniform pressure (new clutch): $T = \frac{2}{3}\mu W \frac{r_1^3-r_2^3}{r_1^2-r_2^2}\,n$.
  • Uniform wear (safe design): $T = \mu W R_m n$; $R_m = \frac{r_1+r_2}{2}$.
  • Multi-plate: $n = n_1 + n_2 - 1$ active contact pairs.
  • Uniform wear torque $<$ uniform pressure torque — safe design always uses uniform wear.
  • Single-plate dry: trucks. Multi-plate wet: scooters. Jaw: positive drive, zero-speed engagement.
