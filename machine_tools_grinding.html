Machine Tools and Grinding

1. Topic Overview

This guide covers the core machine-tool and finishing topics commonly asked in AP Genco AE-style PYQs: lathe operations, drilling, shaping and planing, milling, gear cutting and indexing, grinding, broaching, and the most important finishing-process facts. The highest-frequency questions usually test direct identification, basic formulas, work-holding, step ratios, taper calculations, wheel selection, and process capability. The content below merges repeated notes into one revision-friendly structure and keeps the most useful numerical and conceptual points together.

2.2 Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Up milling: Cutter rotates opposite to the feed direction.
  • Climb milling: Cutter rotates in the same direction as the feed.
  • Hobbing: Fast gear-cutting process based on the generating principle.
  • Gear shaving: Finishing operation for gears.
  • Simple indexing: Indexing method based on the standard 40:1 worm ratio.
  • Differential indexing: Used when required divisions cannot be obtained by simple indexing, such as 119 divisions.
  • Grade of grinding wheel: Bond hardness, or the ability of the bond to hold abrasive grains.
  • Structure of grinding wheel: Grain spacing or density.
  • Truing: Restoring the original geometry and concentricity of the grinding wheel.
  • Dressing: Exposing fresh sharp abrasive grains on the wheel face.
  • Carborundum: Silicon carbide, an artificial abrasive.
  • Broach: Multi-tooth cutting tool with roughing, semi-finishing, and finishing teeth.
  • Surface broaching: Broaching operation where either the work or tool moves across the other.
  • Centreless grinding: Grinding process in which the workpiece is not held between centres.

2.3 Standard Values / Constants

  • Milling: climb milling generally gives better finish but needs backlash-free machine.
  • Gear cutting: hobbing is usually the fastest method.
  • Indexing: 119 divisions commonly requires differential indexing.
  • Grinding wheel grades: A to H are soft, Q to Z are hard.
  • Abrasives: aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, CBN, diamond.
  • Carborundum: the common name for silicon carbide.
  • Broaching accuracy: about 1 micron.
  • Flank wear land: VB about 0.3 to 0.6 mm in tool-life checks.
  • Grinding: soft wheel for hard material; hard wheel for soft material.
  • Drilling overtravel: often a few millimetres for through holes.

2.5 Additional High-Yield Notes

  • Knurling: Produces a roughened pattern on the surface for grip; it does not produce chips in the usual cutting sense.
  • Shaper vs planer: A shaper is used for smaller workpieces, while a planer is used for larger workpieces and heavier cuts.
  • Shaper and planer specification: The size of a shaper is generally specified by the length of stroke; a planer is specified by bed length or table size.
  • Horizontal borer vs lathe: In a lathe, the work rotates and the tool is fed; in a horizontal borer, the tool rotates while the work remains fixed.
  • Tool dynamometer: Used to measure cutting forces.
  • Universal rolling mill: Has horizontal and vertical rolls.
  • Burnishing: Improves finish by plastic deformation rather than cutting; it is different from truing and honing.
  • Gang milling: More than one cutter is mounted on the arbor to machine different surfaces in a single pass.
  • Radial rake angle: For HSS cutters, a positive radial rake is generally preferred in many practical MCQ contexts.
  • Honing, lapping, and buffing: Honing is used for cylindrical holes, lapping uses loose abrasive with very high finish and little heat, and buffing gives a high polish.

2.6 More Quick MCQ Facts

  • Capstan / turret lathe: Has no tailstock.
  • Internal gears: Can be produced by gear shaping or broaching, not by hobbing.
  • Broaching vs hobbing: Broaching can make internal gears and splines, while hobbing is generally for external gears.
  • External cylindrical grinding: Used for straight or tapered external surfaces.
  • Centreless grinding: Work advances due to the combined action of the grinding wheel and regulating wheel.
  • Tool and cutter grinder: Used for sharpening multipoint cutters.
  • Surface grinding: Used for flat surfaces.
  • Internal cylindrical grinding: Used for cylindrical or tapered holes.

3. Main Body

3.1 Lathe Operations, Work Holding, Taper Turning, and Gearbox Ratio

1. Concept Pointers
  • Cutting speed on a lathe depends on work diameter and spindle speed.
  • Facing uses a radial length of approximately half the work diameter.
  • Thread cutting is generally done at the slowest spindle speed.
  • Face plate is used for irregular or complex jobs.
  • Four-jaw chuck is used for exact centring and odd-shaped work.
  • Three-jaw chuck is quick but less accurate.
  • Collets are used for small precision work; mandrels are used for hollow jobs.
  • Tailstock set-over is the standard taper-turning method when the taper is short.
  • Gearbox step ratio sets the progression between successive speeds.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{V = \frac{\pi DN}{1000}}$$
$$\boxed{T_m = \frac{L}{fN}}$$
$$\boxed{y = L\sin\alpha}$$
$$\boxed{y \approx \frac{L(D-d)}{2l_r}}$$
$$\boxed{\phi = \left$\frac{N_{\max}}{N_{\min}}\right$^{\frac{1}{n-1}}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • In facing, the cutting length is approximately the radius.
  • Tailstock set-over is useful when the taper length is shorter than the job length.
  • The approximate taper formula is used in standard exam numericals.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Diameter $D = 20$ mm, spindle speed $N = 1000$ rpm.
Find: Cutting speed.

Solution:

  1. Use $V = \pi DN/1000$.
  2. $V = \pi \times 20 \times 1000 / 1000 = 20\pi$ m/min.
  3. Answer: $20\pi$ m/min.

Key Insight: Always keep units consistent. The common trap is forgetting that the formula gives speed in m/min when D is in mm.

Worked Example 2

Given: Taper length $l_r = 100$ mm, larger diameter $D = 40$ mm, smaller diameter $d = 30$ mm, total job length $L = 200$ mm.
Find: Approximate tailstock set-over.

Solution:

  1. Use (y \approx \frac{L(D-d)}{2l_r}).
  2. (y \approx \frac{200(40-30)}{2 \times 100} = \frac{2000}{200} = 10) mm.
  3. Answer: 10 mm.

Key Insight: The taper length is (l_r), not the full job length. That distinction is a frequent exam trap.

3.2 Drilling Machines, Drill Geometry, and Drilling Time

1. Concept Pointers
  • Drilling is used to produce round holes with a rotating drill.
  • Overtravel is included when the hole is through the workpiece.
  • Feed and spindle speed control drilling time.
  • Drill geometry questions often test the drill point and basic operation sequence.
  • Drilling is often followed by reaming, tapping, counterboring, countersinking, or boring.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{T = \frac{t+x}{fN}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • (x) is the overtravel allowance, usually a small value for through holes.
  • In numerical problems, always include overtravel if the hole breaks through the plate.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Thickness $t = 20$ mm, overtravel $x = 5$ mm, feed (f = 0.2) mm/rev, speed $N = 500$ rpm.
Find: Drilling time.

Solution:

  1. Use $T = \frac{t+x}{fN}$.
  2. $T = \frac{20+5}{0.2 \times 500} = \frac{25}{100} = 0.25$ min.
  3. Answer: 0.25 min.

Key Insight: Overtravel is easy to miss; when the hole goes through the job, it must be included in the numerator.

3.3 Shaping and Planing

1. Concept Pointers
  • A shaper uses a reciprocating single-point tool and is suited to smaller workpieces.
  • A planer is generally used for larger jobs and heavier cuts.
  • Shaper cutting speed depends on stroke length, strokes per minute, and cutting ratio.
  • Hydraulic shapers are smoother and can give uniform cutting action.
  • Mechanical shapers are simpler but less flexible in motion control.
  • Planers generally allow larger workpieces than shapers.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{V = \frac{NLC}{1000}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • (N) is strokes per minute.
  • (L) is the stroke length.
  • (C) is the cutting time ratio.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: $N = 40$ strokes/min, $L = 300$ mm, (C = 0.5).
Find: Cutting speed.

Solution:

  1. Use $V = NLC/1000$.
  2. $V = 40 \times 300 \times 0.5 / 1000 = 6$ m/min.
  3. Answer: 6 m/min.

Key Insight: The cutting ratio matters because the tool cuts only during part of the stroke cycle.

3.4 Milling Operations

1. Concept Pointers
  • In up milling, the cutter rotates opposite to feed.
  • In climb milling, the cutter rotates in the same direction as feed.
  • Up milling begins with rubbing and chip thickness increases from entry to exit.
  • Climb milling gives better finish and less tool wear, but requires backlash-free machines.
  • Milling table feed depends on feed per tooth, number of teeth, and speed.
  • Material removal rate uses depth of cut, width of cut, and table feed.
  • In slab milling, chip thickness is directly proportional to feed rate of the workpiece.
  • Milling cutter is mounted on the arbor.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{f_m = f_t zN}$$
$$\boxed{\text{MRR} = dbf_m}$$

Conditions and usage

  • Use the feed-per-tooth relation for table-feed numericals.
  • MRR is usually asked as a direct formula-based calculation.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Feed per tooth (f_t = 0.1) mm/tooth, number of teeth $z = 6$, speed $N = 200$ rpm.
Find: Table feed.

Solution:

  1. Use $f_m = f_t zN$.
  2. $f_m = 0.1 \times 6 \times 200 = 120$ mm/min.
  3. Answer: 120 mm/min.

Key Insight: Table feed is feed per tooth multiplied by the number of teeth and spindle speed.

Worked Example 2

Given: Depth of cut $d = 2$ mm, width of cut $b = 50$ mm, table feed $f_m = 120$ mm/min.
Find: MRR.

Solution:

  1. Use $\text{MRR} = dbf_m$.
  2. $\text{MRR} = 2 \times 50 \times 120 = 12000$ mm(^3)/min.
  3. Answer: 12000 mm(^3)/min.

Key Insight: Be careful with units. If MRR is needed in cm(^3)/min, convert from mm(^3)/min.

3.5 Gear Cutting and Indexing

1. Concept Pointers
  • Hobbing is a generating process and is the fastest common gear-cutting method.
  • Gear milling is a form-cutting method and is slower.
  • Gear shaving is a finishing operation.
  • Differential indexing is used for awkward numbers of divisions such as 119.
  • Simple indexing uses the standard 40:1 dividing-head ratio.
  • Gear shaping can cut internal gears, while hobbing is generally used for external gears.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{\text{Crank movement} = \frac{40}{N}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • (N) is the number of divisions required.
  • If the required divisions cannot be obtained directly, differential indexing is used.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Required number of divisions = 119.
Find: Indexing method.

Solution:

  1. 119 divisions cannot be obtained by simple indexing in the usual way.
  2. Use differential indexing.
  3. Answer: Differential indexing.

Key Insight: Odd numbers like 119 are classic differential-indexing questions.

3.6 Grinding and Finishing

1. Concept Pointers
  • Surface grinding produces flat surfaces.
  • External cylindrical grinding produces straight or tapered external surfaces.
  • Internal cylindrical grinding produces cylindrical or tapered holes.
  • Centreless grinding does not use centres for work holding.
  • Tool and cutter grinders are used to sharpen multipoint cutters.
  • Grinding is a precision finishing process with very small depth of cut.
  • High grinding speed is desirable because it reduces force per grit and offsets the high negative rake of the grit.
  • Wheel grade denotes bond hardness, not grain size.
  • Soft wheel is used for hard material, and hard wheel is used for soft material.
  • Truing restores wheel geometry; dressing exposes fresh abrasive grains.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{\text{Abrasive} - \text{Grain size} - \text{Grade} - \text{Structure} - \text{Bond}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • Wheel grades are often represented from A to Z, with soft to hard increasing along the alphabet.
  • Common abrasives include aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, CBN, and diamond.
  • Carborundum is silicon carbide.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: A grinding wheel marked A 36 L 5 V.
Find: Meaning of V.

Solution:

  1. The last symbol in the marking is the bond.
  2. V means vitrified bond.
  3. Answer: Vitrified bond.

Key Insight: In wheel markings, the final letter is the bond type.

Worked Example 2

Given: The wheel loses its original geometry during use.
Find: Correct operation to restore it.

Solution:

  1. Restoring geometry and concentricity is truing.
  2. Answer: Truing.

Key Insight: Truing restores shape; dressing exposes sharp grains. They are not the same.

3.7 Grinding Machine Types and Centreless Grinding

1. Concept Pointers
  • Surface grinding is used for flat surfaces.
  • External cylindrical grinding is used for straight or tapered external surfaces.
  • Internal cylindrical grinding is used for internal holes.
  • Centreless grinding supports the work by a blade and regulating wheel, with no centres.
  • High velocity in grinding reduces force per grit, offsets negative rake, and lowers overall grinding force.
  • The workpiece advances in centreless grinding because of the interaction of the grinding wheel and regulating wheel.
2. Formulas

3.8 Broaching

1. Concept Pointers
  • A broach has roughing, semi-finishing, and finishing teeth.
  • Broaching is very accurate and gives excellent surface finish.
  • Surface broaching is the operation where either the work or the tool moves across the other.
  • Broaching can produce internal gears and splines.
  • Broaching is suitable for complex internal shapes and mass production.
  • Tooling cost is high, so it becomes economical mainly for larger production runs.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{\text{Accuracy} \approx 1\,\mu\text{m}}$$

4. Consolidation Notes

Note on grinding wheel selection:
The grade rule and the soft-wheel/hard-material rule are frequently tested together. Grade refers to bond hardness, while structure refers to grain spacing. See Section 3.6.

Note on truing and dressing:
These are often confused. Truing restores geometry and concentricity; dressing refreshes the abrasive surface. See Section 3.6.

Note on lathe work holding:
Face plate, four-jaw chuck, three-jaw chuck, collets, and mandrels are all practical work-holding answers that appear in direct MCQs. See Section 3.1.

Note on gear cutting:
Hobbing, gear milling, and gear shaving are commonly compared. Hobbing is the fastest and is a generating process, while shaving is a finishing operation. See Section 3.5.
