2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Work Study: Systematic examination of methods of carrying out activities so as to improve the use of resources and establish standards of performance. Combines Method Study + Work Measurement.
  • Basic Tool of Work Study: Stop-watch.
  • Method Study: Systematic recording and critical examination of existing and proposed ways of doing work to simplify methods and reduce costs.
  • Work Measurement: Application of techniques designed to establish the time for a qualified worker to carry out a specified job at a defined level of performance.
  • Observed Time (OT): Clock time taken by a worker to complete an operation during a time study.
  • Rating Factor: Assessment factor applied to convert observed performance to normal performance.
  • Normal Time (NT): Time required by a qualified worker to perform a task at normal pace (NT=OT×Rating FactorNT = OT \times \text{Rating Factor}NT=OT×Rating Factor).
  • Standard Time (ST): Total time allowed to complete a task, incorporating all allowances (ST=NT+AllowancesST = NT + \text{Allowances}ST=NT+Allowances).
  • Interference Allowance: Allowance granted when an operator attends more than two machines simultaneously and must wait when two machines require attention at once.
  • Work Sampling: Work measurement technique involving large numbers of instantaneous random observations to estimate activity proportions. Observations are taken using a table of random numbers.
  • PMTS (Predetermined Motion Time Systems): Work measurement technique where times established for basic human motions (therbligs) are used to build up the time for a job at a defined level of performance. Includes MTM, Work-Factor, BMT. (Stop-watch study is NOT a PMTS technique).
  • Therbligs: 18 fundamental hand/eye motion elements established by Frank and Lillian Gilbreth for micro-motion analysis.
  • SIMO Chart (Simultaneous Motion Chart): Micro-motion process chart recording therbligs of left and right hands against a time scale measured in winks (1/2000 min1/2000\text{ min}1/2000 min).
  • Chronocyclegraph: Motion recording technique using pulsating light sources attached to worker hands to record path, speed, and direction of rapid movements.
  • Product Layout (Flow Shop / Line): Machines arranged strictly according to the sequence of operations. High volume, low variety, line balancing applies only here.
  • Process Layout (Functional): Similar machines grouped together by function (e.g., all lathes in one section). High flexibility, high material handling effort.
  • Group Technology (GT) / Cellular Layout: Machines of different types grouped into cells based on part families sharing similar design/manufacturing attributes. Offers highest overall layout flexibility for medium volume–medium variety production.
  • Fixed-Position Layout: Product remains stationary (ship building, large aircraft, dams) while men, materials, and equipment move to it.
  • Line Organization (Scalar / Military): Oldest and simplest organizational structure where authority flows vertically from top to bottom. Characterized by strong discipline, quick decision-making, and simplicity.
  • Value Engineering (VE): Systematic team approach to analyze product functions to achieve required function at lowest total cost without sacrificing quality, reliability, or aesthetics (V=F/CV = F/CV=F/C).
  • Job Analysis: Process of discovering and identifying pertinent information relative to the nature and requirements of a specific job.
  • Job Evaluation: Method of determining the relative worth/value of different jobs within an organization for wage administration.

3.1 — Plant Layout & Group Technology

1. Concept Pointers
  • Product Layout (Line / Synthetic):
    • Machines arranged in strict sequence of operations required by the product.
    • Best suited for standardized, high-volume, low-variety mass production (automobile assembly lines).
    • Production scheduling is simplest, labour efficiency highest, material handling path straight.
    • Line balancing applies ONLY to product layout.
    • Drawbacks: High capital cost, zero flexibility in design changes, single machine breakdown halts entire line.
  • Process Layout (Functional / Analytical):
    • Similar machines grouped together by function (all lathes in turning section, all mills in milling section).
    • Designed for low-volume, high-variety job shop production.
    • Features higher material-handling effort and greater flexibility in work distribution.
    • High WIP inventory, complex routing, longer throughput time, low fixed cost / high variable cost.
  • Fixed-Position Layout:
    • Product remains stationary in a single location; workers, materials, and machinery are brought to it.
    • Used for massive, heavy, or immobile products (ship building yards, large aircraft manufacturing, construction projects).
  • Group Technology (GT) & Cellular Layout:
    • Groups parts with similar design features or manufacturing processes into part families.
    • Cellular manufacturing systems are designed on the basis of Group Technology.
    • Ideal for medium-range variety and medium-range quantity production.
    • Combines the smooth material flow of product layout with the flexibility of process layout.
    • Offers highest overall flexibility among common layout types when correctly implemented.
  • Key Exam Comparisons:
    • Vehicle assembly line →\to→ Product layout.
    • Ship building yard & large aircraft assembly →\to→ Fixed-position layout (never product layout).
    • Similar machines at one location →\to→ Process layout.
2. Formulas & Selection Framework
  • Volume–Variety Matrix:
    High Volume + Low Variety→Product Layout\boxed{\text{High Volume + Low Variety} \to \text{Product Layout}}High Volume + Low Variety→Product Layout​

    Low Volume + High Variety→Process Layout\boxed{\text{Low Volume + High Variety} \to \text{Process Layout}}Low Volume + High Variety→Process Layout​

    Medium Volume + Medium Variety→Group Layout (GT)\boxed{\text{Medium Volume + Medium Variety} \to \text{Group Layout (GT)}}Medium Volume + Medium Variety→Group Layout (GT)​

3.2 — Work Study, Method Study & Process Charts

1. Concept Pointers
  • Work Study: Includes Method Study (to improve work methods) and Work Measurement (to set standard times).
  • Basic Tool of Work Study: Stop-watch.
  • ASME Process Chart Symbols:
    • Operation (○): Represents a main step where raw material is altered in its physical/chemical characteristics (e.g., turning, drilling, painting).
    • Transport (→ or ⇒): Represents movement of workers, materials, or equipment from one location to another.
    • Inspection (□): Represents examination of quality or verification of quantity.
    • Delay / Temporary Storage (D): Represents temporary hesitation or waiting period between operations.
    • Storage (▽): Represents controlled permanent storage where material is held under authorization.
  • Combined Symbols: Used when two activities are performed simultaneously at the same workstation (e.g., painting while transporting on a chain conveyor →\to→ Circle inside Arrow).
  • Special Charts:
    • Flow Process Chart: Tracks material, worker, or equipment flow using standard ASME symbols.
    • Man-Machine Chart: Shows simultaneous progress of worker activities and machine loading to identify idle time.
    • Flow Chart Diamond Symbol: Represents a decision/branching node (evaluating two conditions).
    • SIMO Chart: Records therbligs against a time scale in winks; used for micro-motion method analysis.

3.3 — Work Measurement, Time Study & Allowances

1. Concept Pointers
  • Work Measurement Definition: Application of techniques to establish the time for a qualified worker to carry out a specified job at a defined level of performance.
  • Techniques of Work Measurement: Stop-watch Time Study, Synthesis from standard data, Work Sampling, PMTS, Analytical Estimating. (Random sampling is NOT a distinct work measurement technique name).
  • Time Study Protocol:
    • Carried out for an average worker operating at normal pace.
    • Rating factor converts Observed Time (OT) to Normal Time (NT): NT=OT×Rating FactorNT = OT \times \text{Rating Factor}NT=OT×Rating Factor.
    • Performance rating factor is also used for fixation of incentive rates.
  • Allowances:
    • Added to Normal Time to calculate Standard Time (ST): ST=NT×(1+A)=NT+AllowancesST = NT \times (1 + A) = NT + \text{Allowances}ST=NT×(1+A)=NT+Allowances.
    • Allowances cover personal needs, fatigue, contingency, and process delays.
    • Allowances are ALWAYS calculated as a percentage of Normal Time (never Observed Time).
    • Interference Allowance: Extra time granted when a single worker attends more than two machines simultaneously.
  • Work Sampling:
    • Statistical technique based on probability theory.
    • Sample observations MUST be taken using a table of random numbers to avoid bias.
    • Sample size formula for 95%95\%95% confidence (Z≈2Z \approx 2Z≈2): N=4p(1−p)A2N = \frac{4p(1-p)}{A^2}N=A24p(1−p)​.

  • Question: In work sampling, at a 95%95\%95% confidence level, the required number of observations NNN for activity proportion ppp and absolute accuracy AAA is given by:

3.4 — Therbligs, Micro-motion Study & PMTS

1. Concept Pointers
  • Therbligs:
    • 18 fundamental motion elements introduced by Frank and Lillian Gilbreth to analyze micro-motions.
    • Count: Exactly 18 fundamental hand/eye motions.
    • Divided into effective therbligs (Reach, Move, Grasp, Release) and ineffective therbligs (Hold, Delay, Search, Select).
  • Micro-motion Analysis:
    • Motion analysis represents an operation in terms of individual basic motions of the worker.
    • Chronocyclegraph: Uses pulsating light source attached to worker hands to record path, velocity, and direction of movement.
  • SIMO Chart (Simultaneous Motion Chart):
    • Records therbligs of left hand and right hand against a micro-time scale.
    • Time unit: Wink (1 Wink=1/2000 minute=0.03 seconds1 \text{ Wink} = 1/2000 \text{ minute} = 0.03 \text{ seconds}1 Wink=1/2000 minute=0.03 seconds).
  • PMTS (Predetermined Motion Time Systems):
    • Standardized systems providing predetermined time values for basic human motions.
    • Examples: MTM (Method-Time Measurement), Work-Factor System, Basic Motion Time Study (BMT).
    • Stop-watch time study is NOT a PMTS system (it measures actual elapsed time directly).

3.5 — Organization Structures & Job Concepts

1. Concept Pointers
  • Line Organization (Scalar / Military Organization):
    • Oldest and simplest organization structure.
    • Authority flows vertically downwards in a direct scalar chain.
    • Advantages: Strong discipline, quick decision making, and simple to start (all of these).
  • Line and Staff Organization: Adds staff specialists to advise line executives.
  • Functional Organization: Functional specialists have direct authority over their function.
  • Complex Industry Preference: Large/complex industries (like the steel industry) prefer a combination of Line, Staff, and Functional organization.
  • Job Analysis vs Job Evaluation:
    • Job Analysis: Process of discovering and identifying pertinent information relative to the nature and duties of a specific job.
    • Job Evaluation: Systematic method of determining the relative monetary value / worth of different jobs within an organization.
  • Job Design Factors: Influenced by individual differences, technology, organization structure, and internal climate. (Job design is NOT a process of organizational communication).
  • Management Principles:
    • Promotion: Movement within an organization to a higher position with greater responsibility.
    • Controlling: Managerial function involving measurement and correction of performance against plans.
    • MBO (Management by Objectives): Process of setting mutually agreed goals at all levels of the organization.
2. Worked Example

Worked Example 1: Organization Type for Steel Industry & Military Analogy

  • Question: (1) Military organization is another name for which layout/structure? (2) Which organization structure is preferred for a steel industry?
  • Solution:
    1. Military organization = Line organization.
    2. Steel industry (complex manufacturing) = Line, Staff, and Functional organization.
  • Key Insight: Line organization is scalar and direct (like the military). Complex continuous plants require line authority combined with staff and functional expertise.

3.6 — Value Engineering & Value Analysis

1. Concept Pointers
  • Core Definition of Value: Ratio of Function (Utility) to Cost.
    V=FC=Utility of ProductCost of Product\boxed{V = \frac{F}{C} = \frac{\text{Utility of Product}}{\text{Cost of Product}}}V=CF​=Cost of ProductUtility of Product​​
  • Objective of Value Engineering: Eliminate unnecessary costs without impairing required function, quality, performance, or safety.
  • Timing of Application:
    • Value Engineering (VE) is applied primarily at the design / pre-manufacturing stage.
    • Value Analysis (VA) is applied to existing products already in production.
  • Triggers for Value Engineering:
    1. Introduction of new product designs.
    2. Firm inability to meet delivery schedules / cost targets.
    3. Falling Return on Investment (ROI).
    • (Necessary under all three conditions).
  • Value Engineering vs Conventional Cost Reduction:
    • Conventional cost reduction often widens tolerances or cheapens materials (impairing quality).
    • VE focuses systematically on function analysis to lower cost while maintaining or enhancing quality.
  • Industrial Designer Role: Creates aesthetically pleasing forms, shapes, and ergonomics for products.
  • Concurrent Engineering: Approach where product design and manufacturing process planning are conducted simultaneously.
