1. Impact of Jets

When a fluid jet strikes a plate or vane, the impulse-momentum principle gives the resulting force. The key distinction is between a single plate and a series of vanes — the latter is relevant to turbine analysis.

1.1 Jet on a Fixed Normal Flat Plate

$$F = \rho A V^2$$
  • $A$ — jet cross-sectional area (m²); $V$ — jet velocity (m/s)
  • The jet loses all normal momentum on impact and spreads along the plate

1.2 Jet on a Single Moving Normal Flat Plate (velocity $u$)

$$F = \rho A (V - u)^2$$
$$\text{Power (work done per second)} = F \cdot u = \rho A (V-u)^2 u$$
  • $(V - u)$ = relative velocity of jet with respect to the moving plate
  • Mass flow rate striking plate per second = $\rho A (V - u)$, not $\rho AV$

1.3 Jet on a Series of Moving Flat Vanes (Wheel)

When the plate is one of many on a wheel, every part of the jet is continuously intercepted. The entire jet mass per second strikes the vanes:

$$F = \rho A V (V - u)$$
$$\eta = \frac{2u(V-u)}{V^2}, \qquad \eta_{\max} = 50\% \text{ at } u = \frac{V}{2}$$
  • Maximum efficiency 50% at bucket speed = half jet speed
  • In practice: single moving plate $F \propto (V-u)^2$; series of vanes $F \propto V(V-u)$

1.4 Jet on a Fixed Inclined Flat Plate (angle $\theta$ to plate surface)

$$F_n = \rho A V^2 \sin\theta$$
  • $F_n$ — force normal to the plate; $\theta$ = angle between jet and plate surface (not the normal)
  • Force along jet direction = $F_n \sin\theta$; force perpendicular to jet = $F_n \cos\theta$

1.5 Jet on a Moving Curved Vane

$$F_x = \rho A (V-u)^2(1 + \cos\phi)$$
$$\text{Power} = F_x \cdot u = \rho A (V-u)^2(1+\cos\phi)\,u$$
  • $\phi$ — exit angle of the vane (deflection angle from the inlet direction); for a semicircular vane $\phi = 180°$, giving maximum force
  • For series of curved vanes: $F_x = \rho A V(V-u)(1+\cos\phi)$

2. Water Hammer & Surge Tank

2.1 Water Hammer

Water hammer occurs when flow in a penstock or pipe is rapidly decelerated, usually by quick valve closure. The kinetic energy of the moving water column converts into a high-pressure acoustic wave that travels back and forth along the pipe. The wave speed depends on pipe elasticity and fluid compressibility.

$$t_{cr} = \frac{2L}{C} \qquad \text{(critical valve closure time)}$$
  • $L$ — pipe length from reservoir to valve (m); $C$ — pressure wave speed (m/s)
  • If valve closes in $t \le t_{cr}$: sudden (rapid) closure — maximum water hammer pressure
  • If valve closes in $t > t_{cr}$: gradual closure — reduced pressure surge
  • Pressure rise for instantaneous closure: $\Delta P = \rho C V$ where $V$ = initial flow velocity

2.2 Surge Tank

A surge tank is an open standpipe or chamber connected to the penstock near the turbine. It provides a nearby reservoir that limits the rate of pressure change when the turbine load changes.

  • During load rejection (turbine shut down): water flows into surge tank, limiting pressure rise
  • During load acceptance (turbine starting): surge tank supplies water, preventing excessive pressure drop
  • Placement: as close to the turbine as possible for maximum effectiveness

3. Hydraulic Turbines

Hydraulic turbines convert the potential and kinetic energy of water into mechanical shaft energy. They are classified as impulse (all energy in kinetic form before runner) or reaction (mixed pressure and kinetic energy at runner entry).

3.2 Specific Speed

Specific speed $N_s$ is the speed of a geometrically similar turbine of unit size developing unit power under unit head. It is used to select the appropriate turbine type for given site conditions.

$$N_s = \frac{N\sqrt{P}}{H^{5/4}}$$
  • $N$ — rotational speed (rpm); $P$ — shaft power output (kW or metric HP); $H$ — net head (m)
  • Low $N_s$ → high head, low flow → Pelton. High $N_s$ → low head, high flow → Kaplan.

3.3 Pelton Wheel (Impulse Turbine)

The Pelton wheel is the only commercially used impulse turbine. A high-speed free jet of water strikes double-cupped buckets mounted on the runner periphery. All the hydraulic energy is converted to kinetic energy in the nozzle before entry.

$$V_1 = C_v\sqrt{2gH} \qquad \text{(jet velocity)}$$
$$u = \phi\sqrt{2gH} \qquad \text{(bucket/peripheral speed; } \phi \approx 0.43\text{–}0.48\text{)}$$
$$\eta_{h,\max} = \frac{1+\cos\phi}{2} \quad \text{at } u = \frac{V_1}{2}$$
$$Z = 15 + \frac{D}{2d} = 15 + \frac{m}{2} \qquad \text{(number of buckets, Taygun's formula)}$$
  • $C_v$ — nozzle velocity coefficient (≈ 0.97–0.99); $\phi$ — speed ratio
  • $m = D/d$ — jet ratio (D = runner diameter, d = jet diameter); typically $m = 11$–15
  • $\phi$ in efficiency formula is vane exit angle; for perfect reversal $\phi = 180°$, but friction makes real $\phi < 180°$
  • Optimum bucket speed $u = V_1/2$ gives maximum hydraulic efficiency

3.4 Francis Turbine (Inward-Flow Reaction Turbine)

Water enters radially inward through guide vanes (wicket gates) and exits axially. Both pressure energy and kinetic energy drive the runner. The guide vane angle is adjustable for part-load efficiency.

$$Q = \pi D_1 B_1 V_{f1} = \pi D_2 B_2 V_{f2} \qquad \text{(discharge; continuity at inlet/outlet)}$$
$$\eta_h = \frac{V_{w1}u_1}{gH} \qquad \text{(hydraulic efficiency; radial exit: } V_{w2}=0\text{)}$$
  • $D_1, D_2$ — inlet/outlet runner diameter; $B_1, B_2$ — width at inlet/outlet; $V_{f1}, V_{f2}$ — radial (flow) velocity
  • $V_{w1}$ — whirl (tangential) component of absolute velocity at runner inlet
  • For maximum efficiency, design for radial exit: $V_{w2} = 0$ (no swirl leaving runner)

3.5 Kaplan Turbine (Axial-Flow Reaction Turbine)

Similar to Francis but flow is axial rather than radial. Both runner blades and guide vanes are adjustable, giving excellent part-load efficiency over a wide range of heads and flows.

$$Q = \frac{\pi}{4}(D_o^2 - D_b^2)V_f$$
  • $D_o$ — outer tip diameter; $D_b$ — hub (boss) diameter; $V_f$ — axial flow velocity
  • Suitable for very low heads (1–30 m) with high discharges
  • Propeller turbine = fixed blade Kaplan (no adjustment)

3.6 Draft Tube

A diverging tube fitted below the runner of a reaction turbine. It recovers the exit kinetic energy as pressure, allowing the turbine to be installed above the tailrace level without sacrificing net head.

$$\eta_d = \frac{V_1^2/(2g) - V_2^2/(2g) - h_f}{V_1^2/(2g)}$$
  • $V_1$ — velocity at draft tube inlet (runner exit); $V_2$ — velocity at draft tube outlet; $h_f$ — friction loss in draft tube
  • A well-designed draft tube converts most of the runner exit kinetic energy into useful pressure

  • Unit quantities allow comparison of turbines operating at different heads; used for model-prototype scaling

3.8 Cavitation in Turbines

Cavitation occurs when local pressure falls below the vapour pressure, forming vapour bubbles that implode violently near runner surfaces. This causes pitting, vibration, and noise. Kaplan and Francis turbines are more susceptible than Pelton wheels.

$$\sigma = \frac{H_a - H_v - H_s}{H} \qquad \text{(Thoma's cavitation parameter)}$$
  • $H_a$ — atmospheric pressure head; $H_v$ — vapour pressure head; $H_s$ — suction head (draft tube height)
  • Cavitation avoided if $\sigma > \sigma_\text{critical}$ (specific to turbine type)
  • Reducing $H_s$ (lower turbine installation) reduces cavitation risk

Key Points to Remember

  • Fixed plate: $F = \rho AV^2$. Single moving plate: $F = \rho A(V-u)^2$. Series of moving vanes: $F = \rho AV(V-u)$ — full jet mass rate continuously intercepted.
  • Maximum efficiency for flat vane series: $\eta_{\max} = 50\%$ at $u = V/2$.
  • Moving curved vane: $F_x = \rho A(V-u)^2(1+\cos\phi)$. Maximum when $\phi = 180°$ (semicircular vane).
  • Water hammer critical time: $t_{cr} = 2L/C$. Closure faster than $t_{cr}$ = sudden closure (maximum pressure surge).
  • Pelton: impulse; high head (>250 m); atmospheric pressure at runner; jet velocity $V_1 = C_v\sqrt{2gH}$; optimum $u = V_1/2$.
  • Pelton max hydraulic efficiency: $\eta_h = (1+\cos\phi)/2$ where $\phi$ is vane exit angle. Perfect reversal ($\phi = 180°$) gives $\eta_h = 100\%$ theoretically.
  • Taygun's formula: $Z = 15 + D/(2d)$ buckets. Jet ratio $m = D/d \approx 11$–15.
  • Francis: reaction; medium head (30–600 m); inward radial flow. For max efficiency: design for $V_{w2} = 0$ (no exit swirl).
  • Kaplan: reaction; low head (<30 m); axial flow; adjustable blades — best part-load performance of all turbine types.
  • Specific speed $N_s = N\sqrt{P}/H^{5/4}$: Pelton 8–70; Francis 50–300; Kaplan 300–1000. Higher $N_s$ → lower head, higher flow.
  • Draft tube recovers exit kinetic energy as pressure; allows turbine to be placed above tailrace without losing effective head.
  • Cavitation: $\sigma = (H_a - H_v - H_s)/H$. Reduce $H_s$ (install turbine lower) to avoid cavitation. Pelton wheels are not susceptible (operate at atmospheric pressure).
  • Unit quantities ($N_u$, $Q_u$, $P_u$) remove the effect of head, allowing model-to-prototype scaling and performance curve comparison.
