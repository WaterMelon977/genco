1. Flow Regimes & Reynolds Number

The Reynolds number $Re$ is the ratio of inertia forces to viscous forces and is the primary parameter governing whether pipe flow is laminar or turbulent.

$$Re = \frac{\rho V D}{\mu} = \frac{V D}{\nu}$$
  • $V$ — mean velocity (m/s); $D$ — pipe internal diameter (m); $\nu = \mu/\rho$ — kinematic viscosity (m²/s)
  • $Re < 2300$ — laminar (viscous forces dominate; smooth parallel layers)
  • $2300 < Re < 4000$ — transitional (unstable; either regime possible)
  • $Re > 4000$ — turbulent (inertia forces dominate; chaotic mixing)

2. Laminar Pipe Flow (Hagen-Poiseuille)

Fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe produces a parabolic velocity profile. The flow is driven entirely by a pressure gradient; viscosity controls the velocity distribution.

2.1 Velocity Profile & Mean Velocity

$$u(r) = u_{\max}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right), \qquad u_{\max} = 2\,V_{\text{avg}}$$
  • $r$ — radial distance from centreline (m); $R$ — pipe radius (m)
  • Maximum velocity at centreline is exactly twice the cross-sectional average

2.2 Pressure Drop & Head Loss

$$\Delta P = \frac{32\,\mu\,V\,L}{D^2} = \frac{128\,\mu\,Q\,L}{\pi D^4}$$
$$h_f = \frac{32\,\mu\,V\,L}{\rho\,g\,D^2} = \frac{128\,\mu\,Q\,L}{\rho\,g\,\pi\,D^4}$$
  • Valid for: fully developed, steady, incompressible, laminar ($Re < 2300$) flow in circular pipes
  • Head loss proportional to $V^1$ (or $Q^1$) — first power, unlike turbulent where $h_f \propto V^2$

2.3 Wall Shear Stress

$$\tau_w = \frac{\Delta P \cdot D}{4L} = \frac{f\,\rho\,V^2}{8}$$

2.4 Friction Factor (Laminar)

$$f = \frac{64}{Re} \qquad \text{(Darcy friction factor, laminar pipe)}$$
  • Independent of pipe roughness in laminar flow — roughness has no effect when $Re < 2300$

3. Pipe Friction Losses (Major Losses)

Friction loss — also called major loss — occurs along the full length of a pipe due to fluid viscosity and wall roughness. It is given by the Darcy-Weisbach equation, valid for both laminar and turbulent flows.

$$h_f = \frac{f\,L\,V^2}{2\,g\,D} = \frac{8\,f\,L\,Q^2}{\pi^2\,g\,D^5}$$
  • $f$ — Darcy friction factor (dimensionless); note $f_\text{Darcy} = 4\,f_\text{Fanning}$
  • $h_f \propto L$, $h_f \propto V^2$, $h_f \propto D^{-5}$ for constant $Q$ — diameter has the greatest sensitivity

4. Minor Losses (Local Losses)

Minor losses arise at pipe fittings, changes in section, bends, and valves. In short pipelines with many fittings they can exceed friction losses. All minor losses follow the form $h_L = K\,V^2/2g$.

5. TEL, HGL & Power Transmission

The Total Energy Line (TEL) plots total head $= p/\rho g + V^2/2g + z$ along the pipe. The Hydraulic Gradient Line (HGL) plots piezometric head $= p/\rho g + z$. For real (viscous) fluids, TEL always slopes downward in the direction of flow.

$$\text{TEL} = \text{HGL} + \frac{V^2}{2g} \qquad \text{(gap is always the velocity head)}$$
  • If pipe diameter increases downstream: velocity decreases, so HGL rises toward TEL
  • TEL never rises unless a pump adds energy; HGL can rise locally at diameter expansions
  • Slope of TEL = energy gradient $i = h_f/L = fV^2/(2gD)$

5.1 Power Transmitted Through a Pipe

$$P = \rho\,g\,Q\,(H - h_f)$$
$$\text{Maximum power when } h_f = \frac{H}{3}, \quad \eta_{\max} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 66.7\%$$
  • $H$ — total available head at inlet (m); $h_f$ — friction head loss along pipe (m)
  • At maximum power condition, one-third of supply head is consumed by friction

5.2 Siphon

A siphon carries fluid over an elevation higher than its source. The summit pressure must remain above the liquid's vapour pressure to prevent cavitation and flow interruption. In practice, the summit is limited to approximately 7.75 m above the HGL for water at normal temperatures.

6. Pipe Networks

6.1 Pipes in Series

For pipes in series, the same discharge flows through each pipe and head losses add up. The equivalent pipe satisfies Dupuit's equation:

$$\frac{L_\text{eq}}{D_\text{eq}^5} = \frac{L_1}{D_1^5} + \frac{L_2}{D_2^5} + \frac{L_3}{D_3^5} + \cdots$$

6.2 Pipes in Parallel

For pipes in parallel, head loss is equal across all branches and discharges add up. Replacing one pipe of diameter $D$ with $n$ pipes of equal diameter $d$ giving the same flow:

$$D = d\cdot n^{2/5}$$

6.3 Non-Circular Ducts — Hydraulic Diameter

$$D_h = \frac{4A}{P}, \qquad m = \frac{A}{P} = \frac{D_h}{4}$$
  • $A$ — cross-sectional area (m²); $P$ — wetted perimeter (m)
  • Use $D_h$ in place of $D$ in Darcy-Weisbach and Reynolds number for non-circular sections
  • Circular pipe full: $D_h = D$; square duct side $a$: $D_h = a$

6.4 Chezy Formula

$$V = C\sqrt{m\,i}$$
  • $C$ — Chezy coefficient (m¹/²/s); $m$ — hydraulic mean depth = $A/P$; $i$ — hydraulic gradient = $h_f/L$
  • Relationship to Darcy: $C = \sqrt{8g/f}$

7. Boundary Layer Theory

When a real fluid flows over a solid surface, viscosity causes a region near the surface — the boundary layer — where velocity increases from zero at the wall to the free-stream value $U_\infty$ at the boundary layer edge. Outside the boundary layer, flow is essentially inviscid.

7.1 Boundary Layer Thickness Definitions

The boundary layer thickness $\delta$ is defined as the distance from the wall where $u = 0.99\,U_\infty$. Three integral thickness definitions characterise the mass-flow, momentum, and energy deficits:

$$\delta^* = \int_0^\delta \left(1 - \frac{u}{U_\infty}\right)dy \qquad \text{(displacement thickness)}$$
$$\theta = \int_0^\delta \frac{u}{U_\infty}\left(1 - \frac{u}{U_\infty}\right)dy \qquad \text{(momentum thickness)}$$
$$\delta_e = \int_0^\delta \frac{u}{U_\infty}\left(1 - \frac{u^2}{U_\infty^2}\right)dy \qquad \text{(energy thickness)}$$
  • Shape factor: $H = \delta^*/\theta$; $H \approx 2.59$ (laminar Blasius); $H \approx 1.3$–$1.4$ (turbulent)
  • As $H$ increases above the turbulent value, separation is imminent

7.2 Boundary Layer Growth on a Flat Plate

Regime$\delta/x$Growth law$Re_\text{crit}$
Laminar (Blasius)$5/\sqrt{Re_x}$$\delta \propto x^{1/2}$$Re_x < 5\times10^5$
Turbulent (1/7-power)$0.37/Re_x^{1/5}$$\delta \propto x^{4/5}$$Re_x > 5\times10^5$
  • $Re_x = U_\infty x/\nu$; turbulent boundary layer grows faster than laminar
  • Laminar displacement thickness: $\delta^* = \delta/3$ (linear profile) to $\delta^*/\delta \approx 0.344$ (Blasius)

7.3 Boundary Layer Separation

Separation occurs when an adverse pressure gradient ($dp/dx > 0$, decelerating flow) causes the near-wall fluid to reverse direction. At the separation point, the wall shear stress vanishes: $\tau_w = 0$.

  • After separation, a turbulent wake with recirculating flow forms downstream
  • Turbulent boundary layers resist separation better than laminar ones (higher near-wall momentum)
  • Streamlined bodies delay separation; bluff bodies (cylinders, spheres) separate early causing high form drag

Key Points to Remember

  • Darcy-Weisbach: $h_f = fLV^2/(2gD)$. For laminar flow $f = 64/Re$. For smooth turbulent (Blasius): $f = 0.316\,Re^{-0.25}$ (valid up to $Re = 10^5$).
  • Hagen-Poiseuille: $\Delta P = 32\mu VL/D^2$. Laminar head loss $\propto V^1$; turbulent $\propto V^2$. Centreline velocity $u_{\max} = 2V_{\text{avg}}$ (laminar pipe).
  • Sudden expansion loss: $h_L = (V_1-V_2)^2/2g$ (Borda-Carnot). Never use $V_1^2 - V_2^2$ — it is the squared difference, not the difference of squares.
  • Exit loss $K = 1.0$ (all kinetic energy lost); entrance loss $K = 0.5$ (sharp-edged); contraction $K \approx 0.5$.
  • TEL always above HGL by $V^2/2g$. TEL slopes downward continuously for real fluids. HGL can rise locally where pipe expands and velocity decreases.
  • Maximum power transmitted through a pipe: $h_f = H/3$; efficiency = 2/3 ≈ 66.7%.
  • Pipes in series: head losses add, $Q$ same. Pipes in parallel: heads same, $Q$ adds. Equivalent pipe uses Dupuit equation: $L/D^5 = \Sigma L_i/D_i^5$.
  • Hydraulic diameter for non-circular sections: $D_h = 4A/P$. Use in Darcy equation and Reynolds number in place of $D$.
  • Boundary layer thickness on flat plate: laminar $\delta \propto x^{1/2}$ (Blasius $\delta/x = 5/\sqrt{Re_x}$); turbulent $\delta \propto x^{4/5}$ (1/7-power law).
  • Displacement thickness $\delta^*$ = mass-flow deficit distance; momentum thickness $\theta$ = momentum deficit distance.
  • Separation occurs at zero wall shear ($\tau_w = 0$) under adverse pressure gradient. Turbulent BL separates later than laminar.
  • Kinetic energy correction $\alpha = 2$ (laminar); momentum correction $\beta = 4/3$ (laminar). Both approach 1.0 for turbulent flow.
  • Laminar pipe flow: roughness has no effect on $f$. Turbulent flow: $f$ depends on both $Re$ and relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$ (Moody diagram).
