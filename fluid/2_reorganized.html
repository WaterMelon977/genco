2.1 — All Formulas (with symbols & units)

1. Major & Minor Pipe Losses

  • Darcy-Weisbach Formula (Major Friction Loss):
    $${\boxed{h_f = \frac{f L V^2}{2 g D} = \frac{8 f L Q^2}{\pi^2 g D^5}}}$$
  • $h_f$: head loss due to friction $\text{m}$
  • $f$: Darcy friction factor (dimensionless; $f = 4 f'$, where $f'$ is Fanning friction factor)
  • $L$: pipe length $\text{m}$
  • $V$: mean flow velocity $\text{m/s}$
  • $D$: pipe diameter $\text{m}$

  • $Q$: volume flow rate / discharge $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$

  • Laminar Darcy Friction Factor:

    $${\boxed{f = \frac{64}{Re}}}$$

  • Sudden Expansion Loss (Borda-Carnot):

    $${\boxed{h_L = \frac{(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2g} = \frac{V_1^2}{2g} \left(1 - \frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)^2 = \frac{V_1^2}{2g} \left(1 - \frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2}\right)^2}}$$

  • Sudden Contraction Loss:

    $${\boxed{h_L = \frac{V_2^2}{2g} \left(\frac{1}{C_c} - 1\right)^2 \approx 0.5 \frac{V_2^2}{2g}}}$$

  • Pipe Entrance Loss (Sharp-edged):

    $${\boxed{h_L = 0.5 \frac{V^2}{2g}}}$$

  • Pipe Exit Loss:

    $${\boxed{h_L = 1.0 \frac{V^2}{2g}}}$$

  • Bend / Fitting / Valve Loss:

    $${\boxed{h_L = K \frac{V^2}{2g}}}$$

2. HGL, TEL & Power Transmission

  • Total Energy Line & Hydraulic Gradient Line Relationship:

    $${\boxed{\text{TEL} = \text{HGL} + \frac{V^2}{2g}}}$$
    $${\text{TEL} = \frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z, \quad \text{HGL} = \frac{p}{\rho g} + z}$$

  • Energy Gradient (Slope of TEL):

    $${\boxed{i = \frac{h_f}{L} = \frac{f V^2}{2 g D}}}$$

  • Power Transmitted through Pipe:

    $${\boxed{P = \rho g Q (H - h_f)}}$$

  • Maximum Power Transmission Condition & Efficiency:

    $${\boxed{h_f = \frac{H}{3} \quad \implies \quad \eta_{\max} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 66.7\%}}$$

3. Pipe Networks & Non-Circular Ducts

  • Series Pipes Equivalent Length / Diameter (Dupuit's Equation):

    $${\boxed{\frac{L_{\text{eq}}}{D_{\text{eq}}^5} = \frac{L_1}{D_1^5} + \frac{L_2}{D_2^5} + \frac{L_3}{D_3^5} + \cdots}}$$

  • Replacing Single Pipe of Diameter $D$ by $n$ Parallel Pipes of Diameter $d$:

    $${\boxed{D = d \cdot n^{2/5}}}$$

  • Hydraulic Diameter $D_h$ & Hydraulic Mean Depth $m$:

    $${\boxed{D_h = \frac{4A}{P}, \quad m = \frac{A}{P}}}$$

  • Circular pipe full: $D_h = D, \quad m = \frac{D}{4}$

  • Square duct side $a$: $D_h = a, \quad m = \frac{a}{4}$

  • Chezy Formula:

    $${\boxed{V = C \sqrt{m i}}}$$

4. Laminar & Turbulent Flow in Pipes

  • Hagen-Poiseuille Pressure Drop (Laminar Pipe Flow):

    $${\boxed{\Delta P = \frac{32 \mu V L}{D^2} = \frac{128 \mu Q L}{\pi D^4}}}$$

  • Parabolic Velocity Profile (Laminar Pipe):

    $${\boxed{u(r) = u_{\max} \left(1 - \left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^2\right)}}$$
    $${\boxed{u_{\max} = 2 V_{\text{avg}}}}$$

  • Wall Shear Stress (Laminar Pipe):

    $${\boxed{\tau_w = \frac{\Delta P \cdot D}{4 L} = \frac{f \rho V^2}{8}}}$$

  • Blasius Friction Factor (Smooth Turbulent Pipe, $Re < 10^5$):

    $${\boxed{f = \frac{0.316}{Re^{0.25}}}}$$

5. Boundary Layer Theory

  • Displacement Thickness $\delta^*$:

    $${\boxed{\delta^* = \int_0^\delta \left(1 - \frac{u}{U_\infty}\right) dy}}$$

  • Momentum Thickness $\theta$:

    $${\boxed{\theta = \int_0^\delta \frac{u}{U_\infty} \left(1 - \frac{u}{U_\infty}\right) dy}}$$

  • Energy Thickness $\delta_e$:

    $${\boxed{\delta_e = \int_0^\delta \frac{u}{U_\infty} \left(1 - \left(\frac{u}{U_\infty}\right)^2\right) dy}}$$

  • Laminar Boundary Layer Thickness (Flat Plate, Blasius):

    $${\boxed{\frac{\delta}{x} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{Re_x}} \quad \implies \quad \delta \propto x^{1/2}}}$$

  • Turbulent Boundary Layer Thickness (Flat Plate, 1/7-Power Law):

    $${\boxed{\frac{\delta}{x} \approx \frac{0.37}{Re_x^{1/5}} \quad \implies \quad \delta \propto x^{4/5}}}$$

  • Nikuradse Hydrodynamic Roughness Classification:

  • Smooth: $\frac{k}{\delta'} < 0.25$
  • Transition: $0.25 \le \frac{k}{\delta'} \le 6.0$
  • Fully Rough: $\frac{k}{\delta'} > 6.0$

2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Major Loss: Head loss caused by fluid viscosity and pipe wall friction along the length of a pipe $h_f$.
  • Minor Loss: Local head losses caused by flow disturbance at entrances, exits, expansions, contractions, bends, valves, and pipe fittings.
  • Total Energy Line (TEL): Graphical plot of total head $\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z$ along flow path; slopes continuously downward for real fluids.
  • Hydraulic Gradient Line (HGL): Graphical plot of piezometric head $\frac{p}{\rho g} + z$ along flow path; lies below TEL by velocity head $\frac{V^2}{2g}$.
  • Fully Developed Pipe Flow: Region where boundary layers merge and velocity profile becomes invariant along flow direction $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$.
  • Hagen-Poiseuille Flow: Fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe with parabolic velocity profile and centerline velocity twice the mean velocity $u_{\max} = 2 V_{\text{avg}}$.
  • Equivalent Pipe: Single uniform pipe of length $L_{\text{eq}}$ and diameter $D_{\text{eq}}$ that produces the exact same total head loss for the same discharge as a complex compound pipe system.
  • Hydraulic Diameter $D_h$: Equivalent diameter used in friction formulas for non-circular conduits $D_h = \frac{4A}{P}$.
  • Boundary Layer Thickness $\delta$: Distance from solid boundary to point where local velocity reaches $99\%$ of free-stream velocity $u = 0.99 U_\infty$.
  • Displacement Thickness $\delta^*$: Distance by which the solid boundary would need to be shifted outward to account for mass flow rate deficit in the boundary layer.
  • Momentum Thickness $\theta$: Distance by which the solid boundary would need to be shifted to account for momentum flux deficit.
  • Boundary Layer Separation: Phenomenon where boundary layer detaches from body surface due to adverse pressure gradient $\frac{dp}{dx} > 0$ decelerating near-wall fluid until wall shear stress vanishes $\tau_w = 0$.
  • Laminar Sub-Layer $\delta'$: Extremely thin viscous sub-layer adjacent to wall in turbulent boundary layer flow.

2.3 — Standard Values & Constants

  • Critical Reynolds Number (Pipe Flow): $Re_{\text{cr}} \approx 2000$ (Laminar if $Re < 2000$; Turbulent if $Re > 4000$).
  • Critical Reynolds Number (Flat Plate): $Re_{x,\text{cr}} \approx 5 \times 10^5$.
  • Critical Reynolds Number (Open Channel): $Re_{\text{cr}} \approx 500$.
  • Laminar Pipe Friction Factor: $f = \frac{64}{Re}$ (Darcy), $f' = \frac{16}{Re}$ (Fanning).
  • Laminar Pipe Kinetic Energy Correction Factor $\alpha$: $\alpha = 2.0$.
  • Laminar Pipe Momentum Correction Factor $\beta$: $\beta = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$.
  • Maximum Power Transmission Efficiency: $\eta_{\max} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 66.7\%$ when $h_f = \frac{H}{3}$.
  • Siphon Maximum Suction Lift Limit: $h \approx 7.75 \text{ m of water}$ (practical limit to prevent cavitation).
  • Linear Velocity Profile Ratios $\frac{u}{U_\infty} = \frac{y}{\delta}$: $\frac{\delta^*}{\delta} = \frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{\theta}{\delta} = \frac{1}{6}$.

SECTION 3: MAIN BODY

3.1 Major & Minor Pipe Losses

1. Concept Pointers
  • Pipe flow losses are categorized into major losses (friction along length) and minor losses (local fitting disturbances).
  • In long pipelines $L \gg D$, major friction loss dominates, making minor losses negligible.
  • In short, complex pipelines with multiple fittings, minor losses can equal or exceed friction loss.
💡 Key Concept: Darcy-Weisbach Equation: Major head loss $h_f = \frac{f L V^2}{2 g D} = \frac{8 f L Q^2}{\pi^2 g D^5}$. Notice $h_f \propto L$, $h_f \propto V^2$, and $h_f \propto D^{-5}$ for constant discharge $Q$!

[!TRAP] Sudden Expansion Loss (Borda-Carnot): $h_L = \frac{(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2g}$. NEVER confuse this with $\frac{V_1^2 - V_2^2}{2g}$! Expansion loss depends on the square of velocity difference, not difference of velocity squares.

[!FOCUS] Minor Loss Summary: - Sudden Expansion: $h_L = \frac{(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2g}$ - Sudden Contraction: $h_L = \frac{V_2^2}{2g} \left(\frac{1}{C_c} - 1\right)^2 \approx 0.5 \frac{V_2^2}{2g}$ - Sharp Entrance: $h_L = 0.5 \frac{V^2}{2g}$ - Exit: $h_L = 1.0 \frac{V^2}{2g}$

2. Decision Tree
Head Loss Question?
├─ Long Pipe Friction → Darcy-Weisbach: h_f = (f*L*V^2) / (2*g*D)
├─ Sudden Expansion → Borda-Carnot: h_L = (V1 - V2)^2 / (2*g)
├─ Sudden Contraction → h_L = 0.5 * V2^2 / (2*g)
└─ Laminar Flow → Hagen-Poiseuille: h_f = (32*mu*V*L) / (rho*g*D^2)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Friction is always the major loss in long pipelines.
  • [ ] $f = 64/Re$ for laminar; depends on $Re$ and $\epsilon/D$ for turbulent.
  • [ ] Expansion loss uses velocity difference $(V_1 - V_2)^2$.
  • [ ] Default contraction/entrance coefficient is $0.5$ when $C_c$ is omitted.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Water flows from a pipe of diameter $100 \text{ mm}$ $V_1 = 8 \text{ m/s}$ into a larger pipe of diameter $200 \text{ mm}$. Calculate the head loss due to sudden expansion $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$. - Solution:

$${A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies V_2 = V_1 \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2 = 8 \times \left(\frac{100}{200}\right)^2 = 8 \times \frac{1}{4} = 2 \text{ m/s}}$$
$${h_L = \frac{(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2g} = \frac{(8 - 2)^2}{2 \times 9.81} = \frac{36}{19.62} = 1.835 \text{ m of water}}$$
- Key Insight: Always calculate downstream velocity $V_2$ via continuity $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$ before using Borda-Carnot loss formula.

Worked Example 2 - Problem: Petrol $S = 0.7$ flows through a $200 \text{ mm}$ diameter pipe of length $1000 \text{ m}$ at a discharge $Q = 0.05 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$. If Darcy friction factor $f = 0.01$, calculate friction head loss. - Solution:

$${A = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.2)^2 = 0.0314 \text{ m}^2, \quad V = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.05}{0.0314} = 1.592 \text{ m/s}}$$
$${h_f = \frac{f L V^2}{2 g D} = \frac{0.01 \times 1000 \times (1.592)^2}{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.2} = \frac{25.34}{3.924} = 6.458 \text{ m of petrol}}$$
- Key Insight: Head loss $h_f$ computed from Darcy formula is expressed in meters of the flowing fluid (petrol column).

3.2 HGL, TEL & Power Transmission

1. Concept Pointers
  • TEL represents total energy per unit weight along the pipeline.
  • HGL represents piezometric head $p/\rho g + z$ along the pipeline.
  • For real fluids, TEL always slopes downward in direction of flow due to friction losses.
  • Maximum power transmitted through a pipe occurs when one-third of total available head is lost in friction $h_f = H/3$.
💡 Key Concept: TEL and HGL Gap: $\text{TEL} - \text{HGL} = \frac{V^2}{2g}$. The gap between TEL and HGL equals velocity head. HGL can rise locally if pipe diameter increases $V \downarrow \implies \frac{V^2}{2g} \downarrow$, but TEL NEVER rises unless a pump is inserted.

[!TRAP] Power Transmission Ratio: At maximum power transmission, $h_f = \frac{H}{3}$. The ratio of head lost in friction to total supply head is $1:3$, and maximum transmission efficiency is $\eta_{\max} = \frac{H - H/3}{H} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 66.7\%$.

[!FOCUS] Siphon Limit: In a siphon pipe, the summit must not be placed more than $7.75 \text{ m}$ above the HGL to prevent liquid pressure from dropping below vapour pressure and causing cavitation/air lock.

2. Decision Tree
Power / Hydraulic Line Problem?
├─ TEL vs HGL Gap → Vertical distance = V^2 / (2*g)
├─ Maximum Power Condition → Set h_f = H / 3
├─ Maximum Efficiency → 66.7%
└─ Siphon Summit Pressure → Pressure must be > Vapour Pressure (-7.75m water gauge)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] TEL lies strictly above HGL by velocity head $\frac{V^2}{2g}$.
  • [ ] Slope of TEL = energy gradient $i = h_f/L$.
  • [ ] Max power transmission occurs when $h_f = H/3$.
  • [ ] Siphon summit max elevation $\approx 7.75 \text{ m}$ above HGL.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A reservoir supplies water through a $1000 \text{ m}$ long pipe under a total head of $90 \text{ m}$. Find the maximum hydraulic power that can be transmitted through the pipe $\rho = 1000 \text{ kg/m}^3, g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$ when $Q = 0.05 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$. - Solution:

$${h_{f,\text{opt}} = \frac{H}{3} = \frac{90}{3} = 30 \text{ m}}$$
$${H_{\text{net}} = H - h_f = 90 - 30 = 60 \text{ m}}$$
$${P_{\max} = \rho g Q H_{\text{net}} = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.05 \times 60 = 29.43 \text{ kW}}$$
- Key Insight: Net available head at maximum power is $H - H/3 = 2H/3 = 60 \text{ m}$.

3.3 Hagen-Poiseuille Laminar Pipe Flow

1. Concept Pointers
  • Fully developed laminar pipe flow $Re < 2000$ exhibits a parabolic velocity profile.
  • Maximum velocity $u_{\max}$ occurs at the centerline $r = 0$.
  • Average velocity $V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u_{\max}}{2}$. Average velocity occurs at radius $r = \frac{R}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 R$.
  • Wall shear stress $\tau_w$ varies linearly across cross-section from zero at center to maximum at wall.
  • Friction factor $f = \frac{64}{Re}$ (Darcy), independent of pipe roughness.
💡 Key Concept: Hagen-Poiseuille Pressure Drop: $\Delta P = \frac{32 \mu V L}{D^2} = \frac{128 \mu Q L}{\pi D^4}$. Head loss $h_f \propto V$ for laminar flow, whereas $h_f \propto V^2$ for turbulent flow!

[!TRAP] Darcy vs Fanning Friction Factor: $f_{\text{Darcy}} = \frac{64}{Re}$ while $f'{\text{Fanning}} = \frac{16}{Re}$. $f{\text{Darcy}} = 4 f'_{\text{Fanning}}$. Always check which friction factor is requested!

[!FOCUS] Correction Factors for Laminar Pipe: - Kinetic Energy Correction Factor: $\alpha = 2.0$ - Momentum Correction Factor: $\beta = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$

2. Decision Tree
Laminar Pipe Flow Problem?
├─ Velocity Ratio → u_max = 2 * V_avg
├─ Mean Velocity Location → r = 0.707 * R
├─ Pressure Drop → Delta P = (32 * mu * V * L) / D^2
└─ Darcy Friction Factor → f = 64 / Re
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $u(r) = u_{\max} (1 - (r/R)^2)$.
  • [ ] Centerline velocity $u_{\max} = 2 V_{\text{avg}}$.
  • [ ] Shear stress $\tau = -\frac{r}{2} \frac{dp}{dx}$ (zero at center, max at wall).
  • [ ] Head loss $h_f \propto 1/D^4$ for fixed $Q$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Oil of dynamic viscosity $\mu = 0.8 \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ flows through a $50 \text{ mm}$ diameter pipe of length $100 \text{ m}$ at a centerline velocity of $2 \text{ m/s}$. Find the pressure drop and wall shear stress. - Solution:

$${u_{\max} = 2 \text{ m/s} \implies V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u_{\max}}{2} = 1 \text{ m/s}}$$
$${\Delta P = \frac{32 \mu V L}{D^2} = \frac{32 \times 0.8 \times 1 \times 100}{(0.05)^2} = \frac{2560}{0.0025} = 1,024,000 \text{ Pa} = 1.024 \text{ MPa}}$$
$${\tau_w = \frac{\Delta P \cdot D}{4 L} = \frac{1024000 \times 0.05}{4 \times 100} = \frac{51200}{400} = 128 \text{ Pa}}$$
- Key Insight: Use average velocity $V_{\text{avg}} = 1 \text{ m/s}$ (half of centerline velocity) in Hagen-Poiseuille formula.

3.4 Series/Parallel Pipe Systems & Non-Circular Ducts

1. Concept Pointers
  • Pipes in Series: Discharge $Q$ is constant throughout $Q_1 = Q_2 = Q_3$, and head losses are additive $h_{f,\text{total}} = h_{f1} + h_{f2} + h_{f3}$.
  • Pipes in Parallel: Head loss $h_f$ is equal across all branches $h_{f1} = h_{f2}$, and total discharge is additive $Q_{\text{total}} = Q_1 + Q_2$.
  • Equivalent Pipe: Replaces compound series system with single pipe of diameter $D_{\text{eq}}$ using Dupuit's equation $\frac{L_{\text{eq}}}{D_{\text{eq}}^5} = \sum \frac{L_i}{D_i^5}$.
  • Non-circular ducts use Hydraulic Diameter $D_h = \frac{4A}{P}$.
💡 Key Concept: Replacing One Pipe with $n$ Parallel Pipes: To replace a single pipe of diameter $D$ with $n$ parallel pipes of diameter $d$ of equal length and friction factor: $D = d \cdot n^{2/5}$ or $d = D \cdot n^{-2/5}$.

[!TRAP] Hydraulic Diameter vs Hydraulic Radius: - Hydraulic Mean Depth (Radius): $m = \frac{A}{P}$. For full circular pipe, $m = \frac{D}{4}$. - Hydraulic Diameter: $D_h = \frac{4A}{P} = 4m$. For full circular pipe, $D_h = D$. For square duct of side $a$, $D_h = a$.

2. Decision Tree
Pipe Network Problem?
├─ Series Network → Q same, h_f additive. Dupuit: L_eq / D_eq^5 = sum(L_i / D_i^5)
├─ Parallel Network → h_f same, Q additive
└─ Replace 1 pipe with n parallel pipes → D = d * n^(2/5)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Series: $Q_1 = Q_2$, $h_{f,\text{total}} = h_{f1} + h_{f2}$.
  • [ ] Parallel: $h_{f1} = h_{f2}$, $Q_{\text{total}} = Q_1 + Q_2$.
  • [ ] Dupuit's formula exponent is 5 $D^5$.
  • [ ] Non-circular duct hydraulic diameter $D_h = 4A/P$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A main pipe of diameter $D$ is to be replaced by $32$ parallel pipes of diameter $d$ having the same length and friction factor. Express $D$ in terms of $d$. - Solution:

$${D = d \cdot n^{2/5} = d \cdot (32)^{2/5} = d \cdot (2^5)^{2/5} = d \cdot 2^2 = 4 d}$$
- Key Insight: $D = 4d$. The diameter exponent is $2/5$ derived from $Q \propto D^{5/2}$.

3.5 Turbulent Flow Characteristics

1. Concept Pointers
  • Turbulent flow $Re > 4000$ is characterized by random, high-frequency velocity and pressure fluctuations.
  • Turbulent velocity profile is much flatter (logarithmic) compared to parabolic laminar profile.
  • Centerline to mean velocity ratio $V_{\max}/V_{\text{avg}} \approx 1.2$ for turbulent flow (compared to $2.0$ for laminar).
  • Shear stress in turbulent flow includes viscous shear plus turbulent Reynolds shear $\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy} + \eta \frac{du}{dy}$.
  • Fully rough turbulent pipe friction factor depends ONLY on relative roughness $\epsilon/D$, independent of $Re$.
💡 Key Concept: Scaling Laws for Diameter Reduction: For constant discharge $Q$ in turbulent flow: - If length $L$ is doubled $\implies h_f \times 2$. - If diameter $D$ is halved $D \to D/2$ $\implies h_f \propto D^{-5} \implies h_f \times 2^5 = 32$ times!

[!TRAP] Hydraulically Smooth vs Rough Walls: A wall is hydraulically smooth if surface roughness height $k$ is completely submerged inside the laminar sub-layer $\frac{k}{\delta'} < 0.25$. Smoothness is a flow-dependent property, not an absolute polish!

2. Decision Tree
Turbulent Pipe Flow Problem?
├─ Smooth Pipe Friction → Blasius: f = 0.316 / Re^0.25
├─ Fully Rough Pipe → f depends only on relative roughness (epsilon / D)
└─ Diameter Halved at Constant Q → h_f increases by 32 times (2^5)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Logarithmic velocity profile: $u/u_* = 2.5 \ln(y/R) + B$.
  • [ ] Velocity ratio $u_{\max} / V_{\text{avg}} \approx 1.2$.
  • [ ] Head loss $h_f \propto V^2$ (turbulent) vs $h_f \propto V$ (laminar).
  • [ ] Shear stress is maximum at wall and zero at centerline.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: In a fully developed turbulent pipe flow, if the diameter of the pipe is reduced by half while maintaining the same volume flow rate $Q$ and friction factor $f$, find the factor by which head loss increases. - Solution:

$${h_f = \frac{8 f L Q^2}{\pi^2 g D^5} \implies h_f \propto D^{-5}}$$
$${\frac{h_{f2}}{h_{f1}} = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^5 = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_1/2}\right)^5 = 2^5 = 32}$$
- Key Insight: Frictional head loss increases by a factor of 32 for constant $Q$ when diameter is halved in turbulent flow.

3.6 Boundary Layer Theory & Separation

1. Concept Pointers
  • Boundary layer is the thin fluid region adjacent to a solid wall where viscous shear forces dominate.
  • Nominal thickness $\delta$ is defined where local velocity $u = 0.99 U_\infty$.
  • Displacement thickness $\delta^$ measures mass flow deficit: $\delta^ = \int_0^\delta \left(1 - \frac{u}{U_\infty}\right) dy$.
  • Momentum thickness $\theta$ measures momentum deficit: $\theta = \int_0^\delta \frac{u}{U_\infty} \left(1 - \frac{u}{U_\infty}\right) dy$.
  • Boundary Layer Separation occurs when fluid in boundary layer is decelerated by an adverse pressure gradient $\frac{dp}{dx} > 0$, causing wall shear stress to reach zero $\tau_w = \left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0} = 0$.
💡 Key Concept: Boundary Layer Growth Laws: - Laminar Flat Plate Boundary Layer: $\delta \propto x^{1/2}$ or $\frac{\delta}{x} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{Re_x}}$. - Turbulent Flat Plate Boundary Layer: $\delta \propto x^{4/5}$ or $\frac{\delta}{x} = \frac{0.37}{Re_x^{1/5}}$. - Turbulent boundary layers grow MUCH faster than laminar boundary layers.

[!TRAP] Separation Conditions: - $\frac{dp}{dx} < 0$ (Favorable gradient) $\implies$ Flow remains attached (No separation). - $\frac{dp}{dx} = 0$ (Zero gradient, flat plate) $\implies$ Flow remains attached. - $\frac{dp}{dx} > 0$ (Adverse gradient) and $\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|{y=0} = 0 \implies$ Point of Separation! - $\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|{y=0} < 0 \implies$ Detached flow / Reverse flow / Wake.

2. Decision Tree
Boundary Layer Problem?
├─ Nominal Thickness → u = 0.99 * U_infinity
├─ Mass Deficit → Displacement Thickness delta* = integral(1 - u/U) dy
├─ Momentum Deficit → Momentum Thickness theta = integral(u/U * (1 - u/U)) dy
└─ Separation Criterion → dp/dx > 0 and du/dy|_wall = 0
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $\delta \propto x^{1/2}$ (laminar) vs $\delta \propto x^{4/5}$ (turbulent).
  • [ ] Linear profile: $\delta^* = \delta/2$, $\theta = \delta/6$.
  • [ ] Separation requires adverse pressure gradient $dp/dx > 0$.
  • [ ] At separation point: $\tau_w = 0$ and $du/dy|_{wall} = 0$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Assuming a linear velocity profile $\frac{u}{U_\infty} = \frac{y}{\delta}$ inside a boundary layer of thickness $\delta = 12 \text{ mm}$, calculate displacement thickness $\delta^$ and momentum thickness $\theta$. - Solution:

$${\delta^ = \int_0^\delta \left(1 - \frac{y}{\delta}\right) dy = \left[y - \frac{y^2}{2\delta}\right]_0^\delta = \delta - \frac{\delta}{2} = \frac{\delta}{2} = \frac{12}{2} = 6 \text{ mm}}$$
$${\theta = \int_0^\delta \frac{y}{\delta} \left(1 - \frac{y}{\delta}\right) dy = \int_0^\delta \left(\frac{y}{\delta} - \frac{y^2}{\delta^2}\right) dy = \left[\frac{y^2}{2\delta} - \frac{y^3}{3\delta^2}\right]_0^\delta = \frac{\delta}{2} - \frac{\delta}{3} = \frac{\delta}{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2 \text{ mm}}$$
- Key Insight: For linear profile, $\delta^* = \delta/2 = 6 \text{ mm}$ and $\theta = \delta/6 = 2 \text{ mm}$.

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (Core Conceptual)

Q1. Why is friction called the "major" loss in pipe flow?
A: Its magnitude is usually much larger than the sum of all local (minor) losses in long pipelines; minor losses become comparable only in short, complex systems.

Q2. What does "fully developed pipe flow" mean?
A: The velocity profile no longer changes in the stream-wise direction $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$. It can be either laminar or turbulent.

Q3. Why is $u_{\max} = 2 V_{\text{avg}}$ only for laminar flow?
A: A parabolic velocity profile integrates to a mean velocity equal to half the centerline velocity. Turbulent velocity profiles are much flatter, giving $V_{\text{avg}} \approx 0.82 u_{\max}$.

Q4. What causes cavitation in pipe flow?
A: Local absolute pressure dropping to or below the liquid's vapour pressure, causing vapour bubbles to form and violently collapse against pipe walls.

Q5. State the relation between wall shear stress and pressure drop in horizontal pipe flow.
A: Force balance on a fluid cylinder gives $\tau_w = \frac{\Delta P \cdot D}{4L}$. This relation holds for both laminar and turbulent flows.

Q6. Why does head loss scale as $D^{-4}$ in laminar pipe flow for fixed discharge $Q$?
A: From Hagen-Poiseuille $Q = \frac{\pi \Delta P D^4}{128 \mu L} \implies \Delta P \propto \frac{Q \mu L}{D^4} \implies h_f \propto D^{-4}$.

Q7. What is the maximum power transmission condition in a pipeline?
A: Differentiating power $P = \rho g Q (H - h_f)$ with respect to velocity yields optimum head loss $h_f = \frac{H}{3}$, giving maximum transmission efficiency $\eta = 66.7\%$.

Q8. What does the vertical distance between TEL and HGL represent?
A: Exactly the velocity head $\frac{V^2}{2g}$. TEL is ALWAYS above HGL.

Q9. What is the practical continuous-flow limit for a siphon elevation?
A: The summit of a siphon cannot be placed more than $\approx 7.75 \text{ m}$ above the hydraulic grade line to prevent cavitation and vapor lock.

Q10. What is the hydraulic diameter of a square duct of side $a$?
A: $D_h = \frac{4A}{P} = \frac{4 a^2}{4a} = a$.

Q11. Which forces are balanced in fully developed Hagen-Poiseuille flow?
A: Pressure force and viscous force (inertia force is zero because acceleration is zero).

Q12. What are the kinetic energy and momentum correction factors for laminar pipe flow?
A: $\alpha = 2.0$ (kinetic energy correction factor) and $\beta = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$ (momentum correction factor).

Q13. What causes boundary layer separation?
A: An adverse pressure gradient $\frac{dp}{dx} > 0$ that decelerates near-wall fluid until wall shear stress vanishes $\tau_w = 0$.

Q14. What condition marks the exact point of boundary layer separation?
A: Wall shear stress $\tau_w = 0$ (i.e., $\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0} = 0$).

Q15. How does laminar boundary layer thickness grow with distance $x$ from leading edge?
A: $\delta \propto x^{1/2}$ (or $\sqrt{x}$).

SECTION 5: CONSOLIDATION NOTES

  • Note on Darcy vs Hagen-Poiseuille Head Loss:
  • In laminar pipe flow, Darcy formula $h_f = \frac{f L V^2}{2 g D}$ with $f = \frac{64}{Re}$ yields $h_f = \frac{64 \mu}{\rho V D} \frac{L V^2}{2 g D} = \frac{32 \mu V L}{\rho g D^2}$, which is identical to the Hagen-Poiseuille head loss formula.
  • Note on Velocity Ratios Across Regimes:
  • Laminar Pipe Flow: $u_{\max}/V_{\text{avg}} = 2.0$.
  • Turbulent Pipe Flow: $u_{\max}/V_{\text{avg}} \approx 1.2$.
  • Note on Friction Loss vs Diameter Reduction:
  • For constant $Q$: Laminar $h_f \propto D^{-4}$; Turbulent $h_f \propto D^{-5}$.
