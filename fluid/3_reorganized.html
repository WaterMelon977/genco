1. Reynolds Number — Geometry-Specific Criteria

The Reynolds number expresses the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. Its critical value for transition depends on the flow geometry and is not a universal constant.

GeometryReynolds numberLaminarTurbulent
Circular pipe$Re = VD/\nu$$Re < 2000$$Re > 4000$
Flat plate (BL)$Re_x = U_\infty x/\nu$$Re_x < 5\times10^5$$Re_x > 5\times10^5$
Open channel$Re = VR_h/\nu$$Re < 500$$Re > 1000$
Parallel plates$Re = Vh/\nu$ (gap $h$)$Re < 1000$$Re > 1000$ (approx.)
  • Open channel uses hydraulic radius $R_h = A/P$ as length scale — critical $Re = 500$, not 2000
  • At transitional Reynolds numbers, flow is sensitive to disturbances; either regime is possible

2. Laminar Flow in a Circular Pipe (Hagen-Poiseuille)

Fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe results from a balance between the applied pressure gradient and viscous shear. The velocity profile is axisymmetric and parabolic.

2.1 Velocity Profile

$$u(r) = u_{\max}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$$
$$u_{\max} = 2\,V_{\text{avg}}$$
  • $r$ — radial position from centreline; $R$ — pipe radius; $u_{\max}$ occurs at $r = 0$ (centreline)
  • Average velocity = half of centreline velocity for circular pipe laminar flow

2.2 Shear Stress Distribution

$$\tau(r) = \frac{r}{2}\left(-\frac{dp}{dx}\right), \qquad \tau_w = \frac{\Delta P \cdot D}{4L}$$
  • Shear stress varies linearly with $r$: zero at centreline, maximum at wall
  • $-dp/dx$ is the favourable pressure gradient driving flow

2.3 Pressure Drop

$$\Delta P = \frac{32\,\mu\,V\,L}{D^2} = \frac{128\,\mu\,Q\,L}{\pi D^4}$$
  • Valid for: steady, fully developed, incompressible, laminar ($Re < 2300$) flow in circular pipes
  • $\Delta P \propto \mu,\, V,\, L,\, D^{-2}$ (for constant $V$); or $\propto D^{-4}$ (for constant $Q$)

3. Laminar Flow Between Fixed Parallel Plates

When flow occurs between two infinite parallel flat plates separated by a gap $h$ (both stationary), the velocity profile is again parabolic but the centreline-to-average ratio differs from the circular pipe.

3.1 Velocity Profile

$$u(y) = \frac{1}{2\mu}\left(-\frac{dp}{dx}\right)(hy - y^2)$$
$$u_{\max} = \frac{3}{2}\,V_{\text{avg}} = 1.5\,V_{\text{avg}}$$
  • $y$ — distance from one plate; maximum velocity occurs at mid-gap ($y = h/2$)
  • Centreline velocity is 1.5× average, compared to 2× average for circular pipe

3.2 Pressure Drop & Discharge

$$\Delta P = \frac{12\,\mu\,V\,L}{h^2}, \qquad Q = \frac{w\,h^3\,\Delta P}{12\,\mu\,L}$$
$$\tau_w = \frac{\Delta P \cdot h}{2L}$$
  • $w$ — width of plates perpendicular to flow; $Q \propto h^3$ (very sensitive to gap size)

4. Boundary Layer Velocity Profiles & Thickness Ratios

Approximate methods for boundary layer analysis assume a velocity profile shape and apply integral momentum methods. The accuracy depends on how well the assumed profile matches the real distribution.

  • Blasius solution uses parabolic approximation; gives exact result for steady, zero-pressure-gradient laminar BL

4.4 von Kármán Momentum Integral Equation

Relates the wall shear stress to the streamwise rate of change of momentum thickness — applicable to both laminar and turbulent boundary layers:

$$\frac{\tau_w}{\rho U_\infty^2} = \frac{d\theta}{dx}$$
  • This is the foundation of all approximate BL methods: assume a velocity profile → compute $\theta/\delta$ ratio → integrate to find $\delta(x)$
  • Combined with $C_f = \tau_w/(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2)$ gives the local skin friction coefficient

5. Couette Flow (Flow Between Moving Plates)

Couette flow occurs when the upper plate moves at velocity $U$ while the lower plate is stationary, with or without an externally applied pressure gradient.

5.1 Pure Couette Flow (No Pressure Gradient)

$$u(y) = U\,\frac{y}{h}$$
  • Linear velocity profile; shear stress $\tau = \mu U/h$ is uniform throughout
  • No pressure drop required — flow sustained entirely by moving boundary

5.2 Generalised Couette Flow (With Pressure Gradient)

$$u(y) = U\,\frac{y}{h} + \frac{1}{2\mu}\left(-\frac{dp}{dx}\right)(hy - y^2)$$
  • Superposition of simple Couette (linear) and Poiseuille (parabolic) components
  • Favourable pressure gradient ($dp/dx < 0$) adds forward flow component; adverse gradient can cause backflow near stationary wall

Key Points to Remember

  • Critical Reynolds numbers by geometry: pipe $Re_{cr} = 2000/4000$; flat plate $Re_{x,cr} = 5\times10^5$; open channel $Re_{cr} = 500/1000$. Open channel uses hydraulic radius, not diameter.
  • Circular pipe laminar: $u_{\max} = 2\,V_{\text{avg}}$ (parabolic, axisymmetric). Shear stress is zero at centreline and maximum at wall.
  • Parallel plates laminar: $u_{\max} = 1.5\,V_{\text{avg}}$. Discharge $Q \propto h^3$ — very sensitive to gap height.
  • $\Delta P$ for circular pipe laminar: $\propto D^{-2}$ at constant $V$; $\propto D^{-4}$ at constant $Q$. Halving pipe diameter increases $\Delta P$ by 16× at the same flow rate.
  • Parabolic BL profile: $\delta^*/\delta = 1/3$; $\theta/\delta = 2/15$; $H = 2.5$. Sinusoidal is slightly more accurate with similar ratios.
  • Blasius exact laminar BL: $\delta/x = 5/\sqrt{Re_x}$; $\delta^*/\delta \approx 0.344$; $H \approx 2.59$.
  • 1/7-power turbulent BL: $u/U_\infty = (y/\delta)^{1/7}$; $\delta^*/\delta = 1/8$; $\theta/\delta = 7/72$; $H \approx 1.29$.
  • Shape factor $H = \delta^*/\theta$ increases as separation approaches. Turbulent BL has lower $H$ (more uniform); laminar has higher $H$ (more peaked profile).
  • Von Kármán equation: $\tau_w/(\rho U_\infty^2) = d\theta/dx$. Foundation of all approximate BL analysis methods.
  • Pure Couette flow has a linear velocity profile sustained by the moving plate, not by a pressure gradient.
  • Turbulent BL grows faster than laminar ($\delta \propto x^{4/5}$ vs $x^{1/2}$) and has higher wall shear stress at the same streamwise position.
  • Laminar pipe friction $f = 64/Re$ is independent of wall roughness. Turbulent friction depends on both $Re$ and $\varepsilon/D$ via the Moody diagram.
