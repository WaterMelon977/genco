1. Compressible Flow & Mach Number

When a fluid's density changes significantly with pressure — typically gases at high speeds — the flow is compressible. The Mach number $M$ is the key parameter governing compressible flow behaviour.

$$M = \frac{V}{c}, \qquad c = \sqrt{\gamma R T} = \sqrt{\frac{K}{\rho}}$$
  • $V$ — flow velocity (m/s); $c$ — local speed of sound (m/s)
  • $\gamma = C_p/C_v$ — ratio of specific heats (≈ 1.4 for air, diatomic gases)
  • $R$ — specific gas constant (287 J/kg·K for air); $T$ — absolute temperature (K)
Flow regimeMach numberBehaviour
Incompressible$M < 0.3$Density change < 5%; Bernoulli applicable
Subsonic$0.3 \le M < 1$Compressibility matters; no shock waves
Transonic$M \approx 1$Mixed sub/supersonic regions
Sonic$M = 1$Flow velocity equals speed of sound
Supersonic$1 < M < 5$Shock waves possible; pressure signals can't propagate upstream
Hypersonic$M > 5$Extreme compressibility and heating effects

1.1 Area-Velocity Relation (Variable Area Ducts)

For isentropic, 1D, steady compressible flow in a variable-area duct, differentiating the continuity equation $\rho AV = \text{const}$ gives:

$$\frac{dA}{A} = (M^2 - 1)\frac{dV}{V}$$
Duct geometryMach regimeVelocity change
Converging ($dA < 0$)Subsonic ($M < 1$)Accelerates ($dV > 0$)
Diverging ($dA > 0$)Subsonic ($M < 1$)Decelerates ($dV < 0$)
Converging ($dA < 0$)Supersonic ($M > 1$)Decelerates ($dV < 0$)
Diverging ($dA > 0$)Supersonic ($M > 1$)Accelerates ($dV > 0$)
Throat ($dA = 0$)Sonic ($M = 1$)Minimum area; transition point

To accelerate flow from subsonic to supersonic, a converging-diverging (de Laval) nozzle is required — flow accelerates in the converging section to $M = 1$ at the throat, then continues to accelerate in the diverging section.

1.2 Compressible Bernoulli Equations

Adiabatic (isentropic) process:

$$\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz = \text{constant}$$

Isothermal process (constant temperature):

$$\frac{P}{\rho}\ln P + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz = \text{constant}$$

1.3 Normal Shock Waves

A normal shock wave is an extremely thin, irreversible discontinuity that forms in supersonic flow. Across the shock, supersonic flow ($M_1 > 1$) abruptly becomes subsonic ($M_2 < 1$). The following changes occur:

  • Static pressure, temperature, and density all increase sharply
  • Stagnation pressure decreases (irreversible process; entropy increases)
  • Stagnation temperature remains constant (adiabatic)
  • Velocity decreases; flow becomes subsonic downstream

1.4 Stagnation Properties

Stagnation (total) state is reached when flow is brought to rest isentropically. Stagnation temperature $T_0$ and pressure $P_0$ are constant in isentropic flow:

$$T_0 = T + \frac{V^2}{2C_p} = T\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)$$
$$\frac{P_0}{P} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$$

2. External Flow — Drag, Lift & Stokes Law

External flow refers to flow over immersed bodies. The key forces are drag (in flow direction) and lift (perpendicular to flow).

2.1 Drag & Lift Forces

$$F_D = C_D \cdot \frac{1}{2}\rho A V^2, \qquad F_L = C_L \cdot \frac{1}{2}\rho A V^2$$
  • $A$ — projected frontal area (m²) for bluff bodies; planform area for airfoils
  • $C_D$, $C_L$ — drag and lift coefficients (dimensionless; depend on shape, $Re$, angle of attack)

Total drag = Friction drag + Pressure (form) drag

  • Streamlined bodies (airfoil, torpedo): friction drag dominates; boundary layer stays attached
  • Bluff bodies (sphere, cylinder, flat plate normal to flow): pressure drag dominates; large separated wake

2.2 Stokes Law (Creeping Flow over a Sphere)

For very slow flow over a sphere ($Re < 0.2$ strictly; $Re < 1$ approximately), inertia is negligible and Stokes's law gives:

$$F_D = 3\pi\mu d V = 6\pi\mu r V$$
$$C_D = \frac{24}{Re} \qquad (Re < 0.2)$$
  • $d$ — sphere diameter; $r$ — sphere radius; $V$ — free-stream velocity
  • Friction drag = 2/3 of total; pressure drag = 1/3 of total
  • Terminal velocity of a falling sphere: $V_t = \frac{(\rho_s - \rho_f)gd^2}{18\mu}$ (from $F_D = W - F_B$)

3. Dimensional Analysis & Model Testing

3.1 Buckingham Pi Theorem

If a physical phenomenon involves $n$ variables and $m$ fundamental dimensions ($M$, $L$, $T$ — giving $m = 3$), the relationship can be expressed using $(n - m)$ independent dimensionless $\Pi$-groups.

$$\text{Number of }\Pi\text{-groups} = n - m$$
  • Choose $m$ repeating variables (must together contain all $m$ dimensions)
  • Combine each remaining variable with the repeating set to form a $\Pi$-group
  • Express the physical law as $f(\Pi_1, \Pi_2, \ldots) = 0$

3.2 Important Dimensionless Numbers

NumberFormulaForce ratioGoverns

3.3 Model Similarity Laws

For a model to reproduce prototype behaviour, the governing dimensionless number must be equal in model and prototype (dynamic similarity). Complete similarity requires geometric, kinematic, and dynamic similarity simultaneously — often impossible in practice, so the most important force is matched.

Similarity lawConditionApplied when
Reynolds similarity$Re_m = Re_p$Viscous forces dominate: pipe flow, submerged submarines, wind tunnels
Froude similarity$Fr_m = Fr_p$Gravity forces dominate: ships, spillways, open channel hydraulics
Mach similarity$M_m = M_p$Compressibility dominates: supersonic aircraft, missiles
Weber similarity$We_m = We_p$Surface tension dominates: spray nozzles, droplet formation

3.4 Froude Scale Ratios

For hydraulic models operating under Froude similarity (gravity forces dominant, e.g. spillways, rivers, ships), if the length scale ratio is $L_r = L_p/L_m$:

Key Points to Remember

  • $M = V/c$; $c = \sqrt{\gamma RT}$. For air: $\gamma = 1.4$, $R = 287$ J/kg·K. Flow is incompressible when $M < 0.3$ (density change < 5%).
  • Area-velocity relation: $(dA/A) = (M^2-1)(dV/V)$. Subsonic: converging duct accelerates; diverging decelerates. Supersonic: opposite — diverging duct accelerates.
  • To achieve supersonic flow from rest requires a converging-diverging (de Laval) nozzle. Throat area is where $M = 1$ (sonic).
  • Normal shock: supersonic ($M_1 > 1$) → subsonic ($M_2 < 1$). Pressure, temperature, density all jump. Stagnation pressure drops (irreversible). Stagnation temperature is unchanged (adiabatic).
  • Stokes law for sphere: $F_D = 3\pi\mu dV$; valid for $Re < 0.2$. Friction drag = 2/3 total; pressure drag = 1/3 total. $C_D = 24/Re$.
  • Drag and lift: $F = C \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A V^2$. $C_D$ depends on body shape and $Re$. Streamlined body: friction drag dominates. Bluff body: form (pressure) drag dominates.
  • Buckingham Pi theorem: $(n - m)$ dimensionless groups from $n$ variables and $m$ fundamental dimensions. $m = 3$ for $M$, $L$, $T$ systems.
  • Reynolds similarity: viscous forces match ($Re_m = Re_p$). Used for submerged models, pipe flow, low-speed wind tunnel tests.
  • Froude similarity: gravity forces match ($Fr_m = Fr_p$). Used for ships, spillways, open channels. Velocity scales as $\sqrt{L_r}$; discharge as $L_r^{5/2}$.
  • Mach similarity: compressibility matches ($M_m = M_p$). Used for supersonic wind tunnels and high-speed aerodynamics.
  • Reynolds and Froude similarity cannot both be satisfied simultaneously using the same fluid — a fundamental conflict in hydraulic model testing.
  • Drag coefficient of sphere drops sharply at $Re \approx 5\times10^5$ (transition to turbulent BL reduces separation-induced pressure drag — the "drag crisis").
