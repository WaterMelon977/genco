1. Basic Fluid Properties

A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously under any applied shear stress, regardless of how small. Unlike solids, fluids cannot sustain a static shear stress. Both liquids and gases are fluids; liquids are nearly incompressible while gases are readily compressible.

1.1 Viscosity

Viscosity is the internal resistance of a fluid to shear deformation. Newton's Law of Viscosity states that the shear stress is proportional to the velocity gradient normal to the flow direction.

$$\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$$
  • $\tau$ — shear stress (Pa or N/m²)
  • $\mu$ — dynamic (absolute) viscosity (Pa·s or N·s/m²)
  • $du/dy$ — velocity gradient (s⁻¹); rate of angular deformation
  • Kinematic viscosity: $\nu = \mu/\rho$ (m²/s)
  • Unit conversions: 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s; 1 stoke = 10⁻⁴ m²/s = 1 cm²/s

Effect of temperature on viscosity: For liquids, viscosity decreases with increasing temperature (cohesive forces weaken). For gases, viscosity increases with temperature (momentum transfer between faster-moving molecules increases: $\mu \propto \sqrt{T}$).

1.2 Surface Tension & Capillarity

Surface tension $\sigma$ (N/m) arises from unbalanced intermolecular forces at a liquid–gas interface. Capillarity occurs in small tubes due to the competition between adhesive (liquid–solid) and cohesive (liquid–liquid) forces.

$$h = \frac{4\sigma\cos\theta}{\rho g d} = \frac{2\sigma\cos\theta}{\rho g R}$$
  • $h$ — capillary rise (+) or depression (−) in metres
  • $\sigma$ — surface tension (N/m)
  • $\theta$ — contact angle: ≈0° for water–glass (wetting, rise); ≈130°–140° for mercury–glass (non-wetting, depression)
  • $d$ — tube diameter (m); valid for small $d$ where gravity–surface tension balance holds

1.3 Bulk Modulus & Compressibility

$$K = -\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V/V} = \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta\rho/\rho}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{K}$$
  • $K$ — bulk modulus of elasticity (Pa); resistance to volumetric compression
  • $\beta$ — compressibility (Pa⁻¹)
  • Speed of sound: $c = \sqrt{K/\rho} = \sqrt{\gamma RT}$ (m/s); valid for ideal gas with ratio of specific heats $\gamma$
  • Standard values: $K_\text{water} \approx 2.1 \times 10^9$ Pa; $K_\text{air}$ varies with pressure

1.4 Other Fluid Properties

  • Specific weight: $\gamma = \rho g$ (N/m³); $\gamma_\text{water} = 9810$ N/m³
  • Specific gravity: $S = \rho_\text{fluid}/\rho_\text{water}$; dimensionless; $S_\text{Hg} = 13.6$
  • Standard atmospheric pressure: 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg = 10.33 m H₂O

2. Fluid Statics

In a static fluid, there is no relative motion between fluid layers, so shear stress is zero everywhere regardless of viscosity. Pressure is the only stress acting, and it acts equally in all directions (Pascal's Law).

2.1 Pressure Measurement

$$P_\text{abs} = P_\text{atm} + P_\text{gauge}, \qquad P_\text{vac} = P_\text{atm} - P_\text{abs}$$
$$p = \rho g h = \gamma h$$
  • $p$ — hydrostatic pressure at vertical depth $h$ (Pa)
  • Pressure increases linearly with depth; depends only on vertical depth, not on container shape (hydrostatic paradox)
  • Barometer: mercury preferred for its high density ($S = 13.6$) and negligible vapour pressure

Differential Manometers

For a U-tube manometer with gauge fluid of specific gravity $S_m$ and flowing fluid $S_f$, the differential head in metres of the flowing fluid is:

$$h = x\left(\frac{S_m}{S_f} - 1\right) \quad \text{(heavier gauge fluid)}$$
$$h = x\left(1 - \frac{S_m}{S_f}\right) \quad \text{(lighter gauge fluid)}$$
  • $x$ — manometer fluid deflection (m)
  • For mercury manometer with water: $h = x(13.6 - 1) = 12.6\,x$

2.2 Hydrostatic Forces on Submerged Surfaces

Plane Surface

$$F = \rho g \bar{h} A$$
  • $F$ — total hydrostatic force (N)
  • $\bar{h}$ — vertical depth of the area centroid below free surface (m)
  • $A$ — area of the submerged plane surface (m²)

The total force acts at the centre of pressure $h^*$, which always lies below the centroid:

$$h^* = \bar{h} + \frac{I_G \sin^2\theta}{A\bar{h}}$$
  • $I_G$ — second moment of area of the surface about its own centroidal axis (m⁴)
  • $\theta$ — angle of inclined surface with the free surface; for vertical surface $\theta = 90°$, so $\sin^2\theta = 1$
  • As $\bar{h} \to \infty$ (deep submergence), $h^* \to \bar{h}$ (centre of pressure approaches centroid)

Curved Surface

$$F = \sqrt{F_H^2 + F_V^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{F_V}{F_H}$$
  • $F_H$ — horizontal component = force on the vertical projection of the curved surface
  • $F_V$ — vertical component = weight of real or imaginary fluid column above the curved surface up to free surface

3. Buoyancy & Flotation Stability

Archimedes' Principle: any body fully or partially submerged in a static fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced, acting through the centre of buoyancy $B$ (centroid of displaced volume).

$$F_B = \rho_\text{fluid}\, V_\text{displaced}\, g$$
$$W_\text{apparent} = W_\text{true} - F_B$$

3.1 Floating Body Equilibrium

A floating body displaces fluid equal to its own weight: $W = F_B$. As a consequence, the fraction of volume submerged equals the ratio of the body's density to the fluid's density:

$$\frac{V_\text{submerged}}{V_\text{total}} = \frac{\rho_\text{body}}{\rho_\text{fluid}} = S_\text{body} \quad \text{(when floating in water)}$$

3.2 Stability of Floating Bodies

When a floating body is tilted slightly, the line of action of the buoyant force intersects the original vertical axis at a point called the metacentre $M$. Stability depends on the metacentric height $GM$:

$$GM = BM - BG, \qquad BM = \frac{I_\text{waterplane}}{V_\text{displaced}}$$
  • $I_\text{waterplane}$ — second moment of the waterplane area about the axis of tilt (m⁴)
  • $BG$ — distance from centre of buoyancy $B$ to centre of gravity $G$ (m)
  • $GM > 0$ (M above G) → stable; $GM = 0$ → neutral; $GM < 0$ → unstable
Body typeStability criterion
Completely submergedStable if $B$ is above $G$
Floating (partially submerged)Stable if metacentre $M$ is above $G$ ($GM > 0$), even if $B$ is below $G$

4. Fluid Kinematics

Kinematics describes fluid motion without reference to the forces causing it. The Eulerian approach describes velocity as a function of position and time: $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{V}(x,y,z,t)$.

4.1 Flow Classifications

ClassificationCriterion
Steady / Unsteady$\partial\mathbf{V}/\partial t = 0$ (steady); $\neq 0$ (unsteady)
Uniform / Non-uniform$\partial\mathbf{V}/\partial s = 0$ along streamline (uniform)
Laminar / Turbulent$Re < 2300$ laminar; $Re > 4000$ turbulent (pipe flow)
Rotational / IrrotationalFluid elements rotate about own axes (rotational) vs no rotation (irrotational)

In steady flow, streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines all coincide.

4.2 Continuity Equation

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0 \quad \text{(3D incompressible)}$$
$$A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 = Q \quad \text{(1D steady, incompressible)}$$

4.3 Stream Function & Velocity Potential

The stream function $\psi$ exists for any 2D incompressible flow. Lines of constant $\psi$ are streamlines, and the flow rate between two streamlines equals $\Delta\psi$:

$$u = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}$$

The velocity potential $\phi$ exists only for irrotational flow. Lines of constant $\phi$ (equipotential lines) are everywhere orthogonal to streamlines:

$$u = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}$$
$$\nabla^2\phi = \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0 \quad \text{(Laplace equation; irrotational flow)}$$

4.4 Acceleration & Vorticity

Total fluid acceleration has two components — local (due to time variation) and convective (due to spatial variation of velocity):

$$a_x = \underbrace{\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}}_{\text{local}} + \underbrace{u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}}_{\text{convective}}$$

Vorticity is twice the angular velocity of a fluid element:

$$\xi_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 2\omega_z$$
  • Irrotational flow: $\xi = 0$ everywhere; velocity potential $\phi$ exists
  • Free vortex (irrotational): $v_\theta \cdot r = \text{const}$, so $v_\theta \propto 1/r$
  • Forced vortex (rotational): $v_\theta = \omega r$, so $v_\theta \propto r$

5. Fluid Dynamics

5.1 Euler's Equation

For steady, inviscid flow along a streamline (pressure and gravity forces only):

$$\frac{dp}{\rho} + V\,dV + g\,dz = 0$$

Integrating along a streamline for an incompressible fluid ($\rho$ = constant) gives Bernoulli's equation.

5.2 Bernoulli's Equation

$$\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z = \text{constant along a streamline}$$
  • $p/\rho g$ — pressure head (m)
  • $V^2/2g$ — velocity (kinetic) head (m)
  • $z$ — datum (potential) head (m)
  • Assumptions: steady flow; incompressible fluid; inviscid fluid; applied along a single streamline; no shaft work
  • With losses and machinery: $\frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_L \pm H_\text{machine}$

The Total Energy Line (TEL) and Hydraulic Gradient Line (HGL) are related by the velocity head:

$$\text{TEL} - \text{HGL} = \frac{V^2}{2g} \quad \text{(TEL always above HGL)}$$

5.3 Flow Measurement Devices

Venturimeter

$$Q = C_d \frac{A_1 A_2}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}}\sqrt{2gh}$$
  • $C_d$ = 0.95–0.99; least head loss among flow meters
  • Convergent angle: 20°–22°; Divergent angle: 5°–7° (small to prevent boundary layer separation)
  • Differential head: $h = x(S_m/S_f - 1)$ metres of flowing fluid

Orifice Meter

$$Q = C_d A_o \sqrt{2gH}, \qquad C_d = C_c \times C_v \approx 0.62\text{–}0.65$$
  • Highest pressure loss; simplest construction; $C_c$ = contraction coefficient, $C_v$ = velocity coefficient

Pitot Tube

$$V = C_v\sqrt{2gh}$$
  • Measures local velocity at a point by creating a stagnation point; $h$ = difference between stagnation and static pressure heads
  • $C_v \approx 0.98\text{–}0.99$; stagnation point: $V = 0$, all kinetic energy converts to pressure

5.4 Impulse–Momentum Equation

$$\sum\mathbf{F} = \dot{m}(\mathbf{V}_\text{out} - \mathbf{V}_\text{in}) = \rho Q(\mathbf{V}_\text{out} - \mathbf{V}_\text{in})$$
  • Applied to control volumes; forces include pressure, body, and reaction forces
  • Kinetic energy correction factor $\alpha = 2.0$ (laminar pipe); momentum correction factor $\beta = 4/3 \approx 1.33$ (laminar pipe)
  • For turbulent flow: $\alpha \approx 1.01\text{–}1.1$; $\beta \approx 1.01\text{–}1.05$

Key Points to Remember

  • Fluid viscosity: liquid $\mu$ decreases with $T$; gas $\mu$ increases with $T$ ($\mu \propto \sqrt{T}$). Unit conversions: 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s; 1 stoke = 10⁻⁴ m²/s.
  • Excess pressure: liquid drop = $4\sigma/d$; soap bubble = $8\sigma/d$ (two interfaces); liquid jet = $2\sigma/d$.
  • Hydrostatic force on a plane surface acts at the centroid: $F = \rho g \bar{h} A$. Centre of pressure $h^*$ always lies below the centroid $\bar{h}$.
  • Pascal's Law: pressure at any point in a static fluid is equal in all directions. Hydrostatic pressure depends only on depth, not container shape (hydrostatic paradox).
  • Archimedes: buoyant force = weight of displaced fluid, acting through centre of buoyancy $B$ (centroid of displaced volume).
  • Floating body: fraction submerged = specific gravity. Stable if $GM > 0$ (metacentre above centre of gravity).
  • For completely submerged body: stable if $B$ is above $G$; floating body can be stable even if $B$ below $G$ provided $M$ is above $G$.
  • Stream function $\psi$ exists for any 2D incompressible flow. Velocity potential $\phi$ exists only for irrotational flow.
  • Free vortex: $v_\theta r = $ const (irrotational); forced vortex: $v_\theta = \omega r$ (rotational — like a rigid body).
  • Streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines coincide only in steady flow.
  • Bernoulli requires: steady, incompressible, inviscid, along a streamline, no shaft work. TEL always lies above HGL by exactly $V^2/2g$.
  • Venturimeter: least head loss ($C_d \approx 0.98$); orifice meter: highest head loss ($C_d \approx 0.62$); divergent cone angle 5°–7° to prevent separation.
  • Kinetic energy correction factor $\alpha = 2$ (laminar); momentum correction factor $\beta = 4/3$ (laminar).
  • Vorticity $\xi = 2\omega$; irrotational flow has $\xi = 0$ everywhere and satisfies $\nabla^2\phi = 0$ (Laplace equation).
