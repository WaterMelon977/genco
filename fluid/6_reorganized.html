1. Pump Classification

Pumps convert mechanical energy into hydraulic energy to move fluid against a pressure difference. They fall into two broad categories based on their operating principle.

2. Reciprocating Pumps

A reciprocating pump uses a piston moving back and forth in a cylinder. Suction and delivery valves (non-return valves) control flow direction. Flow is inherently pulsating — an air vessel is used to smooth the discharge.

2.1 Theoretical Discharge

$$Q_{th} = \frac{ALN}{60} \quad \text{(single-acting)}$$
$$Q_{th} = \frac{2ALN}{60} \quad \text{(double-acting)}$$
  • $A$ — piston cross-sectional area (m²); $L$ — stroke length (m); $N$ — speed (rpm)
  • Double-acting pump displaces fluid on both forward and return strokes
  • For double-acting with piston rod on one side: $Q_{th} = \frac{(2A - a)LN}{60}$ where $a$ = rod cross-section area

2.2 Slip & Coefficient of Discharge

$$\%\,\text{Slip} = \frac{Q_{th} - Q_{act}}{Q_{th}} \times 100 = (1 - C_d) \times 100$$
  • $C_d = Q_{act}/Q_{th}$ — coefficient of discharge (typically 0.97–0.99 for well-maintained pumps)
  • Negative slip: $Q_{act} > Q_{th}$ — occurs when delivery pipe is short and inertia effects are large, keeping delivery valve open during part of the suction stroke

2.3 Power & Head

$$P = \frac{\rho\,g\,Q_{th}\,(h_s + h_d)}{1000} \quad \text{(kW, theoretical)}$$
  • $h_s$ — suction lift (m); $h_d$ — delivery head (m); total = static head the pump must overcome
  • Actual power > theoretical due to friction and mechanical losses

2.4 Indicator Diagram & Air Vessel

The indicator diagram plots cylinder pressure against piston stroke. Ideally it is a rectangle; in practice, inertia and friction modify its shape. The air vessel — a sealed chamber of compressed air connected near the pump — absorbs flow fluctuations, reduces friction work, and prevents cavitation in the suction line.

  • Without air vessel: velocity in suction/delivery pipes varies sinusoidally each stroke
  • With air vessel close to pump: velocity in long pipe beyond the vessel becomes nearly constant = $Q_{avg}/A_{pipe}$
  • Air vessel on suction side prevents separation when suction pressure approaches vapour pressure

3. Centrifugal Pumps

The centrifugal pump is the most widely used pump in industry. An impeller rotating inside a casing imparts kinetic energy to the fluid; the volute casing or diffuser converts this kinetic energy into pressure.

3.1 Working Principle & Priming

The impeller creates a low-pressure zone at its eye (inlet), drawing liquid in, and a high-pressure zone at its periphery. Because a centrifugal pump cannot generate suction on its own if air is present, it must be primed (filled with liquid) before starting. Self-priming pumps have special designs to handle this automatically.

3.2 Manometric Head

$$H_m = h_s + h_d + h_{fs} + h_{fd} + \frac{V_d^2}{2g}$$
  • $h_s, h_d$ — static suction lift and static delivery head (m)
  • $h_{fs}, h_{fd}$ — friction head losses in suction and delivery pipes (m)
  • $V_d^2/2g$ — velocity head at delivery pipe exit (m); sometimes negligible

3.3 Euler's Equation Applied to Impeller

The theoretical work done per unit weight of fluid by the impeller (Euler's head) is:

$$H_{Euler} = \frac{V_{w2}u_2 - V_{w1}u_1}{g}$$
  • $V_{w1}, V_{w2}$ — whirl (tangential) components of absolute velocity at inlet and outlet (m/s)
  • $u_1, u_2$ — peripheral (blade tip) velocity at inlet and outlet (m/s)
  • For radial entry ($V_{w1} = 0$): $H_{Euler} = V_{w2}u_2/g$

3.4 Efficiency & Output

$$\eta_{man} = \frac{g\,H_m}{V_{w2}\,u_2} \qquad \text{(manometric efficiency)}$$
$$Q = \pi D_1 b_1 V_{f1} = \pi D_2 b_2 V_{f2} \qquad \text{(discharge; continuity)}$$
$$\eta_o = \frac{\rho\,g\,Q\,H_m}{1000\,P_{\text{shaft}}} \qquad \text{(overall efficiency)}$$
  • $b_1, b_2$ — impeller width at inlet/outlet (m); $V_{f1}, V_{f2}$ — radial (flow) velocity (m/s)
  • Overall efficiency $\eta_o = \eta_{man} \times \eta_{mech} \times \eta_{vol}$

3.5 Minimum Starting Speed

A centrifugal pump will not deliver flow against the manometric head unless it runs at or above a minimum speed $N_{min}$, at which the centrifugal head generated equals the manometric head:

$$N_{min} = \frac{60}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g\,H_m}{r_2^2 - r_1^2}} \qquad \text{(approximate)}$$

4. Specific Speed, Affinity Laws & NPSH

4.1 Pump Specific Speed

$$N_s = \frac{N\sqrt{Q}}{H_m^{3/4}}$$
  • $N$ — speed (rpm); $Q$ — discharge (m³/s or L/s depending on convention); $H_m$ — head per stage (m)
  • Low $N_s$ → radial flow (centrifugal); high $N_s$ → axial flow. Typical centrifugal: $N_s = 10$–70; mixed flow: 70–160; axial: 160–500
  • Note: Turbine specific speed uses $P$ in numerator ($N\sqrt{P}/H^{5/4}$); pump uses $Q$ ($N\sqrt{Q}/H^{3/4}$)

4.2 Affinity Laws (Similarity Laws for Pumps)

For a given pump at constant impeller diameter, changing rotational speed $N$:

$$\frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{N_1}{N_2}, \qquad \frac{H_1}{H_2} = \left(\frac{N_1}{N_2}\right)^2, \qquad \frac{P_1}{P_2} = \left(\frac{N_1}{N_2}\right)^3$$

For geometrically similar pumps at constant speed, changing impeller diameter $D$:

$$\frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^3, \qquad \frac{H_1}{H_2} = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2, \qquad \frac{P_1}{P_2} = \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^5$$
  • Doubling speed: $Q$ doubles, $H$ quadruples, $P$ increases 8×
  • Affinity laws assume efficiency remains constant (valid for small changes)

4.3 NPSH & Cavitation in Pumps

Cavitation in centrifugal pumps occurs at the impeller eye where pressure is lowest. The Net Positive Suction Head Available (NPSHA) must exceed the pump's required NPSH (NPSHR) to prevent cavitation.

$$\text{NPSH}_A = \frac{P_{atm}}{\rho g} - \frac{P_v}{\rho g} - h_s - h_{fs}$$
  • $P_{atm}$ — atmospheric pressure (Pa); $P_v$ — vapour pressure at operating temperature (Pa)
  • $h_s$ — suction lift; $h_{fs}$ — friction loss in suction pipe
  • To avoid cavitation: $\text{NPSH}_A \ge \text{NPSH}_R$ (required, from pump curve)
  • Reducing $h_s$ (install pump lower, closer to sump) and minimising $h_{fs}$ (short, large-diameter suction pipe) improves NPSH margin

5. Hydraulic Ram

A hydraulic ram is a water-powered pump that uses the inertia (water hammer) of a falling supply to raise a smaller fraction of water to a much greater height — with no external power source. It has only two moving parts: the waste valve and the delivery valve.

5.1 Operating Principle

  1. Supply water flows down from reservoir through drive pipe, building velocity
  2. Waste valve snaps shut; pressure surge (water hammer) forces delivery valve open
  3. Small quantity of water is pumped into air vessel and up the delivery pipe
  4. Delivery valve closes; waste valve reopens; cycle repeats (50–100 times per minute)

5.2 Efficiency

$$\eta_\text{D'Aubuisson} = \frac{q\,h}{Q\,H}$$
$$\eta_\text{Rankine} = \frac{q\,h}{(Q-q)\,H}$$
  • $Q$ — total supply flow rate (m³/s); $q$ — delivery (pumped) flow rate (m³/s)
  • $H$ — supply head (m); $h$ — total delivery height above supply reservoir (m)
  • D'Aubuisson's efficiency is based on total supply; Rankine's on wasted supply $(Q-q)$
  • Typical efficiency: 60–80% depending on head ratio $h/H$

6. Hydraulic Accumulator

A hydraulic accumulator stores energy in the form of pressurised fluid and releases it rapidly when demand exceeds pump capacity. It acts as an energy buffer, allowing a smaller pump to handle intermittent peak loads.

  • Consists of a vertical cylinder containing a heavy ram (piston) loaded with dead weights
  • Fluid pumped in raises the ram; ram descends under its own weight to deliver fluid at constant pressure
  • Energy stored: $E = P \cdot V_\text{displacement}$ (N·m) where $P = W/A$ (weight on ram / ram area)
  • Applications: hydraulic presses, forging machines, dock gates, cranes
$$P = \frac{W}{A} \qquad \text{(pressure maintained by accumulator)}$$
$$E = P \cdot A \cdot h = W \cdot h \qquad \text{(energy stored; }h\text{ = ram stroke)}$$

7. Hydraulic Intensifier

A hydraulic intensifier (pressure booster) increases the pressure of a fluid beyond what the supply pump can provide, using differential piston areas. It receives a large volume of low-pressure fluid and outputs a small volume at high pressure.

$$p_2 A_2 = p_1 A_1 \implies p_2 = p_1\left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2$$
  • $p_1, A_1$ — low-pressure supply and large piston area; $p_2, A_2$ — high-pressure output and small piston area
  • Pressure multiplication factor = $A_1/A_2 = (D_1/D_2)^2$
  • Flow rate is reduced by same ratio: $Q_2 = Q_1 \cdot A_2/A_1$ (power is conserved, minus losses)
  • Applications: hydraulic presses, punching machines, riveting machines

Key Points to Remember

  • Reciprocating pump: single-acting $Q_{th} = ALN/60$; double-acting $Q_{th} = 2ALN/60$. Positive displacement — actual discharge close to theoretical.
  • Slip = $(Q_{th} - Q_{act})/Q_{th} \times 100$. Negative slip ($Q_{act} > Q_{th}$) occurs with short delivery pipes due to inertia keeping delivery valve open.
  • Air vessel smooths pulsating flow of reciprocating pump; placed as close to pump as possible. Reduces friction work and prevents separation on suction side.
  • Centrifugal pump requires priming (filling with liquid before starting) — cannot self-prime if air is present in the casing.
  • Manometric head: $H_m = h_s + h_d + h_{fs} + h_{fd} + V_d^2/2g$. This is the total head the pump must develop.
  • Manometric efficiency: $\eta_{man} = gH_m/(V_{w2}u_2)$. For radial entry: $V_{w1} = 0$, so Euler head $= V_{w2}u_2/g$.
  • Impeller discharge (continuity): $Q = \pi D_1 b_1 V_{f1} = \pi D_2 b_2 V_{f2}$.
  • Pump specific speed: $N_s = N\sqrt{Q}/H_m^{3/4}$. Different from turbine specific speed ($N\sqrt{P}/H^{5/4}$).
  • Affinity laws (speed change): $Q \propto N$, $H \propto N^2$, $P \propto N^3$. Doubling speed: double $Q$, quadruple $H$, 8× power.
  • Affinity laws (diameter change): $Q \propto D^3$, $H \propto D^2$, $P \propto D^5$.
  • NPSH$_A$ must exceed NPSH$_R$ to avoid cavitation. Increase NPSH$_A$ by reducing suction lift $h_s$ or minimising suction pipe friction $h_{fs}$.
  • Series pumps: heads add, same flow. Parallel pumps: flows add, same head. Series for high head; parallel for high flow.
  • Hydraulic ram: no external power; uses water hammer to lift a small $q$ to height $h$ using large $Q$ falling through $H$. D'Aubuisson efficiency = $qh/(QH)$.
  • Hydraulic accumulator: stores energy at constant pressure $p = W/A$ using a weighted ram. Energy stored = $W \times$ stroke length.
  • Hydraulic intensifier: boosts pressure using differential piston areas. $p_2 = p_1(A_1/A_2)$. Flow rate reduced proportionally — power conserved.
