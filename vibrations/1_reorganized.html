SECTION 2: QUICK REFERENCE SHEET

2.1 — All Formulas & Quantitative Relations (with symbols & units)

1. Undamped Free Vibrations

  • Natural Angular Frequency $\omega_n$:
    $${\boxed{\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \quad (\text{rad/s})}}$$
  • $k$: spring stiffness / equivalent stiffness $\text{N/m}$

  • $m$: mass $\text{kg}$

  • Natural Cyclic Frequency $f_n$:

    $${\boxed{f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \quad (\text{Hz})}}$$

  • $g$: acceleration due to gravity $9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$
  • $\delta$: static deflection under load ($= \frac{mg}{k}$, $\text{m}$)

  • Practical formula (with $\delta$ in cm): $\boxed{f_n \approx \frac{4.987}{\sqrt{\delta}} \quad (\text{Hz})}$

  • Vertical Bar Longitudinal Stiffness $k$ & Frequency:

    $${\boxed{k = \frac{A \cdot E}{L} \implies f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{A \cdot E}{m \cdot L}} \quad (\text{Hz})}}$$

  • $A$: bar cross-sectional area $\text{m}^2$
  • $E$: Young's modulus $\text{N/m}^2$
  • $L$: bar length $\text{m}$

2. Damped Free Vibrations

  • Critical Damping Coefficient $c_c$:

    $${\boxed{c_c = 2\sqrt{k \cdot m} = 2 \cdot m \cdot \omega_n \quad (\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m})}}$$

  • Damping Ratio $\xi$:

    $${\boxed{\xi = \frac{c}{c_c} = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{k \cdot m}}}}$$

  • $c$: viscous damping coefficient $\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m}$
  • $\xi < 1.0 \implies$ Underdamped (oscillatory decay)
  • $\xi = 1.0 \implies$ Critically damped (fastest return without oscillation)

  • $\xi > 1.0 \implies$ Overdamped (slow aperiodic return)

  • Damped Natural Frequency $\omega_d$:

    $${\boxed{\omega_d = \omega_n \cdot \sqrt{1 - \xi^2} \quad (\text{rad/s})}}$$

  • Logarithmic Decrement $\delta$:

    $${\boxed{\delta = \ln\left(\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}}\right) = \frac{2\pi \cdot \xi}{\sqrt{1 - \xi^2}}}}$$

  • $x_n, x_{n+1}$: successive peak amplitudes on the same side
  • For small damping $\xi < 0.2$: $\boxed{\delta \approx 2\pi \cdot \xi}$
  • Over $N$ cycles: $\boxed{\delta = \frac{1}{N} \cdot \ln\left(\frac{x_0}{x_N}\right)}$
  • Successive Amplitude Ratio: $\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}} = e^{\delta}$ (forms a geometric progression)

3. Forced Vibrations & Resonance

  • Magnification Factor $M$:
    $${\boxed{M = \frac{A}{\delta_{\text{st}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2 \cdot \xi \cdot r)^2}}}}$$
  • $A$: steady-state amplitude $\text{m}$
  • $\delta_{\text{st}}$: static deflection under dynamic force amplitude ($= \frac{F_0}{k}$, $\text{m}$)
  • $r$: frequency ratio $= \frac{\omega}{\omega_n}$

  • $\omega$: external forcing frequency $\text{rad/s}$

  • Resonance $r = 1.0$ Attributes:

  • Phase lag: exactly $\phi = 90^\circ$ (independent of $\xi$ for viscous damping)
  • Maximum dynamic amplitude (approx): $\boxed{M_{\text{max}} \approx \frac{1}{2\xi}}$
  • Dynamic force balance: Damping force equals impressed force $c \omega A = F_0$.

4. Vibration Isolation & Transmissibility

  • Transmissibility Ratio $TR$:
    $${\boxed{TR = \frac{F_T}{F_0} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2 \cdot \xi \cdot r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2 \cdot \xi \cdot r)^2}}}}$$
  • $F_T$: force transmitted to the foundation $\text{N}$
  • $F_0$: exciting force amplitude $\text{N}$

  • Undamped Transmissibility $\xi = 0$: $\boxed{TR = \left| \frac{1}{1 - r^2} \right|}$

  • Isolation Region:

  • Vibration isolation $TR < 1.0$ is possible ONLY if $r > \sqrt{2}$ $\omega > \sqrt{2}\omega_n$.
  • At $r = \sqrt{2}$, $TR = 1.0$ for all values of damping ratio $\xi$.

5. Critical / Whirling Speeds of Shafts

  • Critical Angular Speed $\omega_c$:

    $${\boxed{\omega_c = \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \quad (\text{rad/s})}}$$
    $${\boxed{N_c = \frac{60}{2\pi} \cdot \omega_c \quad (\text{rpm})}}$$

  • Whirling Deflection Relation:

    $${\boxed{y = \frac{e \cdot r^2}{1 - r^2}}}$$

  • $y$: additional radial deflection / whirl amplitude $\text{m}$
  • $e$: eccentricity of mass center from geometric shaft axis $\text{m}$
  • $r$: speed ratio $= \frac{\omega}{\omega_c}$

6. Torsional Vibrations & Nodes

  • Number of Nodes $N_{\text{nodes}}$:

    $${\boxed{N_{\text{nodes}} = n_{\text{rotors}} - 1}}$$

  • Two-Rotor System Node Position:

    $${\boxed{I_A \cdot l_A = I_B \cdot l_B}}$$

  • $I_A, I_B$: polar mass moments of inertia of rotors $A$ and $B$ $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$
  • $l_A, l_B$: distances of node from rotors $A$ and $B$ $\text{m}$
  • Total shaft length: $L = l_A + l_B$

7. Special & Approximate Methods

  • Dunkerley's Equation (Multi-Load Shaft Transverse Vibration):
    $${\boxed{\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{f_n^2}}}$$
  • $f$: fundamental frequency of system with all loads acting together $\text{Hz}$

  • $f_i$: natural frequency of shaft due to load $i$ acting alone $\text{Hz}$

  • Rolling Cylinder with Central Spring:

    $${\boxed{\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{3m}} \quad (\text{rad/s})}}$$

  • Accounts for translational mass $m$ and rotational inertia $I = \frac{1}{2}mR^2$ of cylinder rolling without slip.

2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Free Vibration: Oscillation of a system restoring itself after an initial displacement, acting under internal forces only with no continuous external forces.
  • Forced Vibration: Vibration driven by a continuous external periodic force, taking place at the frequency of the exciting force.
  • Longitudinal Vibration: Shaft particles move parallel to the shaft axis; shaft undergoes alternating tensile and compressive stresses.
  • Transverse Vibration: Shaft particles move perpendicular to the shaft axis; shaft undergoes alternating bending stresses.
  • Torsional Vibration: Shaft particles swing in circular paths about the shaft axis; shaft undergoes alternating shear stresses.
  • Critical / Whirling Speed: The speed at which a rotating shaft becomes dynamically unstable, vibrating violently in the transverse direction due to resonance between rotation frequency and transverse natural frequency.
  • Damping Ratio $\xi$: The ratio of actual viscous damping coefficient to the critical damping coefficient $\xi = c/c_c$.
  • Logarithmic Decrement $\delta$: The natural logarithm of the ratio of any two successive peak amplitudes on the same side during damped free vibration.
  • Transmissibility $TR$: The ratio of the force transmitted to the foundation to the exciting force acting on the mass.
  • Node: A point or section along a vibrating shaft where the vibration amplitude (translational or rotational) is zero.

2.3 — Standard Values & Physical Constants

  • Damping Categories:
  • Underdamped: $\xi < 1.0$ (oscillatory motion)
  • Critically Damped: $\xi = 1.0$ (fastest return to equilibrium without oscillation)
  • Overdamped: $\xi > 1.0$ (slow return to equilibrium, no oscillation)
  • Phase Angle at Resonance: $\phi = 90^\circ$ $1.5708 \text{ rad}$.
  • Transmissibility crossover point: $r = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.
  • Rigid Rotor Threshold: Operating speed $N < 70\% \cdot N_c$.

SECTION 3: MAIN BODY

3.1 Undamped Free Vibrations & Natural Frequency

1. Concept Pointers
  • Longitudinal vs Transverse vs Torsional:
  • Longitudinal: Particles move parallel to shaft axis (alternating tensile/compressive stresses).
  • Transverse: Particles move perpendicular to shaft axis (alternating bending stresses).
  • Torsional: Particles move in circles about shaft axis (alternating shear stresses).
  • Natural Frequency Mechanics:
  • Depends solely on stiffness $k$ and mass $m$.
  • Independent of initial amplitude or velocity for linear systems.
  • Static Deflection Method:
  • Transverse/longitudinal natural frequency can be determined using static deflection under self-weight:
    $${f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}}$$
  • Gravity Independence:
  • Although the formula $\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}$ contains $g$, the actual natural frequency of a spring-mass system is INDEPENDENT OF GRAVITY! On the Moon, where $g$ is $1/6$ of Earth, static deflection $\delta$ decreases by a factor of 6, leaving the ratio $g/\delta = k/m$ unchanged.
💡 Key Concept: Gravity Independence of Spring-Mass Frequency: A spring-mass system has the same natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ on Earth, on the Moon, and in zero-gravity space! Static deflection adjusts to balance gravity, but the ratio $g/\delta$ remains constant.

[!TRAP] Direction Classification Trap: - Parallel to shaft axis $\implies$ Longitudinal. - Perpendicular to shaft axis $\implies$ Transverse. - Angular twisting about shaft axis $\implies$ Torsional. Do not mix up these classifications in MCQs!

2. Decision Tree
Vibration Frequency / Type Check?
├─ Are particles moving parallel to axis? → LONGITUDINAL VIBRATION
├─ Are particles moving perpendicular to axis? → TRANSVERSE VIBRATION
├─ Are particles twisting about axis? → TORSIONAL VIBRATION
├─ Is static deflection delta known? → fn = (1 / 2*pi) * sqrt(g / delta)
└─ Does natural frequency change on the Moon? → NO (fn remains identical)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Longitudinal: parallel, tensile/compressive.
  • [ ] Transverse: perpendicular, bending.
  • [ ] Torsional: twisting, shear.
  • [ ] $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{g/\delta}$.
  • [ ] Natural frequency is independent of gravity.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A steel shaft of length $L = 1.0 \text{ m}$ is simply supported at its ends and carries a rotor of mass $m = 153 \text{ kg}$ (weight $W = 1.5 \text{ kN}$) at mid-span. If Young's modulus $E = 200 \text{ GPa}$ and shaft moment of inertia $I = 1.9175 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$. Calculate the fundamental natural frequency of transverse vibration. - Solution: - Calculate static deflection $\delta$ under concentrated central load:

$${\delta = \frac{W \cdot L^3}{48 \cdot E \cdot I} = \frac{1500 \times (1.0)^3}{48 \times (200 \times 10^9) \times (1.9175 \times 10^{-8})}}$$
$${\delta = \frac{1500}{184080} \approx 0.0081486 \text{ m} \approx 0.815 \text{ cm}}$$
- Calculate natural frequency:
$${f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{9.81}{0.0081486}} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{1203.88} \approx \frac{34.697}{6.283} \approx 5.52 \text{ Hz}}$$
- Key Insight: $f_n \approx 5.52 \text{ Hz}$. Deflection under central load uses $\delta = \frac{W L^3}{48EI}$.

3.2 Effect of Damping & Decay Metrics

1. Concept Pointers
  • Damping Force:
  • Viscous damping force is proportional to velocity $F_d = -c \dot{x}$ and acts to oppose motion.
  • Damping States:
  • Underdamped $\xi < 1.0$: System oscillates with decaying amplitude.
  • Critically damped $\xi = 1.0$: No oscillation; returns to equilibrium in minimum time.
  • Overdamped $\xi > 1.0$: No oscillation; returns to equilibrium slowly.
  • Logarithmic Decrement $\delta$:
  • Natural log of successive peak ratios on the same side: $\delta = \ln(x_n/x_{n+1})$.
  • For successive peaks, amplitude decays exponentially, forming a GEOMETRIC PROGRESSION $\frac{x_1}{x_2} = \frac{x_2}{x_3} = \dots = e^\delta$.
  • For small damping $\xi < 0.2 \implies \delta \approx 2\pi\xi$.
💡 Key Concept: Logarithmic Decrement & Amplitude Ratio: Successive peak amplitudes during underdamped free vibration decrease in a GEOMETRIC PROGRESSION $\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}} = e^\delta = \text{constant}$.

[!TRAP] Damped vs Undamped Frequency: Damped natural frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1-\xi^2}$ is ALWAYS less than undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$.

2. Decision Tree
Damped Free Vibration Check?
├─ Is damping ratio xi < 1.0? → UNDERDAMPED (Oscillatory, amplitude decays exponentially)
├─ Is damping ratio xi = 1.0? → CRITICALLY DAMPED (Fastest return, no oscillation)
├─ Is damping ratio xi > 1.0? → OVERDAMPED (Slow return, no oscillation)
├─ Successive peak amplitude ratio trend? → GEOMETRIC PROGRESSION (ratio = e^delta)
└─ Small damping logarithmic decrement approximation? → delta approx 2 * pi * xi
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $c_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2m\omega_n$.
  • [ ] $\xi = c/c_c$.
  • [ ] $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1-\xi^2}$.
  • [ ] $\delta = \ln(x_n/x_{n+1}) = 2\pi\xi / \sqrt{1-\xi^2}$.
  • [ ] Successive amplitudes form a geometric progression.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: In a damped free vibration system, successive peak amplitudes on the same side are measured as $0.70 \text{ m}$ and $0.28 \text{ m}$. Calculate the damping ratio $\xi$ of the system. - Solution: - Calculate logarithmic decrement:

$${\delta = \ln\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{0.70}{0.28}\right) = \ln(2.5) \approx 0.9163}$$
- Calculate damping ratio:
$${\xi = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}} = \frac{0.9163}{\sqrt{39.4784 + 0.8396}} = \frac{0.9163}{\sqrt{40.318}} = \frac{0.9163}{6.3496} \approx 0.144}$$
- Key Insight: $\xi \approx 0.144$. Use $\delta = \ln(x_1/x_2)$ and the exact conversion formula.

3.3 Forced Vibrations & Resonance

1. Concept Pointers
  • System Frequency:
  • In steady-state forced vibration, the system vibrates at the impressed external forcing frequency $\omega$, NOT the natural frequency $\omega_n$.
  • Resonance $r = 1.0$:
  • Occurs when external forcing frequency matches natural frequency $\omega = \omega_n \implies r=1$.
  • Dynamic magnification factor $M$ peaks. For zero damping, amplitude goes to infinity.
  • Phase Angle at Resonance: The phase lag $\phi$ between exciting force and displacement is EXACTLY $90^\circ$ (independent of damping).
  • Force Balance at Resonance: At $r=1$, the spring force $k x$ balances the inertia force $-m \omega^2 x$. The external exciting force $F_0$ is balanced entirely by the damping force $c \dot{x}$!
💡 Key Concept: Forced Response at Resonance: At $r = 1$, the exciting force is balanced ENTIRELY by the damping force! The phase angle is exactly $\phi = 90^\circ$, regardless of the amount of damping.

[!TRAP] Resonance Phase Angle Trap: The phase lag between forcing function and system response at resonance is ALWAYS $90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$ radians). Do not select $0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$!

2. Decision Tree
Forced Vibration & Resonance Check?
├─ Frequency of system vibration in steady state? → Exciting forcing frequency omega
├─ Phase lag at resonance (r = 1)? → EXACTLY 90 degrees (independent of damping xi)
├─ What force balances exciting force at resonance? → DAMPING FORCE (c * omega * A = F_0)
└─ Magnification Factor at resonance? → M approx 1 / (2 * xi)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Steady state frequency is forcing frequency $\omega$.
  • [ ] Resonance $r = 1.0 \implies \phi = 90^\circ$.
  • [ ] At resonance, exciting force = damping force.
  • [ ] $M = 1 / \sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\xi r)^2}$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A machine mass $m = 50 \text{ kg}$ is mounted on springs of total stiffness $k = 30 \text{ kN/m}$ with damping ratio $\xi = 0.2$. If it experiences a harmonic exciting force of frequency $3 \text{ Hz}$. Calculate the damped natural frequency and the steady-state vibration frequency. - Solution: - Undamped natural frequency:

$${\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{30000}{50}} = \sqrt{600} \approx 24.495 \text{ rad/s} \implies f_n = \frac{24.495}{2\pi} \approx 3.90 \text{ Hz}}$$
- Damped natural frequency:
$${f_d = f_n \sqrt{1 - \xi^2} = 3.90 \times \sqrt{1 - (0.2)^2} = 3.90 \times \sqrt{0.96} = 3.90 \times 0.9798 \approx 3.82 \text{ Hz}}$$
- Steady-state vibration frequency of machine: - In steady-state forced vibration, the system vibrates at the exciting frequency. - Steady-state frequency $= 3.0 \text{ Hz}$. - Key Insight: Damped frequency $= 3.82 \text{ Hz}$, steady-state vibration frequency $= 3.0 \text{ Hz}$ (matches the forcing input).

3.4 Vibration Isolation & Transmissibility

1. Concept Pointers
  • Transmissibility $TR$:
  • Ratio of force transmitted to foundation to exciting force amplitude.
  • Isolation Range $TR < 1.0$:
  • Vibration isolation is possible ONLY when frequency ratio $r > \sqrt{2}$.
  • If $r < \sqrt{2} \implies TR > 1.0$, which amplifies the transmitted force.
  • Effect of Damping on Isolation:
  • At $r = \sqrt{2}$, $TR = 1.0$ for all values of damping ratio $\xi$ (all transmissibility curves intersect at $r=\sqrt{2}$).
  • For $r > \sqrt{2}$ (isolation region): Adding damping INCREASES transmissibility $TR$ (reduces isolation effectiveness). Thus, low damping (or no damping) is preferred in the isolation range!
  • For $r < \sqrt{2}$ (amplification region): Adding damping DECREASES transmissibility $TR$ (protects the system near resonance).
💡 Key Concept: Vibration Isolation Rule: Isolation $TR < 1.0$ occurs ONLY when frequency ratio $r > \sqrt{2}$. In this isolation region, damping actually INCREASES force transmission $TR \uparrow$, making a highly damped isolator less effective than an undamped one!

[!TRAP] Crossover Point Damping Trap: At frequency ratio $r = \sqrt{2}$, transmissibility $TR = 1.0$ regardless of the damping ratio $\xi$. Damping curves all intersect at this exact point!

2. Decision Tree
Vibration Isolation & Transmissibility Check?
├─ Is frequency ratio r > sqrt(2)? → ISOLATION REGION (TR < 1.0)
├─ Is frequency ratio r < sqrt(2)? → AMPLIFICATION REGION (TR > 1.0)
├─ What is TR at r = sqrt(2)? → TR = 1.0 (independent of damping ratio xi)
└─ Effect of damping for r > sqrt(2)? → Damping INCREASES TR (worsens isolation)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $TR = \sqrt{1 + (2\xi r)^2} / \sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\xi r)^2}$.
  • [ ] Isolation if $r > \sqrt{2}$.
  • [ ] At $r = \sqrt{2} \implies TR = 1.0$.
  • [ ] For $r > \sqrt{2}$, damping increases transmitted force.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A refrigerator unit of mass $m = 35 \text{ kg}$ is supported on three springs of equal stiffness $k$. The exciting force frequency is $480 \text{ rpm}$. Design the spring stiffness $k$ (in $\text{N/mm}$) such that only $10\%$ of the exciting force is transmitted to the floor, assuming zero damping $\xi = 0$. - Solution: - Exciting frequency: $\omega = 480 \times \frac{2\pi}{60} = 16\pi \approx 50.265 \text{ rad/s}$. - $TR = 10\% = 0.10$. - Since $TR < 1.0$, we are in the isolation region $r > \sqrt{2}$. - For $\xi = 0 \implies TR = \left| \frac{1}{1-r^2} \right| \implies 0.10 = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1} \implies r^2 - 1 = 10 \implies r^2 = 11 \implies r \approx 3.3166$. - $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} \implies 3.3166 = \frac{50.265}{\omega_n} \implies \omega_n \approx 15.155 \text{ rad/s}$. - Total stiffness: $k_{\text{total}} = m \cdot \omega_n^2 = 35 \times (15.155)^2 = 35 \times 229.68 \approx 8039 \text{ N/m} = 8.039 \text{ N/mm}$. - Stiffness per spring (3 springs in parallel):

$${k = \frac{k_{\text{total}}}{3} = \frac{8.039}{3} \approx 2.68 \text{ N/mm}}$$
- Key Insight: $k \approx 2.68 \text{ N/mm}$ per spring. Use $TR = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1}$ for undamped isolation.

3.5 Critical Speeds & Whirling of Shafts

1. Concept Pointers
  • Whirling Deflection:
  • Centrifugal force on rotor eccentricity $e$ causes shaft bending deflection $y$:
    $${y = \frac{e \cdot r^2}{1 - r^2}}$$
  • At $\omega = \omega_c \implies r = 1.0$, deflection $y \to \infty$ (Resonance).
  • Critical Speed:
  • Whirling speed $\omega_c$ matches natural frequency of transverse vibration: $\omega_c = \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}$.
  • Whirling takes place strictly in the TRANSVERSE DIRECTION (perpendicular to shaft axis).
  • Rigid Rotor Definition:
  • A rotor is considered rigid if its operating speed $N$ is less than $70\%$ of its critical speed $N_c$ $N < 0.7 N_c$. A rigid rotor balanced at one speed remains balanced at all lower speeds.
  • Parametric Influence:
  • Critical speed of a shaft is influenced by shaft diameter (stiffness), span length, rotor mass, and mass eccentricity.
💡 Key Concept: Critical Speed of Shaft: Critical speed $\omega_c$ coincides exactly with the natural frequency of transverse vibration $\omega_c = \sqrt{g/\delta}$. Whirling is a bending resonance in the TRANSVERSE direction!

[!TRAP] Direction of Whirling: Whirling is a transverse (bending) phenomenon, NOT longitudinal or torsional!

2. Decision Tree
Critical Speed & Whirling Check?
├─ Whirling direction of vibration? → TRANSVERSE (bending) direction
├─ Critical Speed value calculation? → wc = sqrt(g / delta) rad/s
├─ Dynamic deflection y at speed ratio r? → y = e * r^2 / (1 - r^2)
└─ Rigid rotor speed threshold? → Operating speed N < 70% of critical speed Nc
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Whirling is transverse vibration.
  • [ ] $\omega_c = \sqrt{g/\delta}$.
  • [ ] Deflection $y = \frac{e r^2}{1-r^2}$.
  • [ ] Rigid rotor operating speed $< 0.70 N_c$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A light shaft carries a rotor of mass $m = 0.2 \text{ kg}$ at its mid-point. The transverse stiffness of the shaft at the rotor location is $k = 72 \text{ kN/m} = 72000 \text{ N/m}$. Calculate the critical speed in rad/s. - Solution: - Whirling critical speed matches transverse natural frequency:

$${\omega_c = \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{72000}{0.2}} = \sqrt{360000} = 600 \text{ rad/s}}$$
- Key Insight: $\omega_c = 600 \text{ rad/s}$. Link critical speed directly to natural frequency.

3.6 Torsional Vibrations & Nodes

1. Concept Pointers
  • Nodes in Torsional Systems:
  • Node is a cross-section of zero angular twist amplitude.
  • For $n$ rotors on a single shaft, the system can vibrate with up to $n-1$ nodes.
    • Two-rotor system $\implies 1$ node.
    • Three-rotor system $\implies 2$ nodes.
  • Two-Rotor Node Location:
  • The node divides the shaft into two segments vibrating in opposite directions.
  • Torque balance about the node:
    $${I_A \cdot l_A = I_B \cdot l_B}$$
  • Segment lengths: $l_A = \frac{I_B L}{I_A + I_B}$ and $l_B = \frac{I_A L}{I_A + I_B}$.
  • The node lies closer to the rotor with the LARGER mass moment of inertia.
💡 Key Concept: Torsional Node Rule: For $n$ rotors, maximum number of nodes is $n-1$. In a two-rotor system, the single node lies closer to the heavier rotor (larger moment of inertia $I$).

[!TRAP] Node Location Trend: If $I_A > I_B$, the node lies closer to rotor $A$ $l_A < l_B$. Do not place the node closer to the lighter rotor!

2. Decision Tree
Torsional Vibration & Nodes Check?
├─ Number of nodes for n-rotor shaft? → Nodes = n - 1
├─ Where is the node in 2-rotor system? → Closer to the rotor with LARGER moment of inertia I
└─ Node location formula? → IA * lA = IB * lB
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Nodes $= n - 1$.
  • [ ] 2-rotor system: 1 node.
  • [ ] $I_A l_A = I_B l_B$.
  • [ ] Node is closer to heavier rotor.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A steel shaft of length $L = 1.2 \text{ m}$ carries two rotors $A$ and $B$ at its ends. The polar mass moment of inertia of rotor $A$ is $I_A = 80 \text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ and that of rotor $B$ is $I_B = 40 \text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$. Locate the position of the node from rotor $A$. - Solution: - Node location condition: $I_A \cdot l_A = I_B \cdot l_B$. - Since $l_A + l_B = L = 1.2 \text{ m} \implies l_B = 1.2 - l_A$. - Substitute: $80 \cdot l_A = 40 \cdot (1.2 - l_A) \implies 80 l_A = 48 - 40 l_A \implies 120 l_A = 48 \implies l_A = 0.4 \text{ m}$. - The node is located $0.4 \text{ m}$ from rotor $A$. - Key Insight: $l_A = 0.4 \text{ m}$ (closer to the heavier rotor $A$).

3.7 Special / Approximate Methods & Systems

1. Concept Pointers
  • Dunkerley's Method:
  • Provides a quick lower-bound (conservative) estimate of fundamental frequency for shafts carrying multiple loads:
    $${\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} + \dots}$$
  • Rolling Cylinder Central Spring System:
  • A cylinder of mass $m$ rolling without slip with a spring attached to its center has natural frequency:
    $${\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{3m}}}$$
  • The factor $2/3$ accounts for rotational kinetic energy of the cylinder $I = \frac{1}{2}mR^2$.
💡 Key Concept: Dunkerley's Frequency Estimation: Dunkerley's equation $\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \sum \frac{1}{f_i^2}$ provides a conservative LOWER BOUND estimate of the actual fundamental frequency.
2. Decision Tree
Special System Frequency Check?
├─ Is it Dunkerley's multi-load shaft approximation? → 1/f^2 = 1/f1^2 + 1/f2^2 + ...
├─ Is it Rolling Cylinder with central spring? → omega_n = sqrt(2*k / 3*m)
└─ Does spring-mass natural frequency change on the Moon? → NO
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Dunkerley: $\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \sum \frac{1}{f_i^2}$.
  • [ ] Rolling cylinder: $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{3m}}$.
  • [ ] Spring-mass frequency is gravity independent.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A shaft carries two rotors. The individual natural frequencies of transverse vibration of the shaft under each load acting alone are $100 \text{ Hz}$ and $200 \text{ Hz}$. Estimate the fundamental frequency of the system using Dunkerley's method. - Solution: - Apply Dunkerley's formula:

$${\frac{1}{f^2} = \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} = \frac{1}{100^2} + \frac{1}{200^2} = \frac{1}{10000} + \frac{1}{40000} = \frac{5}{40000} = \frac{1}{8000}}$$
- $f^2 = 8000 \implies f = \sqrt{8000} \approx 89.44 \text{ Hz}$. - Key Insight: $f \approx 89.44 \text{ Hz}$. Dunkerley's estimate is lower than either individual component frequency.

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (Core Conceptual)

Q1: List the three main types of free vibrations based on particle motion relative to the shaft axis.
A: 1. Longitudinal Vibration (parallel to axis), 2. Transverse Vibration (perpendicular to axis), 3. Torsional Vibration (circular path about axis).

Q2: What is the static deflection formula for natural cyclic frequency $f_n$ of transverse vibration?
A: $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}$, where $\delta$ is the static deflection under self-weight.

Q3: How does natural frequency of a spring-mass system change on the Moon?
A: It remains unchanged. Since $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$, natural frequency is independent of gravity.

Q4: Define the critical damping coefficient $c_c$ and state its formula.
A: The damping coefficient that separates oscillatory decay from non-oscillatory motion: $c_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2m\omega_n$.

Q5: What is the relationship between damped natural frequency $\omega_d$ and undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$?
A: $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \xi^2}$.

Q6: What is logarithmic decrement $\delta$? Write its relation to damping ratio $\xi$.
A: $\delta = \ln(x_n/x_{n+1})$, the natural log of the ratio of successive peak amplitudes. Relation: $\delta = \frac{2\pi\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}$.

Q7: Describe the geometric progression behavior of successive amplitudes in underdamped free vibration.
A: The ratio of any two successive peak amplitudes is constant: $\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}} = e^\delta$.

Q8: At what frequency does a forced vibration system oscillate in steady state?
A: At the external exciting forcing frequency $\omega$.

Q9: What is the phase angle between exciting force and displacement at resonance?
A: Exactly $90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$ radians), independent of the damping ratio.

Q10: Which force balances the external exciting force in a forced vibration system at resonance $r=1.0$?
A: The viscous damping force $c \omega A = F_0$.

Q11: Under what frequency ratio condition is vibration isolation $TR < 1.0$ possible?
A: Only when frequency ratio $r = \omega/\omega_n > \sqrt{2}$.

Q12: What is the value of transmissibility $TR$ at frequency ratio $r = \sqrt{2}$?
A: $TR = 1.0$ for all values of the damping ratio $\xi$.

Q13: Define critical (whirling) speed of a shaft.
A: The speed at which rotating shaft deflection becomes theoretically infinite due to resonance with the transverse natural frequency of the system.

Q14: State the number of nodes in a torsional system with $n$ rotors on a single shaft.
A: $N_{\text{nodes}} = n_{\text{rotors}} - 1$.

Q15: State Dunkerley's equation for approximate transverse natural frequency of a multi-load shaft.
A: $\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{f_n^2}$.

SECTION 5: CONSOLIDATION NOTES & HIGH-YIELD EXAM TRAPS

  • Types of Vibrations:
  • Longitudinal: parallel, tensile/compressive.
  • Transverse: perpendicular, bending.
  • Torsional: twisting, shear.
  • Natural Frequency: $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$; $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{g/\delta}$. Independent of gravity!
  • Damping Ratio: $\xi = c/c_c = c/(2\sqrt{km})$. Underdamped $\xi < 1.0$, critical $\xi = 1.0$, overdamped $\xi > 1.0$.
  • Logarithmic Decrement: $\delta = \ln(x_n/x_{n+1}) = 2\pi\xi/\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$. Successive amplitudes form a geometric progression.
  • Resonance: Forcing frequency $\omega = \omega_n \implies r = 1$.
  • Phase lag $\phi = 90^\circ$ (independent of damping).
  • Exciting force balanced entirely by damping force.
  • Vibration Isolation: Possible ONLY for $r > \sqrt{2}$.
  • At $r = \sqrt{2} \implies TR = 1.0$ (damping has no effect).
  • For $r > \sqrt{2} \implies$ damping INCREASES force transmission ($TR \uparrow$, worsens isolation).
  • Critical Speeds: Whirling speed $\omega_c$ matches transverse natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{g/\delta}$.
  • Whirling deflection: $y = \frac{e r^2}{1-r^2}$.
  • Rigid rotor speed limit: $N < 70\% N_c$.
  • Torsional Nodes: $N_{\text{nodes}} = n_{\text{rotors}} - 1$. Two rotors $\implies 1$ node. Torque balance $I_A l_A = I_B l_B$ (node closer to heavier rotor).
  • Dunkerley: $\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \sum \frac{1}{f_i^2}$ (conservative lower bound).
  • Rolling Cylinder: Center spring natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{3m}}$.
