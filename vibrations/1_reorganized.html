1. Free Vibrations & Natural Frequency

A mechanical system displaced from equilibrium and released will oscillate at its natural frequency — a property determined entirely by mass and stiffness, not by the amplitude of the initial disturbance or by gravity.

1.1 Types of Free Vibration

TypeDirection of motionStress inducedStiffness $k$
Longitudinal Parallel to shaft axis Tensile / compressive $k = AE/L$
Transverse Perpendicular to shaft axis Bending Cantilever: $3EI/L^3$; Simply supported: $48EI/L^3$
Torsional Rotational about shaft axis Torsional shear $q = GJ/L$

1.2 Natural Frequency — Spring-Mass System

Undamped Natural Frequency
$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \;\text{[rad/s]} \qquad f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \;\text{[Hz]}$$
$k$ = stiffness [N/m]  ·  $m$ = mass [kg].
Independent of gravity $g$. The same system gives identical $\omega_n$ on Earth, the Moon, or in space.

1.3 Static Deflection Method

When a mass $m$ hangs from a spring under gravity, it causes a static deflection $\delta = mg/k$. Substituting this into the natural frequency formula gives a useful practical relation:

Natural Frequency via Static Deflection
$$f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \approx \frac{4.987}{\sqrt{\delta\,[\text{cm}]}} \;\text{Hz}$$
$\delta$ in metres for the exact form; $\delta$ in cm for the approximate $4.987$ form.
Used for shafts and beams where $\delta$ is found from beam theory, not $mg/k$ directly.

1.4 Special Configurations

Rolling cylinder with central spring

A solid cylinder of mass $m$ rolling without slip on a horizontal surface, with a spring $k$ attached to its centre, has a higher effective inertia than a simple mass. The rotational kinetic energy contributes an additional $\frac{1}{2}I\dot\theta^2 = \frac{1}{4}m\dot{x}^2$, giving total $KE = \frac{3}{4}m\dot{x}^2$. Setting equal to $\frac{3}{2}m_{\text{eff}}\dot{x}^2$ shows the effective mass is $\frac{3}{2}m$:

Rolling Cylinder + Central Spring
$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{3m}}$$
Rotational inertia increases effective mass to $\tfrac{3}{2}m$, lowering $\omega_n$ relative to a pure sliding mass.

Spring with distributed mass

If the spring itself has mass $m_s$, one-third of its mass adds to the lumped mass:

Effective Mass with Spring Mass
$$m_{\text{eq}} = m + \frac{m_s}{3}$$

Longitudinal bar / shaft

Longitudinal Natural Frequency
$$f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{AE}{mL}}$$
$A$ = cross-sectional area, $E$ = Young's modulus, $L$ = length, $m$ = total mass of bar.

2. Damped Free Vibrations

Viscous damping introduces a force proportional to velocity, $F_d = -C\dot{x}$, which dissipates energy each cycle. The equation of motion for a damped spring-mass system is:

Equation of Motion — Damped System
$$m\ddot{x} + C\dot{x} + kx = 0$$

2.1 Critical Damping & Damping Ratio

Critical damping $C_c$ is the boundary condition: at exactly $C_c$, the system returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without oscillating. Below $C_c$ it oscillates; above it, it creeps back slowly.

Critical Damping Coefficient
$$C_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2m\omega_n \qquad [\text{N·s/m}]$$
Damping Ratio
$$\xi = \frac{C}{C_c} = \frac{C}{2\sqrt{km}}$$
Dimensionless. $\xi < 1$ underdamped · $\xi = 1$ critically damped · $\xi > 1$ overdamped.

2.2 Three Damping Regimes

RegimeConditionMotionPhysical behaviour
Underdamped $\xi < 1$ Oscillatory with exponentially decaying amplitude Oscillates at $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$ until energy is fully dissipated. Most engineering structures fall here.
Critically damped $\xi = 1$ Non-oscillatory; fastest return to equilibrium Returns to rest in minimum time without overshoot. Used in instrument pointers, door closers.
Overdamped $\xi > 1$ Non-oscillatory; sluggish exponential return Takes longer than critical damping to return. System never overshoots.

2.3 Damped Natural Frequency

Damped Natural Frequency
$$\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \xi^2} \qquad [\text{rad/s}]$$
Always $\omega_d < \omega_n$ for any $\xi > 0$.
For small damping ($\xi < 0.1$): $\omega_d \approx \omega_n$ — the damped and undamped frequencies are nearly equal.

2.4 Logarithmic Decrement

In underdamped free vibration, successive peak amplitudes on the same side decay by a constant ratio — they form a geometric progression. The logarithmic decrement $\delta$ quantifies this decay rate:

Logarithmic Decrement — Definition
$$\delta = \ln\!\left(\frac{x_i}{x_{i+1}}\right) = \frac{2\pi\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}} \approx 2\pi\xi \quad (\xi \ll 1)$$
Each successive amplitude is smaller by a factor of $e^{-\delta}$ relative to the previous one.
Log Decrement over $n$ Cycles & Inverse
$$\delta = \frac{1}{n}\ln\!\left(\frac{x_0}{x_n}\right) \qquad \xi = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}}$$
$x_0/x_n = e^{n\delta}$ — amplitude ratio after $n$ complete cycles.
When amplitude ratio spans $n$ cycles, always divide the log by $n$.
Consecutive peak amplitudes $x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots$ form a geometric progression with common ratio $e^{-\delta}$. That is, $x_0/x_1 = x_1/x_2 = x_2/x_3 = e^\delta$.

3. Forced Vibrations & Resonance

When a harmonic force $F(t) = F_0\sin\omega t$ acts on a damped spring-mass system, the steady-state response oscillates at the forcing frequency $\omega$, not at $\omega_n$. The amplitude of this response depends on how close $\omega$ is to $\omega_n$.

3.1 Frequency Ratio

Frequency Ratio
$$r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n}$$
The single most important parameter governing forced response. $r = 1$ is resonance.

3.2 Magnification Factor

The Magnification Factor (MF) is the ratio of the dynamic steady-state amplitude $A$ to the static deflection $\delta_{\text{st}} = F_0/k$:

Magnification Factor (Dynamic Amplification)
$$\text{MF} = \frac{A}{\delta_{\text{st}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\xi r)^2}}$$
$r \ll 1$ (slow forcing): MF $\to 1$ — response equals static deflection.
$r = 1$ (resonance): MF $= 1/(2\xi)$ — damping alone limits amplitude.
$r \gg 1$ (fast forcing): MF $\to 0$ — system cannot follow rapid oscillations.

3.3 Resonance — $r = 1$

At resonance the forcing frequency equals the natural frequency. Three simultaneous conditions hold:

Resonance Conditions (all three hold at $r = 1$)

1.   Phase lag between force and displacement $= 90°$  (independent of $\xi$)
2.   Steady-state amplitude $A = \dfrac{F_0}{2\xi k} = \dfrac{\delta_{\text{st}}}{2\xi}$
3.   The damping force alone balances the excitation:   $C\omega A = F_0$

Spring force and inertia force cancel exactly at resonance — only damping provides the restoring effect.
At resonance, the phase lag is always $90°$ regardless of the damping ratio. This is a purely geometric property of the frequency response.

3.4 Peak Amplitude (Near Resonance)

For an underdamped system, the MF reaches its absolute maximum slightly below $r = 1$, at:

Peak Resonance Frequency & Peak MF
$$r_{\text{peak}} = \sqrt{1 - 2\xi^2} \qquad \text{MF}_{\text{peak}} = \frac{1}{2\xi\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}$$
For small $\xi$: $r_{\text{peak}} \approx 1$ and $\text{MF}_{\text{peak}} \approx 1/(2\xi)$.

4. Vibration Isolation & Transmissibility

Vibration isolation aims to reduce the force transmitted from a machine to its foundation (or from the ground to a sensitive instrument). The Transmissibility ratio $T$ measures isolation effectiveness:

Transmissibility Ratio
$$T = \frac{F_{\text{transmitted}}}{F_0} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\xi r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\xi r)^2}}$$
$T < 1$ → isolation achieved (less force transmitted than applied).
$T = 1$ → no benefit (transmitted force equals applied force).
$T > 1$ → amplification (transmitted force exceeds applied — harmful).

4.1 The Three Regions of Transmissibility

Frequency ratio $r$Transmissibility $T$Isolation?Effect of increasing $\xi$
$r < \sqrt{2}$ $T > 1$ No — amplification region Reduces $T$ (beneficial — reduces amplification)
$r = \sqrt{2}$ $T = 1$ for all $\xi$ Crossover — no net effect No effect — all curves pass through this point
$r > \sqrt{2}$ $T < 1$ Yes — isolation region Increases $T$ (worsens isolation — use minimum damping here)
The crossover point $r = \sqrt{2}$ is universal — every transmissibility curve passes through $T = 1$ at exactly $r = \sqrt{2}$, regardless of damping ratio. This is one of the most tested facts in vibration isolation.

4.2 Undamped Transmissibility ($\xi = 0$)

Undamped Transmissibility
$$T = \left|\frac{1}{1 - r^2}\right|$$
For $r > \sqrt{2}$: $T = \dfrac{1}{r^2 - 1}$, which decreases as $r$ increases — better isolation with stiffer-ratio mounting.

4.3 Design Principle for Isolation

To achieve isolation, the isolation system must be designed so that $\omega/\omega_n > \sqrt{2}$. This requires making $\omega_n$ sufficiently low, i.e., using soft (low-stiffness) mounts. In the isolation zone, minimising damping gives the best transmissibility reduction — but some damping is always needed to limit amplitude during startup transients when the machine passes through resonance.

5. Critical Speeds & Whirling of Shafts

A real rotor always has a small eccentricity $e$ — the distance between its geometric centre and its centre of mass. When the shaft rotates, this eccentricity generates a centrifugal force that causes transverse deflection. At the critical speed, the centrifugal force and the restoring elastic force balance with only damping keeping deflections finite — small eccentricities produce dangerously large vibrations.

5.1 Critical Speed

Critical (Whirling) Speed
$$\omega_c = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \qquad [\text{rad/s}]$$ $$N_c = \frac{60}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \approx \frac{187.7}{\sqrt{\delta\,[\text{m}]}} \qquad [\text{rpm}]$$
$\delta$ = static transverse deflection of the shaft under the rotor weight [m].
Whirling occurs exclusively in the transverse direction.
Critical speed numerically equals the transverse natural frequency.

5.2 Dynamic Deflection due to Eccentricity

Additional Shaft Deflection at Speed $\omega$
$$y = \frac{e\,r^2}{1 - r^2} = \frac{e}{\left(\omega_c/\omega\right)^2 - 1}$$
For $r < 1$: deflection $y$ is in the same direction as eccentricity (heavy side outward).
For $r > 1$: shaft rotates with eccentricity pointing inward — self-centring behaviour.
At $r = 1$: $y \to \infty$ (resonance / whirl — only damping limits deflection).

5.3 Rigid vs Flexible Rotor

A rotor is classified as rigid when operating below $70\%$ of its first critical speed ($N < 0.70\,N_c$). Rigid rotors can be balanced in a single plane (static balancing). Above this limit, flexible rotor (dynamic) balancing is required.

5.4 Dunkerley's Formula — Multi-Load Shafts

When a shaft carries multiple loads, its fundamental critical frequency is lowered compared to any individual load acting alone. Dunkerley's formula provides a conservative (lower-bound) estimate:

Dunkerley's Approximation
$$\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{f_n^2}$$
$f_i$ = natural frequency of the shaft carrying load $i$ alone.
The result underestimates the true fundamental frequency — the actual $f$ is always slightly higher.
Used when exact analysis is complex and a safe lower bound is needed.

6. Torsional Vibrations

In torsional vibration, the shaft cross-sections twist and untwist periodically about the longitudinal axis. The driving parameter is the shaft's torsional stiffness $q = GJ/L$, where $G$ is the shear modulus and $J$ is the polar second moment of area.

6.1 Single-Rotor Torsional Frequency

Torsional Natural Frequency — Single Rotor
$$f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{q}{I}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{GJ}{IL}}$$
$q = GJ/L$ = torsional stiffness [N·m/rad]  ·  $I$ = polar mass moment of inertia of rotor [kg·m²].
$G$ = shear modulus  ·  $J = \pi d^4/32$ for a solid circular shaft.

6.2 Two-Rotor System — Node Location

In a two-rotor torsional system, there is exactly one node — a cross-section that does not twist. The node lies between the two rotors; its position is found from the condition that the angular momentum about the node is zero:

Two-Rotor Node Condition
$$I_A \cdot l_A = I_B \cdot l_B$$ $$l_A = \frac{I_B \cdot L}{I_A + I_B} \qquad l_B = \frac{I_A \cdot L}{I_A + I_B}$$
$l_A$, $l_B$ = distance of node from rotor A, rotor B respectively  ·  $L = l_A + l_B$ = shaft length.
The node lies closer to the rotor with larger moment of inertia.

6.3 Multi-Rotor Node Count

Number of Torsional Nodes
$$N_{\text{nodes}} = n_{\text{rotors}} - 1$$
2 rotors → 1 node  ·  3 rotors → 2 nodes  ·  and so on.
Each node represents a cross-section of zero torsional amplitude in that mode.

The natural frequency can be verified independently from either rotor's segment. For rotor A's segment:

Torsional Frequency — Two-Rotor System (verify from either side)
$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{GJ}{I_A\,l_A}} = \sqrt{\frac{GJ}{I_B\,l_B}}$$
Both expressions must give the same $\omega_n$ — this is the self-consistency check for the node location calculation.

8. Key Points to Remember

Free Vibrations

  • $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ depends only on stiffness and mass — not on gravity. A spring-mass system has the same $\omega_n$ on the Moon as on Earth.
  • $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{g/\delta}$ — the static deflection method. $\delta$ must be in metres for the exact form.
  • Rolling cylinder with central spring: $\omega_n = \sqrt{2k/3m}$. Effective mass is $\frac{3}{2}m$ due to rotational inertia.
  • Massless spring assumption: if spring has mass $m_s$, use $m_{\text{eq}} = m + m_s/3$.

Damping

  • $C_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2m\omega_n$. Critical damping gives fastest non-oscillatory return to rest.
  • $\xi < 1$ (underdamped): oscillates at $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$. Always $\omega_d < \omega_n$.
  • Logarithmic decrement: $\delta = \ln(x_i/x_{i+1}) = 2\pi\xi/\sqrt{1-\xi^2}$.
  • Successive peak amplitudes form a geometric progression with ratio $e^{-\delta}$.
  • Over $n$ cycles: $\delta = \frac{1}{n}\ln(x_0/x_n)$ — always divide by $n$.
  • Inverse relation: $\xi = \delta/\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}$.

Forced Vibrations & Resonance

  • MF $= 1/\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\xi r)^2}$. At $r \ll 1$: MF $\to 1$. At $r \gg 1$: MF $\to 0$.
  • At resonance ($r = 1$): phase lag $= 90°$ always; amplitude $= F_0/(2\xi k)$; damping force $= F_0$.
  • Inertia force and spring force cancel exactly at resonance — damping alone limits amplitude.
  • Phase lag is $90°$ at resonance regardless of damping ratio.

Vibration Isolation

  • Isolation ($T < 1$) only when $r = \omega/\omega_n > \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.
  • At $r = \sqrt{2}$: $T = 1$ for all damping ratios — universal crossover point.
  • In the isolation zone ($r > \sqrt{2}$): increasing damping worsens isolation (raises $T$).
  • For effective isolation: use soft mounts (low $\omega_n$), minimise damping in operating zone.

Critical Speeds & Whirling

  • Critical speed $\omega_c = \sqrt{g/\delta}$ numerically equals the shaft's transverse natural frequency.
  • Whirling is exclusively a transverse phenomenon.
  • $N_c \approx 187.7/\sqrt{\delta[\text{m}]}$ rpm — memorise the constant.
  • Rigid rotor: operate below $70\%$ of $N_c$. Above $N_c$, self-centring occurs (rotor rotates about mass centre).
  • Dunkerley: $1/f^2 \approx \sum 1/f_i^2$. Always underestimates (conservative — safe for design).

Torsional Vibrations

  • Torsional stiffness: $q = GJ/L$. For solid shaft: $J = \pi d^4/32$.
  • Single rotor: $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{GJ/(IL)}$.
  • $n$ rotors on a shaft → $n-1$ torsional nodes.
  • Two-rotor node position: $I_A l_A = I_B l_B$ → node lies closer to the larger-$I$ rotor.
  • Verify node position by computing $\omega_n$ from both segments — they must agree.
