2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Longitudinal Vibration: Oscillatory motion where particles move parallel to the longitudinal axis of the shaft/bar, inducing tensile/compressive stresses.
  • Transverse Vibration: Oscillatory motion where particles move perpendicular to the shaft axis, inducing bending stresses.
  • Torsional Vibration: Oscillatory motion where shaft cross-sections twist and untwist circularly about the longitudinal axis, inducing torsional shear stresses.
  • Undamped Natural Frequency $\omega_n$: The natural frequency at which a system oscillates freely in the absence of external forces and damping.
  • Critical Damping Coefficient $C_c$: The minimum viscous damping coefficient required to prevent oscillation during free response $C_c = 2\sqrt{km}$.
  • Damping Ratio $\xi$: The non-dimensional ratio of actual damping coefficient $C$ to critical damping coefficient $C_c$ $\xi = C/C_c$.
  • Logarithmic Decrement $\delta$: The natural logarithm of the ratio of two consecutive peak amplitudes on the same side during underdamped free vibration $\delta = \ln(x_i/x_{i+1})$.
  • Magnification Factor $\text{MF}$: The ratio of steady-state dynamic amplitude $A$ to static deflection $\delta_{\text{st}} = F_0/k$.
  • Resonance: The condition occurring when forced excitation frequency $\omega$ equals undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$ $r = 1$, causing maximum amplitude and a $90^\circ$ phase lag.
  • Transmissibility $T$: The ratio of force transmitted to the foundation $F_T$ to the input excitation force $F_0$.
  • Vibration Isolation Region: The frequency domain $r = \omega/\omega_n > \sqrt{2}$ where transmissibility $T < 1$, reducing transmitted forces to the foundation.
  • Critical / Whirling Speed $N_c$: The shaft rotational speed at which centrifugal forces on rotor eccentricities cause violent lateral deflection equal to the transverse natural frequency.
  • Node: A point or cross-section along a vibrating shaft where the deflection or twist amplitude remains permanently zero.
  • Rigid Rotor: A rotor operating below $70\%$ of its first critical speed $N < 0.7 N_c$, allowing single-plane or static balancing.

2.3 — Standard Values & Physical Constants

  • Standard Acceleration due to Gravity $g$: $9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$.
  • Static Deflection Coefficient: $f_n \approx \frac{4.987}{\sqrt{\delta}}$ ($\delta$ in cm) or $f_n \approx \frac{0.4987}{\sqrt{\delta}}$ ($\delta$ in m).
  • Isolation Frequency Crossover: $r = \omega/\omega_n = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.
  • Phase Angle at Resonance: $\phi = 90^\circ$ (or $\pi/2 \text{ rad}$).
  • Rigid Rotor Limit: Operating speed $N < 0.70 N_c$.
  • Two-Rotor Node Count: 1 Node $N_{\text{nodes}} = 2 - 1 = 1$.
  • Three-Rotor Node Count: 2 Nodes $N_{\text{nodes}} = 3 - 1 = 2$.

3.1 Undamped Free Vibrations & Natural Frequency

1. Concept Pointers
  • Types of Free Vibration:
  • Longitudinal: Shaft stretches and contracts along its length (parallel to axis). Stiffness $k = \frac{A E}{L}$.
  • Transverse: Shaft bends side-to-side (perpendicular to axis). Stiffness depends on end supports (e.g., cantilever $k = \frac{3 E I}{L^3}$, simply supported $k = \frac{48 E I}{L^3}$).
  • Torsional: Shaft twists about its axis. Stiffness $q = \frac{G J}{L}$.
  • Natural Frequency Principles:
  • System oscillates freely at $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ after an initial disturbance.
  • $\omega_n$ depends purely on mass $m$ and stiffness $k$. It is independent of gravity $g$ for pure spring-mass systems (a spring-mass system has the EXACT same natural frequency on the Moon as on Earth!).
  • Static Deflection Method: If mass $m$ causes static deflection $\delta = \frac{m g}{k}$ under gravity:
    $${\boxed{\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \implies f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}}}$$
  • Special Mass Configurations:
  • Spring Mass Correction: If spring has mass $m_s$, equivalent system mass is $m_{\text{eq}} = m + \frac{m_s}{3}$.
  • Rolling Cylinder + Spring: A solid cylinder of mass $m$, radius $R$, attached to a spring $k$ at its center and rolling without slipping on a horizontal surface:
    $${\text{Kinetic Energy } KE = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} I \dot{\theta}^2 = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{1}{2} m R^2\right) \left(\frac{\dot{x}}{R}\right)^2 = \frac{3}{4} m \dot{x}^2}$$
    $${\text{Potential Energy } PE = \frac{1}{2} k x^2 \implies \boxed{\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2 k}{3 m}}}}$$
💡 Key Concept: Gravity Independence: Pure spring-mass natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ does NOT depend on acceleration due to gravity $g$. Taking a spring-mass system to the Moon $g_{\text{Moon}} = g/6$ changes static deflection $\delta$, but leaves $\omega_n$ UNCHANGED!

[!TRAP] Static Deflection Units: When using $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{g/\delta}$, static deflection $\delta$ MUST be converted to meters $\text{m}$. If $\delta$ is given in $\text{mm}$, divide by $1000$ before taking the square root!

4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Shaft Natural Frequency via Static Deflection - Problem: A simply supported shaft of span $L = 1.0 \text{ m}$ carries a concentrated rotor load $W = 1.5 \text{ kN} = 1500 \text{ N}$ at mid-span. Shaft diameter $d = 25 \text{ mm} = 0.025 \text{ m}$ and Young's modulus $E = 200 \text{ GPa} = 2 \times 10^{11} \text{ N/m}^2$. Calculate: (a) Static deflection $\delta$, (b) Undamped natural frequency $f_n$ in Hz. - Solution: 1. Calculate area moment of inertia $I$:

$${I = \frac{\pi d^4}{64} = \frac{\pi \times (0.025)^4}{64} \approx 1.9175 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4}$$
2. Calculate static deflection $\delta$ at mid-span for simply supported beam:
$${\delta = \frac{W L^3}{48 E I} = \frac{1500 \times (1.0)^3}{48 \times (2 \times 10^{11}) \times (1.9175 \times 10^{-8})} = \frac{1500}{1.8408 \times 10^5} \approx 8.148 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m} = 8.148 \text{ mm}}$$
3. Calculate natural frequency $f_n$:
$${f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{9.81}{8.148 \times 10^{-3}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{1203.98} = \frac{34.70}{2\pi} \approx 5.52 \text{ Hz}}$$
- Key Insight: $f_n = 5.52 \text{ Hz}$. Always compute $I = \pi d^4 / 64$ accurately in consistent SI units.

Worked Example 2: Rolling Cylinder with Central Spring - Problem: A solid uniform cylinder of mass $m = 7 \text{ kg}$ and radius $R = 0.22 \text{ m}$ is attached to a horizontal spring of stiffness $k = 6000 \text{ N/m}$ at its center. The cylinder rolls without slipping on a rough horizontal plane. Calculate the natural frequency $f_n$ in Hz. - Solution: 1. Apply governing rolling cylinder formula:

$${\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2 k}{3 m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6000}{3 \times 7}} = \sqrt{\frac{12000}{21}} = \sqrt{571.43} \approx 23.904 \text{ rad/s}}$$
2. Convert to Hz:
$${f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} = \frac{23.904}{2\pi} \approx 3.804 \text{ Hz}}$$
- Key Insight: $f_n = 3.80 \text{ Hz}$. Rotational inertia increases effective mass from $m$ to $1.5 m$, lowering $\omega_n$ by factor $\sqrt{2/3}$.

Worked Example 3: Spring-Mass Natural Frequency on Moon - Problem: A spring-mass system has a natural frequency $f_n = 10 \text{ Hz}$ on Earth $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$. If transported to the Moon where $g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.635 \text{ m/s}^2$ $g/6$, determine its new natural frequency $f_{n,\text{Moon}}$. - Solution: 1. Formula $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ depends only on stiffness $k$ and mass $m$. 2. Mass $m$ and spring stiffness $k$ remain constant regardless of location. 3. Therefore, $f_{n,\text{Moon}} = f_{n,\text{Earth}} = 10 \text{ Hz}$. - Key Insight: $f_{n,\text{Moon}} = 10 \text{ Hz}$. Static deflection $\delta$ increases on Earth and decreases on the Moon, but ratio $g/\delta = k/m$ remains constant!

3.2 Damped Free Vibrations (Viscous Damping & Logarithmic Decrement)

1. Concept Pointers
  • Viscous Damping Force:
  • Damping force $F_d = -C \dot{x}$ opposes velocity.
  • Differential equation of motion: $m \ddot{x} + C \dot{x} + k x = 0$.
  • Damping Ratio $\xi$:
  • Critical damping coefficient $C_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2 m \omega_n$.
  • Damping ratio $\xi = \frac{C}{C_c} = \frac{C}{2\sqrt{km}}$.
  • Three Damping Regimes:
    • Underdamped $\xi < 1$: Oscillatory decay. Roots are complex conjugates. $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1-\xi^2}$.
    • Critically Damped $\xi = 1$: Non-oscillatory. Returns to equilibrium in shortest possible time without overshooting.
    • Overdamped $\xi > 1$: Non-oscillatory. Slow exponential return to equilibrium.
  • Logarithmic Decrement $\delta$:
  • Defined as natural logarithm of ratio of successive amplitudes on same side:
    $${\delta = \ln\left(\frac{x_0}{x_1}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right) = \dots = \ln\left(\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}}\right)}$$
  • Governing relationship:
    $${\boxed{\delta = \frac{2\pi \xi}{\sqrt{1 - \xi^2}}}}$$
    $${\boxed{\xi = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}}}}$$
  • For small damping $\xi < 0.2$, $\delta \approx 2\pi\xi$.
  • For amplitudes measured across $n$ complete cycles ($x_0$ to $x_n$):
    $${\delta = \frac{1}{n} \ln\left(\frac{x_0}{x_n}\right) \implies \frac{x_0}{x_n} = e^{n \delta}}$$
💡 Key Concept: Geometric Progression of Amplitudes: In underdamped free vibration, consecutive peak amplitudes $x_0, x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots$ form a GEOMETRIC PROGRESSION with common ratio $e^{-\delta}$. Thus $\frac{x_0}{x_1} = \frac{x_1}{x_2} = \frac{x_2}{x_3} = e^\delta$.

[!TRAP] Number of Cycles in Log Decrement: When given amplitude ratio over $n$ cycles (e.g., amplitude drops from $x_0$ to $x_5$ in $5$ cycles), divide by $n = 5$: $\delta = \frac{1}{5} \ln(x_0 / x_5)$! Do NOT forget the $1/n$ factor.

  • [ ] Damping ratio from $\delta$: $\xi = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}}$.

4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Damping Ratio and Damped Frequency Calculation - Problem: A vibrating system has mass $m = 10 \text{ kg}$, stiffness $k = 4000 \text{ N/m}$, and viscous damping coefficient $C = 40 \text{ N}\cdot\text{s/m}$. Calculate: (a) Undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$, (b) Critical damping coefficient $C_c$, (c) Damping ratio $\xi$, (d) Damped natural frequency $\omega_d$, (e) Logarithmic decrement $\delta$. - Solution: 1. Calculate undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$:

$${\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{4000}{10}} = \sqrt{400} = 20 \text{ rad/s}}$$
2. Calculate critical damping coefficient $C_c$:
$${C_c = 2 m \omega_n = 2 \times 10 \times 20 = 400 \text{ N}\cdot\text{s/m}}$$
3. Calculate damping ratio $\xi$:
$${\xi = \frac{C}{C_c} = \frac{40}{400} = 0.10 \quad \text{(Underdamped)}}$$
4. Calculate damped natural frequency $\omega_d$:
$${\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \xi^2} = 20 \sqrt{1 - (0.10)^2} = 20 \sqrt{0.99} = 20 \times 0.994987 \approx 19.90 \text{ rad/s}}$$
5. Calculate logarithmic decrement $\delta$:
$${\delta = \frac{2\pi \xi}{\sqrt{1 - \xi^2}} = \frac{2\pi \times 0.10}{\sqrt{0.99}} = \frac{0.62832}{0.994987} \approx 0.6315}$$
- Key Insight: $\xi = 0.10$, $\omega_d = 19.90 \text{ rad/s}$, $\delta = 0.6315$. Light damping lowers natural frequency by less than $0.5\%$.

Worked Example 2: Damping Ratio from Multi-Cycle Amplitude Decay - Problem: In an underdamped free vibration experiment, the initial amplitude $x_0 = 50 \text{ mm}$. After $5$ complete cycles, the amplitude reduces to $x_5 = 10 \text{ mm}$. Calculate: (a) Logarithmic decrement $\delta$, (b) Damping ratio $\xi$. - Solution: 1. Calculate logarithmic decrement $\delta$ over $n = 5$ cycles:

$${\delta = \frac{1}{n} \ln\left(\frac{x_0}{x_5}\right) = \frac{1}{5} \ln\left(\frac{50}{10}\right) = \frac{1}{5} \ln(5) = \frac{1.6094}{5} \approx 0.3219}$$
2. Calculate damping ratio $\xi$:
$${\xi = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}} = \frac{0.3219}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + (0.3219)^2}} = \frac{0.3219}{\sqrt{39.4784 + 0.1036}} = \frac{0.3219}{\sqrt{39.582}} = \frac{0.3219}{6.2914} \approx 0.05116}$$
- Key Insight: $\delta = 0.3219$, $\xi = 0.0512$ $5.12\%$. For small $\xi$, approximation $\xi \approx \frac{\delta}{2\pi} = \frac{0.3219}{6.2832} = 0.0512$ gives identical result!

3.3 Forced Vibrations, Magnification Factor & Resonance

1. Concept Pointers
  • Forced Harmonic Excitation:
  • System subjected to periodic force $F(t) = F_0 \sin(\omega t)$.
  • Differential equation: $m \ddot{x} + C \dot{x} + k x = F_0 \sin(\omega t)$.
  • Total solution = Transient response (dies out) + Steady-state response.
  • Steady-State Amplitude & Magnification Factor:
  • Steady-state amplitude $A$:
    $${A = \frac{F_0 / k}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2 \xi r)^2}}}$$
  • Magnification Factor $\text{MF}$: Ratio of dynamic amplitude $A$ to static deflection $\delta_{\text{st}} = F_0/k$:
    $${\boxed{\text{MF} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2 \xi r)^2}}}}$$
    where frequency ratio $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n}$.
  • Resonance Phenomena $r = 1$:
  • Occurs when excitation frequency matches natural frequency $\omega = \omega_n$.
  • At $r = 1$:
    $${\text{MF}{\text{res}} = \frac{1}{2\xi} \implies \boxed{A{\text{res}} = \frac{F_0}{2 \xi k}}}$$
  • Phase Angle $\phi$: The steady-state displacement lags the excitation force by phase angle $\phi$:
    $${\tan\phi = \frac{2 \xi r}{1 - r^2}}$$
    • At low frequency $r \ll 1$: $\phi \approx 0^\circ$ (in-phase).
    • At resonance $r = 1$: $\boxed{\phi = 90^\circ}$ ($\pi/2 \text{ rad}$, exact, independent of $\xi$).
    • At high frequency $r \gg 1$: $\phi \approx 180^\circ$ (out-of-phase).
  • Force Equilibrium at Resonance: At $r = 1$, spring inertia force $m \omega^2 A$ balances spring restoring force $k A$. The external force $F_0$ is COMPLETELY BALANCED by viscous damping force $C \omega A$!
💡 Key Concept: Resonance Force Balance: At resonance $r = 1$, inertia force $m \omega^2 A$ and spring stiffness force $k A$ are equal and opposite, cancelling each other completely. The entire external driving force $F_0$ is balanced solely by the VISCOUS DAMPING FORCE $C \omega A$!

[!TRAP] Peak Amplitude vs Resonance: Peak amplitude occurs slightly BEFORE resonance at $r_{\text{peak}} = \sqrt{1 - 2\xi^2}$ (for $\xi < 1/\sqrt{2}$). For light damping $\xi < 0.1$, $r_{\text{peak}} \approx 1$ and peak $\text{MF} \approx \frac{1}{2\xi}$.

Worked Example 1: Resonance Amplitude and Damping Requirement - Problem: A machine of mass $m = 100 \text{ kg}$ mounted on springs of total stiffness $k = 400 \text{ kN/m} = 4 \times 10^5 \text{ N/m}$ is subjected to a harmonic force $F(t) = 200 \sin(\omega t) \text{ N}$. 3.4 Vibration Isolation & Transmissibility Rules

1. Concept Pointers
  • Purpose of Isolation:
  • Prevents transmission of large dynamic forces from vibrating machinery to supporting floor/foundation, or protects sensitive instruments from base motion.
  • Transmissibility Ratio $T$:
  • Ratio of transmitted force magnitude $F_T$ to applied force amplitude $F_0$:
    $${\boxed{T = \frac{F_T}{F_0} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2 \xi r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2 \xi r)^2}}}}$$
  • For zero damping $\xi = 0$:
    $${T = \left\vert \frac{1}{1 - r^2} \right\vert = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1} \quad (\text{for } r > 1)}$$
  • The Critical Isolation Threshold $r = \sqrt{2}$:
  • When $r = \sqrt{2}$:
    $${T = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\xi \sqrt{2})^2}{(1 - (\sqrt{2})^2)^2 + (2\xi \sqrt{2})^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 8\xi^2}{(-1)^2 + 8\xi^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 8\xi^2}{1 + 8\xi^2}} = 1}$$
  • ALL transmissibility curves for ALL damping ratios pass through $T = 1$ at $r = \sqrt{2}$!
  • Three Transmissibility Operating Zones:
  • Zone 1 $r < \sqrt{2}$: $T > 1$ (Force is AMPLIFIED). Damping is BENEFICIAL here because it reduces $T$.
  • Zone 2 $r = \sqrt{2}$: $T = 1$ (Transmitted force equals applied force regardless of damping).
  • Zone 3 $r > \sqrt{2}$: $T < 1$ (ISOLATION REGION). Dynamic force transmitted to foundation is less than applied force.
    • In this region $r > \sqrt{2}$, increasing damping INCREASES transmissibility $T$ (worsens isolation)! Therefore, low damping or un-damped flexible mounts are preferred in the isolation zone once operating speed is reached.
💡 Key Concept: Vibration Isolation Rule: Vibration isolation occurs ONLY IF $r = \omega/\omega_n > \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$. For $r < \sqrt{2}$, transmissibility $T > 1$ and damping helps. For $r > \sqrt{2}$, transmissibility $T < 1$ and adding damping actually WORSENS isolation!

[!TRAP] Undamped Transmissibility Sign Trap: For $r > \sqrt{2}$, $1 - r^2$ is negative. Always use absolute value $T = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1}$ when calculating required frequency ratio for undamped isolators!

Worked Example 1: Isolator Spring Stiffness Calculation (PYQ Pattern) - Problem: A refrigerator unit of mass $m = 35 \text{ kg}$ is driven by a motor running at $N = 480 \text{ rpm}$. The unit is mounted on $4$ equal springs. Determine the required total spring stiffness $k_{\text{total}}$ $\text{N/mm}$ such that only $10\%$ $T = 0.10$ of the shaking force is transmitted to the supporting floor. Assume zero damping $\xi = 0$. - Solution: 1. Calculate excitation angular frequency $\omega$:

$${\omega = \frac{2\pi N}{60} = \frac{2\pi \times 480}{60} = 16\pi \approx 50.265 \text{ rad/s}}$$
2. For $T = 0.10 < 1$, operation must be in isolation zone $r > \sqrt{2}$:
$${T = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1} \implies 0.10 = \frac{1}{r^2 - 1} \implies r^2 - 1 = 10 \implies r^2 = 11}$$
$${r = \sqrt{11} \approx 3.3166}$$
3. Calculate required natural frequency $\omega_n$:
$${\omega_n = \frac{\omega}{r} = \frac{50.265}{3.3166} \approx 15.156 \text{ rad/s}}$$
4. Calculate required total stiffness $k_{\text{total}}$:
$${k_{\text{total}} = m \omega_n^2 = 35 \times (15.156)^2 = 35 \times 229.70 \approx 8039.6 \text{ N/m} = 8.04 \text{ N/mm}}$$
5. Stiffness per spring (if 4 springs in parallel):
$${k_{\text{spring}} = \frac{k_{\text{total}}}{4} = \frac{8.04}{4} = 2.01 \text{ N/mm}}$$
- Key Insight: $k_{\text{total}} = 8.04 \text{ N/mm}$, $k_{\text{spring}} = 2.01 \text{ N/mm}$. $r = \sqrt{11} \approx 3.32 > \sqrt{2}$, satisfying the isolation condition.

3.5 Critical Speeds & Whirling of Shafts

1. Concept Pointers
  • Whirling / Critical Speed Phenomenon:
  • Rotation of a shaft carrying rotors causes centrifugal forces on unbalance eccentricities $e$.
  • At critical speed $N_c$, centrifugal force matches shaft restoring force, producing violent transverse bending oscillations.
  • Whirling occurs strictly in the TRANSVERSE direction (not longitudinal or torsional).
  • Critical angular speed $\omega_c$ equals the transverse natural frequency $\omega_n$ of the shaft:
    $${\boxed{\omega_c = \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}}}$$
    $${\boxed{N_c = \frac{60}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \approx \frac{187.7}{\sqrt{\delta}} \quad (\text{rpm, } \delta \text{ in m})}}$$
  • Whirling Shaft Deflection $y$:
  • Total shaft center deflection $y$ due to rotor mass center eccentricity $e$:
    $${\boxed{y = \frac{e r^2}{1 - r^2} = \frac{e}{\left(\frac{\omega_c}{\omega}\right)^2 - 1}}}$$
  • For speed below critical $\omega < \omega_c$: $y$ is positive (mass center stays outside geometric shaft center).
  • For speed above critical $\omega > \omega_c$: $y$ becomes negative, meaning the heavy center of gravity rotates INWARD towards the rotation axis (self-centering behavior at very high speeds $\omega \gg \omega_c \implies y \to -e$).
  • Dunkerley's Method for Multi-Load Shafts:
  • Approximates the fundamental transverse frequency $f$ of a shaft carrying multiple concentrated loads $m_1, m_2, \dots$ plus shaft self-weight:
    $${\boxed{\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_s^2} + \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{f_n^2}}}$$
    where $f_s$ is natural frequency of shaft self-weight alone, and $f_1, f_2, \dots$ are natural frequencies due to each load acting independently.
  • Dunkerley's method provides a conservative lower bound for critical speed.
💡 Key Concept: Whirling Speed Identity: The critical (whirling) speed of a shaft in rpm $N_c$ is NUMERICALLY EQUAL to the natural frequency of transverse vibration $f_n$ converted to rpm $N_c = 60 f_n$.

[!TRAP] Dunkerley Frequency Sum Trap: Dunkerley's formula sums reciprocal SQUARES $\frac{1}{f^2} = \sum \frac{1}{f_i^2}$, NOT direct frequencies $\frac{1}{f} \neq \sum \frac{1}{f_i}$!

        └── If w > w_c ──► Self-centering (heavy side rotates inward towards axis)

  • [ ] Dunkerley formula $\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_s^2} + \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2}$.
  • [ ] Rigid rotor limit: $N < 0.70 N_c$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Critical Speed of Shaft with Single Rotor - Problem: A light shaft supported in bearings carries a rotor of mass $m = 0.2 \text{ kg}$ at mid-span. The shaft stiffness at the rotor location is $k = 72000 \text{ N/m}$. Calculate: (a) Critical angular speed $\omega_c$ in rad/s, (b) Critical speed $N_c$ in rpm. - Solution: 1. Critical angular speed equals transverse natural frequency:

$${\omega_c = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{72000}{0.2}} = \sqrt{360000} = 600 \text{ rad/s}}$$
2. Calculate critical speed in rpm:
$${N_c = \frac{60 \omega_c}{2\pi} = \frac{60 \times 600}{2\pi} = \frac{36000}{2\pi} \approx 5729.6 \text{ rpm}}$$
- Key Insight: $\omega_c = 600 \text{ rad/s}$, $N_c = 5730 \text{ rpm}$. Direct application of $\omega_c = \sqrt{k/m}$.

Worked Example 2: Dunkerley Multi-Rotor Critical Speed Calculation - Problem: A shaft carries two heavy rotors. Operating alone, Rotor 1 gives a transverse natural frequency $f_1 = 100 \text{ Hz}$, and Rotor 2 gives $f_2 = 200 \text{ Hz}$. Neglecting shaft mass, calculate the combined critical speed $N_c$ in rpm using Dunkerley's method. - Solution: 1. Apply Dunkerley's formula:

$${\frac{1}{f^2} = \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} = \frac{1}{100^2} + \frac{1}{200^2} = \frac{1}{10000} + \frac{1}{40000} = 10^{-4} + 0.25 \times 10^{-4} = 1.25 \times 10^{-4}}$$
2. Solve for combined natural frequency $f$:
$${f^2 = \frac{1}{1.25 \times 10^{-4}} = 8000 \implies f = \sqrt{8000} \approx 89.443 \text{ Hz}}$$
3. Calculate critical speed in rpm:
$${N_c = 60 \times f = 60 \times 89.443 \approx 5366.6 \text{ rpm}}$$
- Key Insight: $N_c = 5367 \text{ rpm}$. Dunkerley's method yields a combined frequency $89.4 \text{ Hz}$ lower than either individual frequency ($100 \text{ Hz}$ and $200 \text{ Hz}$).

3.6 Torsional Vibrations & Multi-Rotor Node Locations

1. Concept Pointers
  • Torsional Shaft Dynamics:
  • Shaft twists back and forth under polar moment of inertia $I$ and torsional stiffness $q = \frac{G J}{L}$.
  • Governing differential equation: $I \ddot{\theta} + q \theta = 0$.
  • Natural angular frequency:
    $${\boxed{\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{q}{I}} = \sqrt{\frac{G J}{I L}}}}$$
  • Nodes in Multi-Rotor Systems:
  • A Node is a section along the shaft where the amplitude of torsional vibration is ZERO.
  • Node Formula: For a shaft carrying $n$ rotors:
    $${\boxed{N_{\text{nodes}} = n_{\text{rotors}} - 1}}$$
    • Single Rotor: 0 nodes (vibrates against fixed end).
    • Two Rotors: 1 node (located between the two rotors).
    • Three Rotors: 2 nodes (fundamental mode has 1 node, second mode has 2 nodes).
  • Two-Rotor System Mechanics:
  • Rotors $A$ and $B$ with moments of inertia $I_A$ and $I_B$ at ends of a shaft of length $L$.
  • Single node $N$ located at distance $l_A$ from rotor $A$ and $l_B$ from rotor $B$ $l_A + l_B = L$.
  • Torque Balance Condition:
    $${\boxed{I_A \cdot l_A = I_B \cdot l_B}}$$
    $${l_A = \frac{I_B \cdot L}{I_A + I_B}, \quad l_B = \frac{I_A \cdot L}{I_A + I_B}}$$
  • Both rotors vibrate at the EXACT SAME natural frequency about node $N$:
    $${\boxed{\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{G J}{I_A l_A}} = \sqrt{\frac{G J}{I_B l_B}}}}$$
💡 Key Concept: Node Shift Principle: The node in a two-rotor system lies closer to the rotor with LARGER moment of inertia $I_A > I_B \implies l_A < l_B$. If $I_A \to \infty$, $l_A \to 0$ (node moves directly under rotor A, behaving like a fixed end!).
2. ASCII Decision Tree
Torsional Multi-Rotor System Analysis
│
├── Determine Number of Rotors (n)
│   └── Number of Nodes = n - 1
│
├── Two-Rotor System (n = 2, 1 Node):
│   ├── Step 1: Calculate Node Position from Rotor A: l_A = I_B * L / (I_A + I_B)
│   ├── Step 2: Calculate Node Position from Rotor B: l_B = L - l_A
│   └── Step 3: Calculate Torsional Frequency: w_n = sqrt( G*J / (I_A * l_A) )
│
└── Three-Rotor System (n = 3, 2 Nodes):
    └── Fundamental mode has 1 node; Second harmonic mode has 2 nodes
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Torsional natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{q}{I}} = \sqrt{\frac{G J}{I L}}$.
  • [ ] Number of nodes $= n_{\text{rotors}} - 1$.
  • [ ] Two-rotor node condition $I_A l_A = I_B l_B$.
  • [ ] Node position $l_A = \frac{I_B L}{I_A + I_B}$.
  • [ ] Node lies closer to heavier rotor.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Two-Rotor Node Position & Torsional Frequency - Problem: A steel shaft of length $L = 1.2 \text{ m}$ and diameter $d = 50 \text{ mm} = 0.05 \text{ m}$ carries two rotors at its ends. Rotor A has moment of inertia $I_A = 20 \text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ and Rotor B has $I_B = 40 \text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$. Modulus of rigidity $G = 80 \text{ GPa} = 8 \times 10^{10} \text{ N/m}^2$. Calculate: (a) Node position $l_A$ from Rotor A, (b) Polar moment of inertia of shaft $J$, (c) Torsional natural frequency $f_n$ in Hz. - Solution: 1. Calculate node position $l_A$ from Rotor A:

$${l_A = \frac{I_B \cdot L}{I_A + I_B} = \frac{40 \times 1.2}{20 + 40} = \frac{48}{60} = 0.8 \text{ m}}$$
$${l_B = L - l_A = 1.2 - 0.8 = 0.4 \text{ m}}$$
2. Calculate shaft polar moment of inertia $J$:
$${J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times (0.05)^4}{32} \approx 6.1359 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4}$$
3. Calculate torsional stiffness of segment A $q_A = \frac{G J}{l_A}$:
$${q_A = \frac{(8 \times 10^{10}) \times (6.1359 \times 10^{-7})}{0.8} = \frac{49087.2}{0.8} = 61359 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m/rad}}$$
4. Calculate torsional natural frequency $\omega_n$:
$${\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{q_A}{I_A}} = \sqrt{\frac{61359}{20}} = \sqrt{3067.95} \approx 55.389 \text{ rad/s}}$$
5. Convert to Hz:
$${f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} = \frac{55.389}{2\pi} \approx 8.815 \text{ Hz}}$$
- Key Insight: $l_A = 0.8 \text{ m}$ (closer to heavier Rotor B at $0.4 \text{ m}$), $f_n = 8.82 \text{ Hz}$. Verifying via segment B: $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{G J}{I_B l_B}} = \sqrt{\frac{49087.2}{40 \times 0.4}} = \sqrt{3067.95} = 55.39 \text{ rad/s}$ (identical!).

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (20 Core Conceptual Pairs)

Q1: What is the primary difference between longitudinal and transverse vibrations?
A: In longitudinal vibration, shaft particles move parallel to the axis causing tensile/compressive stress; in transverse vibration, particles move perpendicular to the axis causing bending stress.

Q2: Write the undamped natural frequency formula for a simple spring-mass system.
A: $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \text{ rad/s}$ or $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \text{ Hz}$.

Q3: How is natural frequency related to static deflection $\delta$?
A: $f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} \approx \frac{4.987}{\sqrt{\delta}}$ (with $\delta$ in cm).

Q4: Does gravity affect the natural frequency of a pure spring-mass system?
A: No, natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ depends only on mass and stiffness, so it is identical on Earth and the Moon.

Q5: What is the natural frequency of a solid rolling cylinder attached to a central spring $k$?
A: $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{3m}}$ (rotational inertia increases effective mass to $1.5 m$).

Q6: Define Critical Damping Coefficient $C_c$.
A: The minimum viscous damping required to prevent oscillation in free vibration, equal to $C_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2 m \omega_n$.

Q7: Describe the motion of an underdamped system $\xi < 1$.
A: The system oscillates with exponentially decaying amplitude at frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1-\xi^2}$.

Q8: Define Logarithmic Decrement $\delta$.
A: The natural logarithm of the ratio of two consecutive peak amplitudes on the same side during underdamped free vibration $\delta = \ln(x_i/x_{i+1}) = \frac{2\pi\xi}{\sqrt{1-\xi^2}}$.

Q9: What mathematical sequence do successive peak amplitudes form in underdamped free vibration?
A: A Geometric Progression with common ratio $e^{-\delta}$.

Q10: Write the Magnification Factor $\text{MF}$ formula for forced vibrations.
A: $\text{MF} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\xi r)^2}}$ where $r = \omega/\omega_n$.

Q11: What is the phase lag between excitation force and displacement at resonance $r = 1$?
A: Exactly $90^\circ$ $\pi/2 \text{ rad}$, independent of the damping ratio.

Q12: At resonance, which internal force balances the applied external force $F_0$?
A: The viscous damping force $C \omega A$ (inertia and spring forces cancel each other completely).

Q13: What is the condition for vibration isolation to be effective $T < 1$?
A: Frequency ratio $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} > \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.

Q14: What is the value of Transmissibility $T$ at $r = \sqrt{2}$ for any damping ratio?
A: $T = 1$ for all damping ratios $\xi$.

Q15: How does increasing damping affect transmissibility in the isolation region $r > \sqrt{2}$?
A: Increasing damping INCREASES transmissibility $T$, which worsens vibration isolation.

Q16: What is the critical (whirling) speed of a shaft?
A: The rotational speed at which centrifugal forces cause violent lateral deflection, matching the shaft's transverse natural frequency $\omega_c = \sqrt{g/\delta}$.

Q17: In which direction does whirling of a shaft occur?
A: Exclusively in the TRANSVERSE direction.

Q18: Write Dunkerley's formula for the fundamental frequency of a multi-load shaft.
A: $\frac{1}{f^2} \approx \frac{1}{f_1^2} + \frac{1}{f_2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{f_n^2}$.

Q19: How many nodes exist in a torsional shaft carrying $n$ rotors?
A: $N_{\text{nodes}} = n - 1$ nodes.

Q20: What is the node location condition for a two-rotor system?
A: $I_A \cdot l_A = I_B \cdot l_B$ (the node lies closer to the rotor with larger moment of inertia).
