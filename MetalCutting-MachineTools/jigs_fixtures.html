Jigs and Fixtures

This guide covers jigs and fixtures for AP Genco AE revision, focusing on the points that recur in PYQs: the difference between a jig and a fixture, the 3-2-1 location principle, locating and clamping devices, advantages, and basic design rules. The aim is to keep only the study material and arrange it in a simple revision-friendly order.

Quick-Reference Card

Jig vs Fixture

  • A jig holds and locates the workpiece and also guides the cutting tool.
  • A fixture holds and locates the workpiece but does not guide the tool.
  • Jigs are commonly used for drilling, reaming, tapping, and counterboring.
  • Fixtures are commonly used for milling, turning, grinding, shaping, welding, and assembly.
  • Both improve productivity, accuracy, and interchangeability.
  • Both reduce the skill required for repeated production.

Location Principle

  • The most common location method is the 3-2-1 principle.
  • The workpiece is restrained using 3 points on the primary datum, 2 on the secondary datum, and 1 on the tertiary datum.
  • The idea is to locate the workpiece completely and prevent unwanted motion.
  • Locators should be placed as far apart as possible for stability.
  • Redundant location should be avoided.
  • Locating points should preferably be on machined surfaces for accuracy.

Clamping and Design

  • Clamping must not disturb the location already achieved by the locators.
  • Clamping force should oppose the cutting force.
  • Quick-acting clamps such as cam, toggle, pneumatic, and hydraulic types are preferred in mass production.
  • Clamping should be strong enough to resist cutting forces without deforming the workpiece.
  • Fool-proofing means the workpiece can be loaded only in the correct orientation.
  • Jigs and fixtures require rigidity, easy loading and unloading, chip removal, safety, and reasonable cost.

Common Devices

  • Template jig: simple plate with holes, used for marking or light drilling.
  • Plate jig: flat plate with drill bushes; workpiece clamped below.
  • Leaf or latch jig: hinged leaf with bushes for easier loading.
  • Box or tumble jig: box-shaped, useful for drilling on multiple faces.
  • Channel or angle-plate jig: used for angular or channel-shaped work.
  • Milling fixture: mounted on a milling machine table and resists cutting forces.
  • Turning fixture: mounted on a lathe faceplate or chuck for non-cylindrical jobs.
  • Grinding fixture: used for surface or cylindrical grinding.
  • Welding fixture: holds parts in position for welding.
  • Assembly fixture: holds components in accurate position during assembly.

Core Theory

Jig and Fixture Definitions

  • A jig is a work-holding device that locates and clamps the workpiece and guides the cutting tool, often with a drill bush.
  • A fixture only locates and clamps the workpiece; it does not guide the tool.
  • Jigs are mainly associated with operations where the tool must be guided accurately.
  • Fixtures are used where the tool path is already controlled by the machine or process.
  • Modern practice also includes modular fixtures and CNC fixtures for flexibility.

3-2-1 Principle of Location

  • A free body in space has 12 degrees of freedom: 6 linear and 6 rotational.
  • The 3-2-1 principle uses 3 locators on the primary datum plane, 2 on the secondary plane, and 1 on the tertiary plane.
  • The principle is also called the six-point location principle.
  • The arrangement uses three mutually perpendicular planes.
  • The 3-2-1 arrangement is the standard way to locate a workpiece in a fixture.
  • Avoid over-constraint, because redundant location creates error rather than improving it.

Locating Devices

  • A diamond pin is used to accommodate variation in centre distance between two holes.
  • A V-block is used to locate cylindrical jobs.
  • Locators should support the workpiece without causing distortion.
  • Locating and clamping are different functions.

Clamping Principles

  • Clamping force should not move the workpiece away from its located position.
  • Clamping force should resist the cutting force.
  • The minimum number of clamps should be used, provided the work remains stable.
  • Clamp design should allow quick loading and unloading.
  • Strap clamp is a simple and low-cost clamp.
  • Diamond pin is a locator, not a clamp.

Advantages, Cost, and Purpose

  • Jigs and fixtures improve machining accuracy.
  • They improve productivity and speed.
  • They support interchangeability of parts.
  • They reduce marking, measuring, and repeated setting time.
  • They reduce dependence on operator skill.
  • They improve consistency in mass or batch production.
  • They usually involve high initial cost.

Design Considerations

  • The device should be rigid.
  • The workpiece should be easy to load and unload.
  • Chip removal should be easy.
