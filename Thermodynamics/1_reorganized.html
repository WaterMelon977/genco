1. Thermodynamic Systems, Properties & Zeroth Law

A thermodynamic system is a defined region of space or a fixed quantity of matter selected for analysis. Everything outside the system boundary is the surroundings. The classification of a system depends entirely on what crosses its boundary.

System TypeMass Crosses?Energy Crosses?Examples
Closed (Control Mass)No, $\Delta m = 0$Yes ($Q$, $W$)Piston–cylinder (no valves)
Open (Control Volume)YesYesTurbine, nozzle, pump, boiler
IsolatedNoNo, $\Delta E = 0$Universe; ideal thermos flask

Properties are divided into two fundamental classes. Intensive properties are independent of the amount of matter — temperature $T$, pressure $P$, density $\rho$, specific volume $v$, specific enthalpy $h$, viscosity $\mu$. Extensive properties scale with mass — total volume $V$, internal energy $U$, enthalpy $H$, entropy $S$. Dividing any extensive property by mass converts it into the corresponding specific (intensive) quantity.

A property is a point function (state function): its value depends only on the current state, not on how the system arrived there. Consequently, the cyclic integral of any property is zero, $\oint d\phi = 0$. Heat $Q$ and work $W$ are path functions — they depend on the process trajectory and their cyclic integrals are generally non-zero.

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics states: if body A is in thermal equilibrium with body C, and body B is also in thermal equilibrium with body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This transitivity of thermal equilibrium is the physical foundation for temperature as a measurable property, and it underpins the calibration of all thermometers.

Key Points
  • System possesses energy; it does not possess heat or work — those are energy in transit.
  • Specific properties (per unit mass) are always intensive, even when derived from extensive ones.
  • Thermal conductivity $k$ and viscosity $\mu$ are intensive properties.
  • Heat capacity $C = mc$ is extensive; specific heat $c$ is intensive.

2. First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law expresses the conservation of energy. For a closed system undergoing any process, the algebraic sum of all heat interactions and all work interactions equals the change in internal energy of the system.

$$\boxed{\Delta U = Q - W}$$
$\Delta U$ — change in internal energy, kJ $Q$ — net heat added to system, kJ (positive into system) $W$ — net work done by system, kJ (positive out of system)

In differential form: $\delta Q = dU + \delta W$. The use of $\delta$ (inexact differential) for $Q$ and $W$ and $d$ (exact differential) for $U$ encodes the path-function vs. state-function distinction.

Enthalpy

Enthalpy is a composite state property defined to simplify steady-flow and constant-pressure analyses.

$$\boxed{H = U + PV \qquad h = u + Pv}$$
$H$ — total enthalpy, kJ $U$ — internal energy, kJ $P$ — absolute pressure, kPa $V$ — volume, m³

At constant pressure, $Q_P = \Delta H = m C_p \Delta T$, which is why enthalpy is the natural energy variable for open-system equipment such as boilers, condensers, and heat exchangers.

Ideal Gas Internal Energy & Mayer's Relation

For an ideal gas, internal energy is a function of temperature alone — $U = f(T)$ only — because there are no intermolecular potential forces. This gives:

$$\boxed{C_p - C_v = R \qquad C_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1} \qquad C_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1}}$$
$C_p$ — specific heat at constant pressure, kJ/kg·K $C_v$ — specific heat at constant volume, kJ/kg·K $R$ — characteristic gas constant, kJ/kg·K $\gamma = C_p/C_v$ — specific heat ratio (air: $\gamma = 1.4$)

Standard values for air: $C_p = 1.005$ kJ/kg·K, $C_v = 0.718$ kJ/kg·K, $\gamma = 1.40$, $R_{air} = 0.287$ kJ/kg·K; universal $R_u = 8.314$ kJ/kmol·K.

Steady-Flow Energy Equation (SFEE)

$$\boxed{Q - W_s = (h_2 - h_1) + \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2} + g(z_2 - z_1)}$$
$W_s$ — shaft work per unit mass, kJ/kg $h$ — specific enthalpy, kJ/kg $V$ — flow velocity, m/s $g$ — gravitational acceleration, m/s²

For a cyclic process, internal energy returns to its initial value, so $\oint dU = 0$, and consequently $\oint \delta Q = \oint \delta W$ — net heat equals net work over a complete cycle.

Key Points
  • Free expansion: $Q = 0$, $W = 0$, $\Delta U = 0$. For an ideal gas, temperature is unchanged. The process is highly irreversible; entropy increases.
  • Throttling: $Q = 0$, $W = 0$, $\Delta KE \approx 0$; therefore $h_1 = h_2$ — the process is isenthalpic.
  • Both free expansion and throttling are irreversible; both produce $\Delta S > 0$.

3. Work & Heat in Thermodynamic Processes

Boundary work for any quasi-static process is $W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\, dV$. The polytropic relation $PV^n = \text{const}$ is a single framework that encompasses all standard processes by varying the polytropic index $n$.

Isobaric0$P(V_2-V_1)$$mC_p\Delta T$Pressure $P$
Isothermal (ideal gas)1$P_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)$$= W$ (since $\Delta U = 0$)Temperature $T$
Reversible adiabatic$\gamma$$(P_1V_1-P_2V_2)/(\gamma-1)$0Entropy $S$
Isochoric$\infty$0$mC_v\Delta T$Volume $V$
Polytropic$n$$(P_1V_1-P_2V_2)/(n-1)$$W\cdot(\gamma-n)/(\gamma-1)$$PV^n$

Adiabatic Relations (Reversible)

$$\boxed{\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^{\!\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}} = \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\!\gamma-1}}$$
On a $P$–$V$ diagram, the adiabatic curve is steeper than the isothermal curve through the same point because $\gamma > 1$. At constant volume, no boundary work is done regardless of what else happens to the system.

4. Second Law of Thermodynamics

The Second Law establishes the direction of natural processes and places an upper limit on conversion efficiency. It has two classical equivalent statements.

Kelvin–Planck statement: It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle, produces net work, and exchanges heat with only a single thermal reservoir. A heat engine must reject heat to a low-temperature sink — 100 % thermal efficiency is prohibited.

Clausius statement: It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and transfers heat from a cold body to a hot body without the expenditure of external work. Refrigeration always requires work input.

A Perpetual Motion Machine of the Second Kind (PMM-II) is a hypothetical device that absorbs heat from a single reservoir and converts it entirely into work ($\eta = 100\%$). It violates the Kelvin–Planck statement and is impossible.

Carnot Cycle & Carnot Efficiency

The Carnot cycle is the most efficient possible cycle operating between two fixed-temperature reservoirs. It consists of two reversible isothermal processes and two reversible adiabatic processes. The Carnot efficiency depends only on absolute reservoir temperatures.

$$\boxed{\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_L}{T_H} = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_H}}$$
$T_H$ — source (high-temperature) reservoir, K $T_L$ — sink (low-temperature) reservoir, K Temperatures must be in Kelvin

Refrigerator and Heat Pump COP

$$\boxed{\text{COP}_R = \frac{Q_L}{W} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{COP}_{HP} = \frac{Q_H}{W} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L} = \text{COP}_R + 1}$$
$Q_L$ — heat absorbed from cold space, kJ $Q_H$ — heat rejected to hot space, kJ $W$ — net work input, kJ

The heat pump COP is always greater than the refrigerator COP by exactly 1 when operating between the same two temperature limits.

Key Points
  • Carnot efficiency depends only on $T_H$ and $T_L$ in Kelvin — it is independent of the working fluid.
  • All reversible engines operating between the same two reservoirs have identical efficiency (Carnot).
  • An irreversible engine always has lower efficiency than a reversible one between the same reservoirs.
  • $\text{COP}_{HP} = \text{COP}_R + 1$ is an exact identity, not an approximation.

5. Entropy & Clausius Inequality

The Clausius Inequality is derived from the combined treatment of the First and Second Laws for any cyclic process:

$$\boxed{\oint \frac{\delta Q}{T} \leq 0}$$
Equality holds for reversible cycles Strict inequality (< 0) holds for irreversible cycles A value > 0 represents an impossible cycle

For a reversible process, the integrand $\delta Q_{rev}/T$ is an exact differential of a new state function called entropy:

$$\boxed{dS = \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T} \qquad \Delta S = \int_1^2 \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}}$$
$S$ — entropy, kJ/K $T$ — absolute temperature, K

Principle of Entropy Increase

For an isolated system (or for the universe as a whole), entropy never decreases:

$$\boxed{\Delta S_{\text{isolated}} = S_{\text{gen}} \geq 0}$$

Equality holds for reversible processes; strict inequality holds for all real (irreversible) processes. Entropy generation $S_{\text{gen}}$ is always non-negative.

Entropy Change Formulas — Ideal Gas

$$\boxed{\Delta s = C_p \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - R\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = C_v \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + R\ln\frac{v_2}{v_1}}$$
$s$ — specific entropy, kJ/kg·K

For a T–s diagram, the area under a reversible process curve equals the heat transferred: $Q_{\text{rev}} = \int T\, dS$. The Carnot cycle appears as a rectangle on the T–s diagram.

Key Points
  • An isentropic process is both reversible AND adiabatic ($\Delta S = 0$).
  • An irreversible adiabatic process is NOT isentropic — it generates entropy, so $\Delta S > 0$ even though $Q = 0$.
  • Entropy of a pure crystalline substance approaches zero at absolute zero (Third Law, Nernst).

6. Availability (Exergy) & Irreversibility

Availability (also called exergy) is the maximum theoretical useful work obtainable as a system moves reversibly from its given state to the dead state — thermal, mechanical, and chemical equilibrium with the surroundings at $T_0, P_0$.

Closed System Availability

$$\boxed{\Phi = (U - U_0) + P_0(V - V_0) - T_0(S - S_0)}$$
Subscript 0 — dead state properties $T_0$ — dead state temperature (ambient), K $P_0$ — dead state pressure (atmospheric), kPa

Irreversibility (Exergy Destruction)

$$\boxed{I = T_0 \cdot S_{\text{gen}}}$$
$I$ — irreversibility (work lost due to irreversibilities), kJ $S_{\text{gen}}$ — entropy generated, kJ/K
Irreversibility is always non-negative. The greater the entropy generation within a process, the greater the work potential destroyed. Minimising $S_{\text{gen}}$ is equivalent to minimising lost work.

7. Air-Standard Cycles

Air-standard analysis treats the working fluid as a fixed mass of air (ideal gas) and replaces combustion with equivalent heat addition. The three principal reciprocating-engine cycles differ in how heat is added.

Otto Cycle (Petrol/Spark-Ignition Engine)

Heat addition and rejection both occur at constant volume (isochoric).

$$\boxed{\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,\gamma - 1}}}$$
$r = V_1/V_2$ — compression ratio Valid range: $r$ and $\gamma$ must be > 1

Diesel Cycle (Compression-Ignition Engine)

Heat addition at constant pressure (isobaric); heat rejection at constant volume.

$$\boxed{\eta_{\text{Diesel}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,\gamma-1}} \cdot \frac{\rho^{\,\gamma} - 1}{\gamma(\rho - 1)}}$$
$\rho = V_3/V_2$ — cut-off ratio (volume at end of heat addition / volume at start) $\rho > 1$ always

Dual (Mixed / Sabathe) Cycle

Heat addition split: part at constant volume, remainder at constant pressure. Intermediate between Otto and Diesel.

$$\boxed{\eta_{\text{Dual}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,\gamma-1}} \cdot \frac{\alpha\rho^{\,\gamma} - 1}{(\alpha - 1) + \gamma\alpha(\rho - 1)}}$$
$\alpha = P_3/P_2$ — pressure ratio (constant-volume heat addition) $\rho$ — cut-off ratio (constant-pressure heat addition)

Efficiency Comparisons

Comparison BasisRanking
Same compression ratio $r$ and same heat input$\eta_{\text{Otto}} > \eta_{\text{Dual}} > \eta_{\text{Diesel}}$
Same peak pressure and same heat input$\eta_{\text{Diesel}} > \eta_{\text{Dual}} > \eta_{\text{Otto}}$

Brayton Cycle (Gas Turbine)

Two isentropic processes and two isobaric processes.

$$\boxed{\eta_{\text{Brayton}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{\,(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}}$$
$r_p = P_2/P_1$ — pressure ratio
Key Points
  • Otto efficiency depends only on compression ratio $r$ and $\gamma$ — not on heat input.
  • Diesel efficiency increases as cut-off ratio $\rho$ decreases. At $\rho = 1$, Diesel efficiency equals Otto efficiency at the same $r$.
  • Higher compression ratio always improves Otto cycle efficiency, subject to knock constraints in practice.
  • The Stirling and Ericsson cycles also achieve Carnot efficiency but are idealised cycles not used for efficiency comparisons in these exams.

8. Properties of Pure Substances

A pure substance has a uniform and fixed chemical composition throughout. Its phase behaviour is governed by the Gibbs Phase Rule:

$$\boxed{F = C - P + 2}$$
$F$ — degrees of freedom (number of independent intensive variables) $C$ — number of components $P$ — number of phases present The constant 2 accounts for temperature and pressure

For a single-component substance ($C = 1$): at a single phase $F = 2$ (both $T$ and $P$ can vary independently); on a two-phase saturation curve $F = 1$ (specifying $T$ fixes $P_{sat}$); at the triple point $F = 0$ (fixed state).

Dryness Fraction (Quality)

$$\boxed{x = \frac{m_g}{m_g + m_f}}$$
$x$ — dryness fraction (dimensionless, $0 \leq x \leq 1$) $m_g$ — mass of dry saturated vapour, kg $m_f$ — mass of saturated liquid, kg

Wet Mixture Property Interpolation

$$\boxed{h = h_f + x\,h_{fg} \qquad v = v_f + x\,v_{fg} \qquad s = s_f + x\,s_{fg}}$$
Subscript $f$ — saturated liquid value Subscript $g$ — saturated vapour value Subscript $fg = g - f$

Key Fixed Points for Water

PointTemperaturePressureSignificance
Triple point$0.01\,^\circ\text{C}$ (273.16 K)0.611 kPaSolid, liquid, vapour coexist; $F = 0$
Critical point$374.14\,^\circ\text{C}$22.06 MPa$h_{fg} = 0$; liquid and vapour become indistinguishable

Mollier Diagram Isobaric Slope

$$\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial s}\right)_P = T}$$

The slope of a constant-pressure line on the $h$–$s$ (Mollier) diagram equals absolute temperature $T$. Derived from $T\,ds = dh - v\,dP$, setting $dP = 0$.

Clausius–Clapeyron Equation

$$\boxed{\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{h_{fg}}{T(v_g - v_f)}}$$
Gives slope of saturation curve on $P$–$T$ diagram $h_{fg}$ — latent heat, kJ/kg
Key Points
  • At the critical point, latent heat of vaporisation $h_{fg}$ is zero — not maximum.
  • Above the critical pressure, a substance cannot be liquefied by isothermal compression.
  • $x = 0$ defines saturated liquid; $x = 1$ defines dry saturated vapour; $x > 1$ is superheated vapour.
  • At saturation, specifying either $T$ or $P$ fixes the other (they are not independent).

9. Real Gases — van der Waals & Joule–Thomson Effect

Compressibility Factor

$$\boxed{Z = \frac{Pv}{RT}}$$
$Z$ — compressibility factor (dimensionless) For ideal gas, $Z = 1$ always

van der Waals Equation of State

$$\boxed{\left(P + \frac{a}{v^2}\right)(v - b) = RT}$$
$a/v^2$ — pressure correction for intermolecular attractions $b$ — volume correction for finite molecular size

At the critical point, the van der Waals isotherm has an inflection point. The critical compressibility factor works out to:

$$\boxed{Z_c = \frac{P_c v_c}{R T_c} = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375}$$

Joule–Thomson Effect

When a real gas undergoes isenthalpic throttling (constant enthalpy), the temperature change is characterised by the Joule–Thomson coefficient:

$$\boxed{\mu_{JT} = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_h}$$
$\mu_{JT} > 0$ — gas cools upon throttling (pressure drop) $\mu_{JT} < 0$ — gas warms upon throttling (H₂ and He at room temperature) $\mu_{JT} = 0$ — inversion temperature; also true for ideal gas
For an ideal gas, $h = f(T)$ only. Since throttling holds $h$ constant, temperature must remain constant: $\mu_{JT} = 0$ for an ideal gas. The Joule–Thomson effect is a real-gas phenomenon arising from intermolecular forces.

10. Thermodynamic Potentials & Maxwell Relations

Four thermodynamic potentials arise from Legendre transforms of the fundamental relation $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV$.

PotentialDefinitionNatural VariablesDifferential
Internal Energy $U$—$S, V$$dU = T\,dS - P\,dV$
Enthalpy $H$$U + PV$$S, P$$dH = T\,dS + V\,dP$
Helmholtz Energy $F$$U - TS$$T, V$$dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV$
Gibbs Energy $G$$H - TS$$T, P$$dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$

Maxwell Relations

Applying the condition for exact differentials to each potential yields four Maxwell relations:

$$\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V \qquad \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P$$ $$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V \qquad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$$

The most practically useful Maxwell relation for calculations is $\left(\partial S/\partial V\right)_T = \left(\partial P/\partial T\right)_V$, which connects entropy to measurable $P$–$V$–$T$ data.

Quick-Fire Checklist
  • $\Delta U = Q - W$ with sign: heat in positive, work out positive.
  • $C_p - C_v = R$; for air: $C_p = 1.005$, $C_v = 0.718$, $\gamma = 1.4$.
  • $\eta_\text{Carnot} = 1 - T_L/T_H$; temperatures always in Kelvin.
  • $\text{COP}_{HP} = \text{COP}_R + 1$.
  • $\oint \delta Q/T \leq 0$; $S_\text{gen} \geq 0$; $I = T_0 S_\text{gen}$.
  • $\eta_\text{Otto} = 1 - r^{1-\gamma}$; same $r$ $\Rightarrow$ Otto > Diesel.
  • Triple point water: $0.01\,^\circ\text{C}$, 0.611 kPa.
  • Critical point water: $374.14\,^\circ\text{C}$, 22.06 MPa, $h_{fg} = 0$.
  • $F = C - P + 2$ (Gibbs Phase Rule).
  • van der Waals $Z_c = 0.375$.
  • Joule–Thomson: $\mu_{JT} = (\partial T/\partial P)_h$; ideal gas $\mu_{JT} = 0$.
  • Helmholtz $F = U - TS$; Gibbs $G = H - TS$.
  • Isobaric slope on Mollier chart: $(\partial h/\partial s)_P = T$.
