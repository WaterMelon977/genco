1. Units of Refrigeration & Capacity

The standard unit of refrigerating capacity is the Tonne of Refrigeration (TR), historically defined as the rate of heat removal required to freeze one tonne (1000 kg) of water at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ into ice at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ in 24 hours.

$$\boxed{1\,\text{TR} = 3.5\,\text{kW} = 210\,\text{kJ/min} = 12600\,\text{kJ/h}}$$

The theoretical derivation using the latent heat of fusion of ice (335 kJ/kg) gives a slightly different value:

$$1\,\text{TR}_\text{theoretical} = \frac{1000 \times 335}{24 \times 60} \approx 232.6\,\text{kJ/min} \approx 3.516\,\text{kW}$$

In practice, the rounded value of $1\,\text{TR} = 3.5\,\text{kW}$ is used in all numerical calculations.

Power from Capacity and COP

$$\boxed{W = \frac{Q_R}{\text{COP}_R}}$$
$W$ — compressor power input, kW $Q_R$ — refrigerating capacity, kW

2. Reversed Carnot Cycle — Ideal COP

The reversed Carnot cycle is the theoretical upper limit for any refrigerating system operating between two fixed temperature reservoirs. It consists of two reversible isothermal processes (heat absorption and heat rejection) and two reversible adiabatic (isentropic) processes. The Carnot COP depends only on the absolute temperatures of the reservoirs.

$$\boxed{\text{COP}_R = \frac{Q_L}{W} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L}}$$
$Q_L$ — heat absorbed at the cold reservoir (refrigerating effect), kJ $W$ — net work input, kJ $T_L$ — cold reservoir (evaporator) temperature, K $T_H$ — hot reservoir (condenser) temperature, K Temperatures MUST be in Kelvin
$$\boxed{\text{COP}_{HP} = \frac{Q_H}{W} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L} = \text{COP}_R + 1}$$

The heat pump COP is always greater than the refrigerator COP by exactly 1. Both increase as the temperature difference $(T_H - T_L)$ decreases.

3. Air Refrigeration — Bell-Coleman (Reversed Brayton) Cycle

The Bell-Coleman cycle is a reversed Brayton cycle operating entirely in the gas phase — there is no phase change of the working fluid. It consists of two isentropic processes (compression and expansion) and two constant-pressure heat transfer processes. The working fluid is air throughout.

The cycle is used primarily in aircraft cabin cooling because air is freely available, the system is lightweight per unit of refrigerating capacity, and there is no risk of toxic refrigerant leaks in the cabin.

$$\boxed{\text{COP}_\text{air} = \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - 1}}$$
$r_p = P_\text{high}/P_\text{low}$ — pressure ratio $\gamma$ — specific heat ratio of air = 1.4 Valid for the ideal Bell-Coleman cycle
As pressure ratio $r_p$ increases, COP decreases. This is the opposite behaviour from a vapour compression system, where higher pressure ratio (at given temperatures) does not directly reduce COP in the same way.

4. Vapour Compression Refrigeration System (VCRS)

The VCRS is the standard refrigeration system for nearly all commercial and domestic applications. It exploits the large latent heat of vaporisation of refrigerants. The cycle consists of four processes:

ProcessStatesTypeDevice
Compression1 → 2Isentropic (ideal)Compressor
Condensation2 → 3Isobaric heat rejectionCondenser
Expansion3 → 4Isenthalpic throttling ($h_3 = h_4$)Expansion valve / capillary tube
Evaporation4 → 1Isobaric heat absorptionEvaporator

VCRS Performance Formulas

$$\boxed{\text{COP}_R = \frac{h_1 - h_4}{h_2 - h_1}}$$
$h_1$ — specific enthalpy at evaporator exit (compressor inlet), kJ/kg $h_2$ — specific enthalpy at compressor exit (condenser inlet), kJ/kg $h_3 = h_4$ — specific enthalpy at condenser exit = expansion valve exit (isenthalpic)
$$\text{Refrigerating Effect (RE)} = h_1 - h_4 \quad \text{kJ/kg}$$
$$\text{Compressor Work} = h_2 - h_1 \quad \text{kJ/kg}$$

Heat Rejection Ratio (HRR)

$$\boxed{\text{HRR} = \frac{Q_\text{condenser}}{Q_\text{evaporator}} = 1 + \frac{1}{\text{COP}_R}}$$

Sub-Cooling (Under-Cooling)

When the liquid refrigerant leaving the condenser is cooled below its saturation temperature before entering the expansion valve, the specific enthalpy at state 4 ($h_4'$) is lower than the standard value $h_4$. This increases the refrigerating effect without changing compressor work (compression process is unaffected), improving COP.

$$\text{RE}_\text{sub-cooled} = h_1 - h_4' > h_1 - h_4$$

The Ozone Depletion Potential (ODP) of a refrigerant is a dimensionless index measuring its ability to destroy stratospheric ozone relative to the baseline refrigerant R-11, which is assigned ODP = 1.0.

Desirable Properties of a Refrigerant

  • High latent heat of vaporisation — minimises refrigerant mass flow rate per unit of capacity.
  • Low freezing point — prevents solidification in the evaporator.
  • Positive evaporator pressure — prevents air and moisture ingress when the evaporator operates below atmospheric pressure.
  • High critical temperature — permits condensation at ambient temperatures.
  • Non-toxic, non-flammable, chemically stable, and compatible with system materials.

6. System Components & Multi-Stage Systems

Expansion Devices

The expansion device drops refrigerant from high-side pressure to low-side pressure through isenthalpic throttling ($h_3 = h_4$).

Capillary tube: A long, narrow copper tube that provides throttling through friction and momentum effects. It is a fixed-restriction device with no moving parts, capable of no capacity modulation. Used in small hermetic systems such as domestic refrigerators, water coolers, and window air conditioners. The system must be precisely charged to function correctly.

Thermostatic Expansion Valve (TEV): A variable-area valve controlled by the pressure and temperature at the evaporator exit. It regulates refrigerant flow to maintain a constant degree of superheat at the evaporator outlet, ensuring the compressor always receives dry vapour. Used in larger, variable-load systems.

Accumulator

An accumulator is placed on the compressor suction line downstream of a flooded evaporator. It traps liquid refrigerant droplets entrained in the suction vapour, preventing liquid slugging and damage to the compressor (liquid hammer). It is essential in flooded evaporator systems.

Cascade Refrigeration System

A cascade system uses two (or more) separate refrigerant loops, each operating with a different refrigerant suited to its temperature range. The low-temperature cycle rejects heat to the high-temperature cycle via a cascade condenser/evaporator heat exchanger. Used to reach very low temperatures ($-40\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $-80\,^\circ\text{C}$) where a single cycle would require impractically high pressure ratios.

$$\boxed{T_\text{opt} = \sqrt{T_H \cdot T_L}}$$
$T_\text{opt}$ — optimum intermediate temperature for maximum combined COP, K $T_H$ — high-side condensing temperature, K $T_L$ — low-side evaporating temperature, K Geometric mean must be computed in Kelvin

7. Vapour Absorption Refrigeration System (VARS)

In a VARS, the mechanical compressor of a VCRS is replaced by a thermal compressor consisting of three components: an Absorber, a Liquid Pump, and a Generator. The system is driven by low-grade thermal energy (waste heat, steam, or solar heat) rather than shaft work. This makes it attractive wherever waste heat is available or electricity is expensive.

The condenser, expansion valve, and evaporator are identical in function to those in a VCRS. What changes is how the low-pressure refrigerant vapour from the evaporator is re-compressed to high-side pressure.

In the absorber, refrigerant vapour is absorbed into a strong solution of absorbent. The resulting weak solution (refrigerant-rich) is pumped at high pressure by the liquid pump (small work input) to the generator. In the generator, heat is applied to drive off the refrigerant vapour, which then flows to the condenser at high pressure. The strong (refrigerant-poor) solution returns to the absorber.

Energy Balance

$$\boxed{Q_G + Q_E = Q_C + Q_A}$$
$Q_G$ — heat input to generator (driving energy), kW $Q_E$ — heat absorbed in evaporator (refrigerating effect), kW $Q_C$ — heat rejected in condenser, kW $Q_A$ — heat rejected in absorber, kW Liquid pump work $W_P$ is negligible and usually ignored
Heat is rejected at TWO places in a VARS: the condenser and the absorber. The combined rejection is $Q_C + Q_A$, not just $Q_C$.

VARS Practical COP

$$\boxed{\text{COP}_\text{VARS} = \frac{Q_E}{Q_G}}$$

VARS Ideal Carnot COP

$$\boxed{\text{COP}_\text{ideal} = \left(\frac{T_E}{T_G - T_E}\right) \times \left(\frac{T_G - T_O}{T_O}\right)}$$
$T_E$ — evaporator temperature, K $T_G$ — generator temperature, K $T_O$ — ambient sink temperature, K

This is the product of the Carnot refrigerator COP $(T_E/(T_G - T_E))$ and the Carnot engine efficiency $((T_G - T_O)/T_O)$, reflecting that the system is a combination of a heat engine (generator side) driving a refrigerator (evaporator side).

Electrolux Three-Fluid System

The Electrolux system uses three fluids: ammonia (refrigerant), water (absorbent), and hydrogen gas (inert carrier gas). Hydrogen gas reduces the ammonia partial pressure in the evaporator, allowing evaporation at low temperature while the total pressure in the system remains uniform. A bubble pump (no mechanical moving parts) drives circulation using heat alone. This makes the system completely silent and maintenance-free — used in hotel room refrigerators and caravan cooling.

Quick-Fire Checklist
  • $1\,\text{TR} = 3.5\,\text{kW} = 210\,\text{kJ/min}$.
  • Carnot $\text{COP}_R = T_L/(T_H - T_L)$; temperatures in Kelvin.
  • $\text{COP}_{HP} = \text{COP}_R + 1$.
  • Bell-Coleman (air cycle): $\text{COP} = 1/(r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - 1)$; COP decreases as $r_p$ increases.
  • Aircraft cooling $\Rightarrow$ air refrigeration cycle (low weight per TR).
  • VCRS COP $= (h_1 - h_4)/(h_2 - h_1)$; throttling: $h_3 = h_4$.
  • $\text{HRR} = 1 + 1/\text{COP}_R$.
  • Sub-cooling: RE increases, COP increases, compressor work unchanged.
  • $T_e \uparrow \Rightarrow$ COP increases; $T_c \uparrow \Rightarrow$ COP decreases.
  • Inorganic: $R\text{-}(700 + \text{MW})$; Organic: $R\text{-}(C-1)(H+1)F$.
  • R-717 = NH₃; R-718 = H₂O; R-744 = CO₂; R-729 = Air.
  • ODP baseline: R-11 = 1.0; R-134a ODP = 0.
  • Halide torch $\Rightarrow$ Freon (bright green flame); Sulphur stick $\Rightarrow$ Ammonia (white smoke).
  • Ammonia requires steel piping — corrodes copper and brass.
  • Capillary tube: domestic/small systems, no capacity control.
  • TEV: maintains constant superheat at evaporator exit.
  • Accumulator: prevents liquid entering compressor in flooded systems.
  • Cascade $T_\text{opt} = \sqrt{T_H \cdot T_L}$ in Kelvin.
  • VARS energy balance: $Q_G + Q_E = Q_C + Q_A$.
  • VARS COP $= Q_E/Q_G$; heat rejected at both condenser and absorber.
  • Aqua-ammonia: NH₃ refrigerant, H₂O absorbent, needs rectifier.
  • LiBr–water: H₂O refrigerant, LiBr absorbent, above $0\,^\circ$C only.
  • Electrolux: three fluids (NH₃, H₂O, H₂), no moving parts.
