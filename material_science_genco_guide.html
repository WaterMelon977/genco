Material Science & Metallurgy Guide

AP GenCo Exam — Mechanical Engineering Comprehensive Study Notes

Overview

This guide covers the official AP GenCo syllabus for Material Science and Metallurgy in the most exam-relevant format. Content is structured around high-frequency MCQ patterns from SSC JE, AE, and state mechanical exams. Each section provides core concepts, key definitions, comparison tables, and quick revision points.

Chapter 1: Structure and Properties of Engineering Materials

1.1 Mechanical Properties – Definitions and Relations

Elasticity
• Definition: Ability to regain original shape/size after load removal
• Key Characteristic: Measured by Young's modulus (E); higher E = more elastic
• Example: Steel: high E; Rubber: low E but large recoverable strain
Stiffness
• Definition: Resistance to elastic deformation
• Key Characteristic: Related to modulus of elasticity
• Example: Higher E = stiffer material
Ductility
• Definition: Ability to undergo large permanent plastic deformation in tension
• Key Characteristic: Draw into wires; measured by % elongation
• Example: Mild steel (most ductile common metal)
Malleability
• Definition: Ability to be hammered/rolled into thin sheets without fracture
• Key Characteristic: Compression-based plasticity
• Example: Lead (maximum malleability); aluminum
Toughness
• Definition: Total energy absorbed from start of loading to fracture
• Key Characteristic: Area under entire stress-strain curve
• Example: High toughness = strength + ductility combined
Resilience
• Definition: Elastic energy stored and recovered on unloading
• Key Characteristic: Area under stress-strain curve up to elastic limit
• Example: Lower than toughness; reversible deformation only
Hardness
• Definition: Resistance to plastic deformation by indentation/scratching
• Key Characteristic: Not a strength measure; related to yield strength
• Example: Cast iron, hardened steel
Brittleness
• Definition: Little/no plastic deformation before sudden fracture
• Key Characteristic: Opposite of ductility
• Example: Glass, cast iron, ceramics
Creep
• Definition: Slow, time-dependent permanent deformation under constant load
• Key Characteristic: Requires both constant load and elevated temperature
• Example: Common in turbine blades, boilers at high temp
Fatigue
• Definition: Failure under repeated or reversed cyclic stresses
• Key Characteristic: Occurs below yield stress; represented by S-N curve
• Example: Rotating shafts, springs, bridges
Damping Capacity
• Definition: Ability to absorb vibration energy
• Key Characteristic: High in grey cast iron due to graphite flakes
• Example: Machinery foundations
Hot Hardness
• Definition: Ability to retain hardness/strength at elevated temperatures
• Key Characteristic: Critical for cutting tools
• Example: High-speed steel, tungsten carbide

1.2 Material Behavior Classification

Isotropic vs Anisotropic

  • Isotropic: Same elastic properties in all directions at a point (most metals when fine-grained; most common in exams)
  • Anisotropic: Properties vary with direction (wood, single crystals, cold-worked metals)

Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous

  • Homogeneous: Properties same at every point in a given direction (ideal assumption)
  • Heterogeneous: Properties vary from point to point (real materials due to grain structure, segregation)

Allotropic/Polymorphic Materials

  • Material exists in different crystal structures at different temperatures
  • Example: Iron — α-iron (BCC) ↔ γ-iron (FCC) ↔ δ-iron (BCC)
  • Critical for understanding heat treatment behavior

1.3 Stress-Strain Curve Interpretation

  • Elastic region: Linear, reversible deformation; Young's modulus = stress/strain
  • Plastic region: Non-linear, permanent deformation; strain hardening occurs
  • Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS): Maximum stress material can sustain
  • Yield point: Stress at which material transitions from elastic to plastic
  • Fracture point: Final breaking of material
💡 Key Concept
:
  • Toughness = area under entire curve to fracture (strength + ductility)
  • Resilience = area under curve up to elastic limit (elastic energy only)
  • Brittleness = sudden fracture with minimal plastic zone (glass, cast iron)

1.4 Viscoelasticity

  • Material exhibits both viscous (time-dependent) and elastic behavior simultaneously
  • Example: Polymers, some composites
  • Load → deformation increases with time (like viscous fluid) but also partially recovers (elastic)
  • Important for creep-resistant applications

Chapter 2: Bonding in Solids

2.1 Types of Atomic Bonding

Metallic
• Mechanism: Valence electrons form "electron sea"; positive ion cores surrounded by mobile electrons
• Characteristics: Strong, conducts electricity, ductile, malleable, high melting point
• Examples: All metals (Fe, Cu, Al, Zn)
Covalent
• Mechanism: Electrons shared between adjacent atoms in fixed positions
• Characteristics: Directional; very hard and brittle; low electrical conductivity; high melting point
• Examples: Diamond, Si, SiC, WC
Ionic
• Mechanism: Complete electron transfer from one atom to another; electrostatic attraction
• Characteristics: Brittle; high melting point; conducts in molten state; soluble in polar solvents
• Examples: NaCl, MgO, Al₂O₃
Van der Waals
• Mechanism: Weak attraction between atoms/molecules due to induced dipoles
• Characteristics: Weak; low melting point; easy to deform
• Examples: Graphite (between layers), noble gases
Hydrogen
• Mechanism: Partial covalent/electrostatic bond involving hydrogen
• Characteristics: Moderate strength; important in polymers and biology
• Examples: Polymers, water molecules

2.2 Relationship Between Bonding and Properties

  • Strong metallic bonding → high melting point, high electrical/thermal conductivity, ductility
  • Covalent bonding → extreme hardness, brittleness (e.g., diamond, WC cutting tools)
  • Ionic bonding → hard but brittle, high melting point, insulators in solid state
  • Weak van der Waals → low melting point, easy to deform
🎯 Exam Focus
: Metallic bonding dominates metals used in engineering; covalent bonds produce the hardest materials (WC, diamond, SiC).

Chapter 3: Crystal Structures and Imperfections

3.1 Crystalline vs Amorphous Materials

  • Crystalline: Atoms arranged in regular, repeating 3D lattice with long-range order
  • Amorphous: Atoms arranged chaotically with no long-range order
  • Most metals are crystalline; glasses and some polymers are amorphous

3.2 Crystal Lattice Types (Most Common in Metals)

Simple Cubic (SC)
• Atoms/Cell: 1
• Coordination Number (CN): 6
• Atomic Packing Factor (APF): 0.52
• Examples: Rarely occurs in pure metals
• Notes: Least dense
Body-Centred Cubic (BCC)
• Atoms/Cell: 2
• Coordination Number (CN): 8
• Atomic Packing Factor (APF): 0.68
• Examples: α-iron (ferrite), Cr, Mo, W
• Notes: 1 atom at corners + 1 at center
Face-Centred Cubic (FCC)
• Atoms/Cell: 4
• Coordination Number (CN): 12
• Atomic Packing Factor (APF): 0.74
• Examples: γ-iron (austenite), Cu, Al, Ni, Au
• Notes: 1 atom at corners + 1 on each face
Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP)
• Atoms/Cell: 6
• Coordination Number (CN): 12
• Atomic Packing Factor (APF): 0.74
• Examples: Zn, Mg, Ti, Co
• Notes: Two layers in different arrangement
📐 Key Formulas
:
  • Atoms per unit cell: Count corner atoms (÷8) + edge atoms (÷4) + face atoms (÷2) + interior atoms (×1)
  • APF = (Volume of atoms in cell) / (Volume of unit cell)
  • Highest packing: FCC and HCP both have APF = 0.74
⚠️ Exam Trap
:
  • BCC has 2 atoms per cell, not 8 or 9
  • FCC has 4 atoms per cell
  • Both FCC and HCP have same APF (0.74), but different stacking arrangements

3.3 Point Defects (Imperfections in Single Atoms)

Vacancy
• Description: Missing atom in lattice position
• Formation: Thermal activation; created during cooling
• Effect: Decreases density; increases diffusion rate; important in creep
• Example: Forms at grain boundaries
Interstitial
• Description: Extra atom occupying gap between lattice atoms
• Formation: Forced insertion (plastic deformation, irradiation)
• Effect: Increases hardness; local strain distortion
• Example: Carbon atoms in iron (hardening)
Frenkel Defect
• Description: Ion displaced from normal site to interstitial position
• Formation: Common in ionic crystals; thermal activation
• Effect: Creates vacancy-interstitial pair
• Example: AgBr, AgCl
Schottky Defect
• Description: Pair of missing ions (anion + cation) in ionic crystals
• Formation: Thermal activation
• Effect: Maintains electrical neutrality in ionic solids
• Example: NaCl, MgO
💡 Concept
:
  • Interstitials cause hardening and strengthening (e.g., carbon in steel)
  • Vacancies cause softening and enable diffusion (important in heat treatment)

3.4 Line Defects (Dislocations)

Edge Dislocation
• Symbol: ⊥ (perpendicular symbol)
• Direction: Perpendicular to slip plane
• Burgers Vector: Perpendicular to dislocation line
• Effect: Causes plastic deformation; stress concentration
Screw Dislocation
• Symbol: / (slash)
• Direction: Parallel to slip plane
• Burgers Vector: Parallel to dislocation line
• Effect: Also causes slip; helical atomic arrangement
💡 Key Concept
:
  • Burgers vector = magnitude and direction of deformation caused by dislocation
  • Vector sum at any node = 0 (equilibrium condition in dislocation networks)
  • Dislocations enable plastic deformation in metals; they move when stress applied
🎯 Exam Focus
: Dislocations are the primary mechanism for plastic deformation in metals. More dislocations → more work hardening → harder but less ductile.

3.5 Grain Structure

  • Metal consists of many crystalline regions (grains) with different orientations
  • Grain boundary = interface between grains; higher energy than bulk
  • Finer grains → higher yield strength, more toughness (Hall-Petch relation: σ = σ₀ + k·d⁻⁰·⁵)
  • Coarser grains → lower strength, more brittle

Chapter 4: Structure of Alloys and Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram

4.1 Solid Solutions

Substitutional Solid Solution

  • Solute atoms replace solvent atoms in lattice positions
  • Limited by Hume-Rothery rules: size difference <15%, same crystal structure, similar electronegativity
  • Example: Cu-Ni system (complete solid solubility)

Interstitial Solid Solution

  • Solute atoms occupy gaps between solvent atoms
  • Only possible for small solute atoms (C, N, H in metals)
  • Example: Carbon in iron (austenite, martensite)
💡 Key Property
: Solid solutions generally stronger and harder than pure metals due to lattice distortion by solute atoms.

4.2 Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram (0–6.67% C at 1 atm)

Critical Temperatures and Phases

727°C
• Carbon %: 0.8%
• Reaction: Austenite (γ-iron) → Solid products
• Products: Ferrite (α-Fe) + Cementite (Fe₃C)
• Key Name: Eutectoid
1147°C
• Carbon %: 4.3%
• Reaction: Liquid → Solid products
• Products: Austenite + Cementite
• Key Name: Eutectic
1538°C
• Carbon %: 0%
• Reaction: Pure iron melting point
• Products: Liquid ↔ δ-iron
• Key Name: —

Critical Temperatures (for pure iron):

  • A₄: 1538°C — δ-iron (BCC) ↔ Liquid
  • A₃: 1394°C — γ-iron (FCC) ↔ δ-iron (BCC)
  • A₂: 912°C — α-iron (BCC) ↔ γ-iron (FCC)
  • Curie point: 768°C — Ferromagnetic ↔ Paramagnetic (α-iron loses magnetism)

Main Phases in Iron-Carbon System

Ferrite (α-iron)
• Formula/Composition: Pure Fe or ~0.02% C
• Crystal Structure: BCC
• Properties: Soft, ductile, magnetic, low carbon solubility
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: 0.02%
Austenite (γ-iron)
• Formula/Composition: Fe with ~2.1% C max at 1147°C
• Crystal Structure: FCC
• Properties: Ductile (at high temp), highest carbon solubility, non-magnetic above 768°C
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: 2.1%
Cementite (Fe₃C)
• Formula/Composition: Iron carbide, 6.67% C
• Crystal Structure: Orthorhombic
• Properties: Hard, brittle, non-magnetic
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: Fixed 6.67%
Martensite
• Formula/Composition: Supersaturated C in BCC α-Fe
• Crystal Structure: Tetragonal (distorted BCC)
• Properties: Very hard, very brittle, needle-like, formed by rapid quenching
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: Up to 2% C retained
Pearlite
• Formula/Composition: Ferrite + Cementite lamellae
• Crystal Structure: Mixture
• Properties: Medium hardness, medium strength, ductile-brittle transition
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: Microstructure, not phase
Bainite
• Formula/Composition: Ferrite + fine cementite
• Crystal Structure: Mixture
• Properties: Intermediate hardness/strength, better toughness than martensite
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: Microstructure, not phase
Ledeburite
• Formula/Composition: Austenite + Cementite at 1147°C
• Crystal Structure: Eutectic mixture
• Properties: Eutectic product (cast iron structure)
• Max Carbon at Room Temp: —

Key Reactions

  1. Eutectoid (727°C, 0.8% C):
    γ-iron (austenite, 0.8%C) → α-iron (ferrite, 0.02%C) + Fe₃C (cementite, 6.67%C)
    Produces pearlite (lamellar structure)

  2. Eutectic (1147°C, 4.3% C):
    Liquid (4.3%C) → γ-iron (2.1%C) + Fe₃C (6.67%C)
    Produces ledeburite; relevant for cast irons

  3. Retained Austenite:
    Untransformed austenite after quenching; occurs in hypereutectoid steels when martensite finish temp (Mf) is below room temperature.

Lever Rule (Phase Fractions)

When two phases coexist, use:

W_α = (C_γ − C₀) / (C_γ − C_α) × 100%
where C₀ = actual composition, C_α and C_γ = phase compositions at that temperature

Example: At 700°C with 0.5% C:

  • Ferrite (α) has ~0.02% C
  • Austenite (γ) has ~0.8% C
  • W_ferrite = (0.8 − 0.5)/(0.8 − 0.02) × 100% ≈ 38%

4.3 Steel Classification

Hypoeutectoid Steel (< 0.8% C):

  • Microstructure: Ferrite + Pearlite
  • Softer, more ductile, easier to machine

Eutectoid Steel (= 0.8% C):

  • Microstructure: 100% Pearlite
  • Reference point; balanced hardness and ductility

Hypereutectoid Steel (0.8% – 2.1% C):

  • Microstructure: Cementite (primary) + Pearlite
  • Harder, more brittle, prone to retained austenite after quenching

Cast Iron (2.1% – 6.67% C):

  • Contains eutectic/eutectoid mixtures
  • Ledeburite present in as-cast structure
  • Gray cast iron (graphite flakes); white cast iron (cementite); ductile iron (graphite nodules)

Chapter 5: Heat Treatment Processes

5.1 Overview Table

Annealing
• Heating Temperature: Above A₃ (critical), then cool slowly
• Cooling Rate: Furnace cool (slowest)
• Microstructure Produced: Coarse pearlite + ferrite
• Properties Achieved: Soft, ductile, stress-relieved
• Purpose: Softening; stress relief; grain refinement
Normalizing
• Heating Temperature: 30–50°C above A₃, air cool
• Cooling Rate: Air cool (faster)
• Microstructure Produced: Fine pearlite + ferrite
• Properties Achieved: Improved strength/toughness, refined grain
• Purpose: Refining; stress removal; mechanical property improvement
Hardening
• Heating Temperature: Above A₃ (austenite range), quench rapidly
• Cooling Rate: Quench: brine > water > oil > air
• Microstructure Produced: Martensite (+ retained austenite if fast)
• Properties Achieved: Hard, brittle
• Purpose: Maximum hardness for cutting tools, dies
Tempering
• Heating Temperature: 150–650°C (below A₁)
• Cooling Rate: Air cool
• Microstructure Produced: Tempered martensite (sorbite, troostite)
• Properties Achieved: Reduced brittleness, improved toughness
• Purpose: Removing quenching stresses; practical hardness
Spheroidizing
• Heating Temperature: Just below A₁ (650°C), prolonged soak
• Cooling Rate: Slow cool
• Microstructure Produced: Cementite spheroids in ferrite matrix
• Properties Achieved: Maximum machinability
• Purpose: Best for machining hypereutectoid steels
Austempering
• Heating Temperature: Quench to 250–450°C, hold isothermally
• Cooling Rate: Slow
• Microstructure Produced: 100% Bainite
• Properties Achieved: Good strength + toughness, low distortion
• Purpose: Producing bainite with minimum cracking risk
Martempering
• Heating Temperature: Quench to just above Ms, hold, then air cool
• Cooling Rate: Controlled
• Microstructure Produced: Martensite (with reduced residual stress)
• Properties Achieved: Hard but less cracking than direct quench
• Purpose: Reducing thermal shock and cracking
📌 Quenching Media
:
Brine (fastest) > Water > Oil > Air (slowest)

5.2 Key Temperatures in Steel

  • A₃ (upper critical): Temperature above which all ferrite transforms to austenite (hypoeutectoid steels)
  • A₂ (lower critical, eutectoid): 727°C — austenite completely transforms to pearlite (eutectoid composition)
  • A₁ (Curie point region): ~768°C — ferromagnetism lost in ferrite
  • Ms (martensite start): Temperature at which martensite formation begins on cooling
  • Mf (martensite finish): Temperature at which martensite formation completes

5.3 Microstructures Produced

Pearlite:

  • Lamellar (striped) mixture of ferrite and cementite
  • Forms from eutectoid decomposition at 727°C
  • Coarse pearlite (slow cooling) → softer; fine pearlite (faster cooling) → harder

Martensite:

  • Supersaturated carbon in distorted BCC iron
  • Formed by rapid quenching (diffusionless shear transformation)
  • Needle-like appearance; very hard; very brittle; metastable phase

Bainite:

  • Ferrite + fine cementite particles (not lamellar)
  • Forms at intermediate temperatures (250–450°C in TTT diagram)
  • Better toughness than martensite; less ductile than pearlite

Sorbite/Troostite:

  • Fine pearlite-like structures resulting from tempering martensite or air cooling
  • Intermediate hardness and toughness

5.4 TTT (Time-Temperature-Transformation) Diagram

  • S-curve shape: Shows start and finish times for pearlite, bainite, martensite formation at constant temperature
  • Nose of curve: Fastest transformation point (highest reaction rate)
  • Critical cooling rate: Minimum cooling speed needed to avoid pearlite and form martensite
  • Steeper steel composition (higher C, alloy content) → lower critical cooling rate (easier to harden)
💡 Concept
:
To form martensite, cooling must be fast enough to "bypass" the pearlite nose region.

Chapter 6: Alloy Steels

6.1 Purpose of Alloying Elements

Alloying elements modify:

  • Hardness: Increase by forming harder phases or solid-solution strengthening
  • Strength: Increase via precipitation hardening or solid-solution strengthening
  • Toughness: Improve by refining grain, reducing brittleness
  • Hardenability: Lower critical cooling rate (deeper hardening into section)
  • Wear Resistance: Increase by forming hard carbides
  • Corrosion Resistance: Improve by forming protective oxide layers
  • Temperature Resistance: Extend temperature range for useful strength (high-speed steel, tool steels)

6.2 Common Alloying Elements and Effects

Carbon (C)
• Effect on Steel: Increases hardness, strength; decreases ductility; critical to all steels
• Purpose: Primary hardening element
• Examples: All steels contain C; 0.2–2.1% range
Manganese (Mn)
• Effect on Steel: Increases hardness, strength; improves hardenability; reduces brittleness
• Purpose: Deoxidizer; strengthener
• Examples: 0.5–1.5% in plain carbon; up to 12% in work-hardening steels
Chromium (Cr)
• Effect on Steel: Increases hardness, wear resistance; improves corrosion resistance; forms hard carbides
• Purpose: Hardening, corrosion resistance; carbide former
• Examples: Stainless steels (11–18%); tool steels (5–12%)
Molybdenum (Mo)
• Effect on Steel: Increases strength at high temperature; improves hardenability; refines grain
• Purpose: High-temperature strength; deep hardening
• Examples: High-speed steel, high-strength steels (0.3–0.8%)
Vanadium (V)
• Effect on Steel: Increases hardness, toughness, wear resistance; forms very hard carbides
• Purpose: Wear resistance; toughness at high hardness
• Examples: Tool steels (1–5%); high-speed steel
Nickel (Ni)
• Effect on Steel: Increases toughness, strength; improves low-temperature impact strength; improves hardenability
• Purpose: Toughness; deep hardening; low-temp toughness
• Examples: Nickel steels (2–5%); austenitic stainless (8–12%)
Silicon (Si)
• Effect on Steel: Increases strength; improves corrosion resistance; deoxidizer
• Purpose: Deoxidation; spring steel strengthening
• Examples: Spring steels, stainless steels (0.5–2%)
Tungsten (W)
• Effect on Steel: Increases hardness, wear resistance; forms hard carbides; high melting point
• Purpose: High-speed tool material; wear resistance
• Examples: High-speed steel (12–18%), tool steels
Cobalt (Co)
• Effect on Steel: Increases hardness at high temperature; refines carbide structure
• Purpose: Hot hardness; cutting tool performance
• Examples: High-speed steel (5–10%); high-temp alloys

6.3 Carbide-Forming Elements (Hard Carbides)

Strong carbide formers (in decreasing order):

  • Tungsten (WC): Extremely hard; used in cutting tool inserts
  • Vanadium (VC): Very hard; improves tool life
  • Chromium (Cr₃C₂, Cr₇C₃): Hard; improves wear resistance
  • Molybdenum (Mo₂C): Moderately hard; forms secondary hardening peak on tempering

Effect: Carbides remain hard at high temperature → cutting tools maintain hardness at speed.

6.4 Common Alloy Steel Types and Applications

Medium-Carbon Alloy
• Composition (% Typical): C: 0.3–0.5; Mn: 0.7–1; Cr/Mo/Ni: 0.5–2
• Properties: Good toughness + strength balance
• Applications: Gears, shafts, crankshafts
High-Carbon Tool Steel
• Composition (% Typical): C: 0.8–1.3; Cr/Mo/V: 1–5
• Properties: High hardness, wear resistance
• Applications: Dies, punches, gauges, chisels
High-Speed Steel (HSS)
• Composition (% Typical): C: 0.7–0.8; W: 12–18; Mo: 0.5–1; Cr: 4; V: 1–2; Co: 5–10
• Properties: Extremely hard at high temperature (hot hardness)
• Applications: Cutting tools, twist drills, reamers
Nickel-Chrome Steel
• Composition (% Typical): C: 0.3–0.5; Ni: 2–5; Cr: 0.5–1.5
• Properties: Tough, high strength, deep hardening
• Applications: Gears, shafts, heavy-duty parts
Stainless Steel (Austenitic)
• Composition (% Typical): C: ≤0.1; Cr: 16–18; Ni: 8–12
• Properties: Corrosion resistant; non-magnetic (austenite); ductile
• Applications: Cutlery, kitchenware, chemical equipment
Stainless Steel (Martensitic)
• Composition (% Typical): C: 0.1–0.3; Cr: 12–17; Ni: minimal
• Properties: Hard, corrosion resistant; magnetic (ferrite-based)
• Applications: Turbine blades, knife blades
Spring Steel
• Composition (% Typical): C: 0.5–0.8; Si: 1.5–2; Cr/Mn: 0.5–1.5
• Properties: High elasticity, good fatigue resistance
• Applications: Leaf springs, coil springs, wire
Bearing Steel
• Composition (% Typical): C: 1.0; Cr: 1.5; very low S, P impurities
• Properties: Very high hardness, wear resistance, low porosity
• Applications: Ball bearings, roller bearings

6.5 Hardenability

Definition: Ability to form martensite throughout the entire section (depth of hardening).

Factors Improving Hardenability:

  • Increased carbon content (>0.3% C)
  • Alloying elements (Mn, Cr, Mo, Ni, V) — all improve hardenability
  • Lower critical cooling rate (easier to achieve martensite even with slower cooling)
💡 Concept
: Small alloying element additions can enable hardening with oil/air cooling instead of brine (reduces distortion and cracking).

Chapter 7: Manufacture of Iron and Steel (Brief Overview)

7.1 Blast Furnace Process

Input: Iron ore (Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄), coke (C), limestone (CaCO₃)

Process:

  1. Ore reduced by CO gas (produced from coke oxidation)
  2. Coke provides heat and reducing agent
  3. Limestone forms slag (fuses impurities)
  4. Molten iron collected at bottom (approx. 4% C, called pig iron or hot metal)

Output: Pig iron (cast iron composition; too brittle for most uses)

7.2 Steelmaking (Conversion of Pig Iron to Steel)

Oxygen Furnace (Converter) Method (Most Common):

  • Blow pure oxygen through molten pig iron
  • Carbon, silicon, manganese oxidize and burn out (exothermic, provides heat)
  • Limestone added to form slag
  • Controlled by adjusting oxygen flow and lime
  • Produces steels with C: 0.05–1.5% (lower carbon than pig iron)
💡 Key Concept
: Steel is essentially pig iron with most carbon and impurities removed by oxidation.

7.3 Desulfurization

  • Sulfur is a harmful impurity in steel (causes brittleness at high temperature — "hot shortness")
  • Removed by adding calcium carbide (CaC₂) or lime + aluminum
  • Forms sulfide slag that floats and is removed

Chapter 8: Powder Metallurgy (Principles)

8.1 Process Sequence (Fixed Order — High Frequency in Exams)

  1. Production of Metal Powder: Atomization, reduction, electrolysis, or mechanical crushing
  2. Blending/Mixing: Powders mixed with lubricants (for better flow) and binders (for green strength)
  3. Compacting/Pressing: Powder pressed in dies to form "green compact" (shaped, low strength)
  4. Sintering: Heating green compact below melting point of major constituent
⚠️ Exam Trap
: Sequence is fixed; any option changing order is wrong.

8.2 Sintering – Core Concepts

Definition: Heating the green compact at elevated temperature (below melting point) to bond particles and increase density.

How It Works:

  • Particles do not melt
  • Bonding occurs by solid-state diffusion (atomic, surface, or bulk diffusion)
  • Atomic bridges form at contact points
  • Porosity decreases; density and strength increase
📌 Main Objectives
:
  1. Bond powder particles together
  2. Increase density and mechanical strength
  3. Reduce porosity and brittleness
  4. Remove oxides from particle surfaces
📌 Factors Affecting Sintering
:
  • Particle size: Finer particles sinter faster (larger surface area)
  • Compacting pressure: Higher pressure → more contact area → faster sintering
  • Sintering temperature: Higher temperature → faster diffusion → faster sintering
  • Sintering time: Longer time → more complete bonding
  • Atmosphere: Controlled atmosphere prevents re-oxidation

Key Fact: Weight of powder used = weight of green compact (mass conservation).

📌 Densification
: NOT caused by grain growth; caused by particle bonding and pore elimination.

8.3 Secondary Operations (Finishing Processes)

Impregnation
• Process: Soak sintered part in oil or lubricant
• Purpose: Fills pores with lubricant → imparts self-lubricating property
• Typical Use: Self-lubricating bearings
Infiltration
• Process: Soak in molten metal of lower melting point
• Purpose: Liquid metal fills pores → increases density and strength
• Typical Use: Increasing strength of porous parts
Coining/Sizing
• Process: Press sintered part to improve dimensions
• Purpose: Improves dimensional accuracy and surface finish
• Typical Use: Precision parts
Heat Treatment
• Process: Standard heat treatment (annealing, hardening, etc.)
• Purpose: Further strengthen or improve properties
• Typical Use: Hardening powder-metal gears
📌 Distinction
:
  • Impregnation → lubrication property
  • Infiltration → densification
  • Sintering is primary process; impregnation/infiltration are secondary

8.4 Applications

  • Tungsten carbide (WC) tool bits and inserts (most common)
  • Self-lubricating bearings (impregnated with oil)
  • Porous filters and sound absorbers
  • Complex shapes difficult to machine (net-shape manufacturing)
  • Magnetic alloys (iron-cobalt, alnico)
  • Refractory materials
🎯 Exam Focus
: Tungsten carbide tools are the most frequently asked application.

Quick Revision Checklist (24-Hour Pre-Exam Review)

Mechanical Properties

  • Elasticity = regain shape after load removal (high E = stiffer)
  • Ductility = tension deformation (wires); Malleability = compression (sheets)
  • Toughness = total area under stress-strain curve; Resilience = area to elastic limit only
  • Creep requires both constant load AND elevated temperature
  • Fatigue failure occurs below yield stress (cyclic loading)
  • Hardness ≠ Strength (hardness is resistance to indentation)

Crystal Structures

  • BCC: 2 atoms/cell, CN = 8, APF = 0.68 (α-iron, Cr, Mo, W)
  • FCC: 4 atoms/cell, CN = 12, APF = 0.74 (γ-iron, Cu, Al, Ni)
  • HCP: 6 atoms/cell, CN = 12, APF = 0.74 (Zn, Mg, Ti)
  • FCC and HCP both have highest packing efficiency (0.74)
  • Isotropic = properties same in all directions

Imperfections

  • Point defects: Vacancy (missing), Interstitial (extra), Frenkel (displaced ion)
  • Interstitials harden (e.g., carbon in steel); Vacancies soften and enable diffusion
  • Dislocations (edge, screw) enable plastic deformation
  • Burgers vector sum at node = 0 (equilibrium)

Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram (CRITICAL)

  • Eutectoid: 727°C, 0.8% C → Austenite → Pearlite (ferrite + cementite)
  • Eutectic: 1147°C, 4.3% C → Liquid → Ledeburite (austenite + cementite)
  • Ferrite: soft, ductile, BCC, ~0.02% C max
  • Austenite: FCC, 2.1% C max at 1147°C
  • Cementite: 6.67% C (fixed), hard, brittle
  • Martensite: supersaturated C in α-iron, very hard, very brittle, needle-like, from rapid quench
  • Pearlite: ferrite + cementite lamellae, from eutectoid decomposition
  • Bainite: ferrite + fine cementite, from intermediate cooling
  • Lever rule: W_phase = (C_other − C₀) / (C_other − C_phase) × 100%

Steel Classification

  • Hypoeutectoid (<0.8%C): ferrite + pearlite, softer
  • Eutectoid (0.8%C): 100% pearlite
  • Hypereutectoid (0.8–2.1%C): cementite + pearlite, harder, prone to retained austenite

Heat Treatment

  • Annealing: heat above A₃, slow cool → soft, ductile, stress-relieved
  • Normalizing: heat above A₃, air cool → refined grain, improved strength/toughness
  • Hardening: quench rapidly → martensite, hard but brittle
  • Tempering: reheat to 150–650°C → removes brittleness, improves toughness
  • Spheroidizing: heat just below A₁ → cementite spheroids → maximum machinability
  • Quench speed: brine > water > oil > air
  • Ms/Mf: Martensite start/finish temperatures
  • TTT diagram: S-curve showing transformation kinetics; critical cooling rate bypasses pearlite nose

Alloy Steels

  • Carbon: primary hardening element
  • Manganese: strengthener, hardenability improver
  • Chromium: hardness, wear resistance, corrosion resistance, carbide former
  • Molybdenum: high-temp strength, deep hardening
  • Vanadium: wear resistance, toughness at high hardness, carbide former
  • Nickel: toughness, deep hardening, low-temp impact strength
  • Tungsten: extreme hardness, high-speed tool material
  • HSS (High-Speed Steel): W, Mo, Cr, V, Co — extreme hot hardness for cutting tools
  • Hardenability = depth of hardening; improved by alloying and carbon content

Powder Metallurgy

  • Sequence: Production → Blending → Compacting → Sintering (FIXED)
  • Sintering: heating below melting point; particles bond via diffusion (do NOT melt)
  • Green compact weight = powder weight (mass conservation)
  • Objectives: bonding, density increase, strength, oxide removal
  • Impregnation: self-lubrication; Infiltration: densification
  • Sintering = primary process; impregnation/infiltration = secondary
  • Application: Tungsten carbide tools (most common)

Bonding

  • Metallic: electron sea, ductile, conducts electricity, high MP
  • Covalent: directional, very hard, brittle, high MP
  • Ionic: electrostatic, hard but brittle, high MP, insulator in solid
  • Van der Waals: weakest, low MP, easy to deform

Key Formulas Summary

Atoms per cell = (corners ÷ 8) + (edges ÷ 4) + (faces ÷ 2) + interior
• Use: Count atoms in unit cell
• Example: BCC = 1 + 1 = 2
APF = (Volume of atoms) / (Volume of cell)
• Use: Packing efficiency
• Example: FCC/HCP = 0.74
Lever Rule: W_α = (C_γ − C₀) / (C_γ − C_α) × 100%
• Use: Find phase fractions in two-phase region
• Example: Ferrite + Pearlite region
Hall-Petch: σ_y = σ₀ + k·d⁻⁰·⁵
• Use: Grain size effect on yield strength
• Example: Finer grain → higher strength
Burgers vector sum at node = 0
• Use: Dislocation equilibrium
• Example: Nodal points in dislocation networks
