1. Blackbody Radiation Laws

A blackbody is the ideal radiator — it absorbs all incident radiation, regardless of wavelength or direction, and emits the maximum possible energy at any given temperature. Real surfaces emit less than a blackbody.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law
$$E_b = \sigma T^4 \qquad \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\;\text{W/m}^2\!\cdot\!\text{K}^4$$
$T$ must be in Kelvin. Doubling absolute temperature increases emissive power by a factor of $2^4 = 16$.
Wien's Displacement Law
$$\lambda_{\max}\,T = 2898\;\mu\text{m·K}$$
As temperature increases, the wavelength of peak emission shifts to shorter wavelengths ($\lambda_{\max} \propto 1/T$). The sun ($\approx 5800\,\text{K}$) peaks in visible; industrial furnaces peak in infrared.
Kirchhoff's Law
$$\varepsilon = \alpha \quad \text{(at the same temperature and wavelength)}$$
A surface that absorbs well also emits well. For a blackbody: $\varepsilon = \alpha = 1$.

2. Surface Radiation Properties

Every real surface is characterised by three radiation properties that must sum to unity. For any surface:

Property Balance
$$\alpha + \rho + \tau = 1$$
$\alpha$ = absorptivity  ·  $\rho$ = reflectivity  ·  $\tau$ = transmissivity
Surface typeDefining propertySimplified balance
Blackbody$\alpha = \varepsilon = 1$; $\rho = \tau = 0$Perfect absorber/emitter
Opaque$\tau = 0$$\alpha + \rho = 1$
Gray body$\varepsilon_\lambda = \text{const}$ (independent of $\lambda$)$\varepsilon = \alpha < 1$; Kirchhoff applies directly
Selective surface$\varepsilon_\lambda$ varies with $\lambda$Solar collectors, space radiators
White body$\rho = 1$Perfect reflector; $\alpha = \tau = 0$

3. Radiosity, Irradiation & Intensity

Irradiation $G$ is the total radiation flux incident on a surface from all directions [W/m²]. Radiosity $J$ is the total radiation flux leaving a surface, combining both emitted and reflected components:

Radiosity
$$J = \varepsilon E_b + \rho G$$
For a blackbody: $\rho = 0$, $\varepsilon = 1$ → $J = E_b$ (all leaving radiation is emitted).
Emissive Power vs Radiation Intensity (Diffuse Surface)
$$E = \pi I$$
$I$ = radiation intensity [W/m²·sr]  ·  valid for a diffuse (Lambertian) emitter.

4. Shape Factors (View Factors)

The shape factor $F_{ij}$ is the fraction of radiation that leaves surface $i$ and is intercepted directly by surface $j$. It is a purely geometric quantity — independent of temperature and surface emissivity.

Reciprocity Relation
$$A_i\,F_{ij} = A_j\,F_{ji}$$
Summation Rule (Enclosure)
$$\sum_{j=1}^{N} F_{ij} = 1$$
All radiation leaving surface $i$ must reach some surface in the enclosure.
GeometrySelf-view factor $F_{ii}$Notes
Flat surface$F_{11} = 0$Cannot see any part of itself
Convex surface$F_{11} = 0$Cannot see itself
Concave surface$F_{11} > 0$Part of radiation curves back to self
Two infinite parallel planes$F_{12} = F_{21} = 1$All radiation from one reaches the other

5. Heat Exchange Between Gray Surfaces

5.1 Two Infinite Parallel Gray Planes

Net Heat Flux — Two Infinite Parallel Gray Planes
$$q_{12} = \frac{\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)}{\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_1} + \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_2} - 1}$$
Equivalent emissivity: $\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \dfrac{1}{1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1}$

5.2 Small Convex Body in a Large Enclosure

When surface 1 is small and convex and is enclosed by a much larger surface 2 ($A_1 \ll A_2$), the enclosure behaves effectively as a blackbody ($F_{12} = 1$, $\varepsilon_2 \to 1$):

Small Body in Large Enclosure
$$Q_{12} = \varepsilon_1 A_1 \sigma (T_1^4 - T_2^4)$$
The large enclosure's emissivity has no effect — only the small body's emissivity matters.

6. Radiation Shields

A radiation shield is a thin, low-emissivity sheet inserted between two radiating surfaces to reduce the net heat transfer. Each shield adds an additional thermal resistance to the radiative network.

Effect of $n$ Shields (Equal Emissivity)
$$Q_n = \frac{Q_0}{n + 1}$$
$Q_0$ = heat transfer without shields; $Q_n$ = with $n$ identical shields of equal emissivity $\varepsilon_s$.
This simplification holds only when all surfaces (including shields) have the same emissivity.

Shields are most effective when made of low-emissivity materials (polished metals like aluminium or stainless steel). The shield formula above applies to parallel gray planes of identical emissivity — for mixed emissivities the full network analysis is needed.

Key Points to Remember

Radiation Summary

  • $E_b = \sigma T^4$, $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\;\text{W/m}^2\!\cdot\!\text{K}^4$. Always use absolute Kelvin.
  • Doubling absolute temperature → emissive power increases $2^4 = 16$ times.
  • Wien's law: $\lambda_{\max} T = 2898\;\mu\text{m·K}$. Higher $T$ → shorter peak wavelength.
  • Kirchhoff's law: $\varepsilon = \alpha$ at thermal equilibrium (same $T$, same $\lambda$).
  • Property balance: $\alpha + \rho + \tau = 1$. Opaque: $\tau = 0$, so $\alpha + \rho = 1$.
  • Blackbody: $\alpha = \varepsilon = 1$, $\rho = \tau = 0$. Gray body: $\varepsilon = \alpha = \text{const} < 1$.
  • Radiosity: $J = \varepsilon E_b + \rho G$. For blackbody: $J = E_b$.
  • Diffuse surface: $E = \pi I$ (emissive power = $\pi$ × intensity).
  • Shape factor $F_{ij}$ is purely geometric — independent of temperature and emissivity.
  • Reciprocity: $A_i F_{ij} = A_j F_{ji}$. Summation: $\sum_j F_{ij} = 1$.
  • Flat and convex surfaces: $F_{11} = 0$. Concave surfaces: $F_{11} > 0$.
  • Infinite parallel planes: $q_{12} = \sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)/(1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1)$.
  • Small body in large enclosure: $Q_{12} = \varepsilon_1 A_1 \sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)$ — only small body's $\varepsilon$ matters.
  • Equal-emissivity shields: $Q_n = Q_0/(n+1)$. One shield → 50% reduction; three shields → 75% reduction.
