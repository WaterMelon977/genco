1. Extended Surfaces — Fins

Fins (extended surfaces) are attached to a primary surface to increase the effective heat transfer area. They are most useful when the convective resistance on one side is very high — typically the gas side of a gas-to-liquid exchanger.

Fin Parameter $m$
$$m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}} \qquad \left[\text{m}^{-1}\right]$$
$P$ = fin perimeter [m]  ·  $A_c$ = fin cross-sectional area [m²]  ·  Higher $m$ → faster temperature decay along fin.

1.1 Fin Efficiency

Fin efficiency $\eta_f$ is the ratio of actual heat transfer from the fin to the heat transfer that would occur if the entire fin were at the base temperature.

Fin Efficiency — Straight Rectangular Fin, Insulated Tip
$$\eta_f = \frac{\tanh(mL)}{mL} \leq 1$$
For a rectangular fin with convective tip, use corrected length $L_c = L + t/2$ (where $t$ is fin thickness) in place of $L$.

1.2 Fin Effectiveness

Fin effectiveness $\varepsilon_f$ is the ratio of heat transfer with the fin to heat transfer from the same base area without the fin. Adding a fin is justified only if $\varepsilon_f > 2$.

Fin Effectiveness
$$\varepsilon_f = \sqrt{\frac{kP}{hA_c}}\,\tanh(mL)$$
$\varepsilon_f > 2.0$ → fin is justified (more than doubles heat transfer).
$\varepsilon_f < 1.0$ → fin reduces heat transfer (insulating effect — avoid).
Fins work best with high $k$, low $h$, slender geometry (large $P/A_c$).

2. Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient & Fouling

In a heat exchanger the total thermal resistance between two fluid streams includes the convective resistance on each side plus the wall conduction resistance. The overall heat transfer coefficient $U$ summarises all resistances:

Overall HTC (Plane Wall, No Fouling)
$$\frac{1}{U} = \frac{1}{h_i} + R_{\text{wall}} + \frac{1}{h_o}$$

Fouling — the deposition of scale, corrosion products, or biological matter on surfaces — increases thermal resistance over time and reduces $U$. The fouling factor $R_f$ quantifies this degradation:

Fouling Factor
$$R_f = \frac{1}{U_{\text{dirty}}} - \frac{1}{U_{\text{clean}}} \qquad \left[\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K/W}\right]$$

3. Shell-and-Tube Heat Exchanger Design

The shell-and-tube is the most widely used heat exchanger in industry. Key design points tested in AP GenCo and SSC JE:

FeaturePurpose
BafflesSupport the tube bundle mechanically and force the shell-side fluid to flow across (rather than along) the tubes, increasing shell-side velocity and $h_o$.
Floating headAllows the tube bundle to expand and contract independently of the shell, accommodating differential thermal expansion under large temperature differences.
Tube-side routingCorrosive, high-pressure, and scaling fluids are routed through the tubes — cheaper to use alloy tubes than an alloy shell, and tubes are easier to clean mechanically.
Shell-side routingViscous fluids go on the shell side to take advantage of the cross-flow geometry, which gives better heat transfer for high-viscosity liquids.
Multi-pass LMTDMultiple tube passes increase tube-side velocity but require a correction factor $F < 1$ applied to the counterflow LMTD: $Q = UA \cdot F \cdot \text{LMTD}_{\text{CF}}$.

4. LMTD Method

The Log Mean Temperature Difference method is used when the inlet and outlet temperatures of both streams are known. The heat duty is:

LMTD Definition
$$\text{LMTD} = \frac{\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2}{\ln\!\left(\Delta T_1 / \Delta T_2\right)}$$
$\Delta T_1$, $\Delta T_2$ = temperature differences between hot and cold streams at each end of the exchanger.
Special case: if $\Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$ → $\text{LMTD} = \Delta T_1$ (simple arithmetic mean).
Heat Duty
$$Q = U A\, \text{LMTD}$$

Counter-flow vs parallel-flow: For the same terminal temperatures, the counterflow arrangement always gives a larger LMTD than parallel flow, meaning it requires less heat transfer area for the same duty. Counterflow is therefore the preferred arrangement wherever it is mechanically feasible.

LMTD Comparison
$$\text{LMTD}_{\text{counter}} > \text{LMTD}_{\text{parallel}}$$
For the same $Q$ and $U$: counterflow needs minimum area $A$.

5. NTU-Effectiveness Method

When outlet temperatures are unknown, the NTU-effectiveness method is preferred over iterating the LMTD. It works with inlet temperatures and the heat exchanger's physical size.

Heat Capacity Rates & Capacity Ratio
$$C_h = \dot{m}_h C_{p,h}, \quad C_c = \dot{m}_c C_{p,c}, \quad C = \frac{C_{\min}}{C_{\max}}$$
$C$ ranges from 0 (one stream is condensing/boiling) to 1 (balanced streams).
Number of Transfer Units
$$\text{NTU} = \frac{UA}{C_{\min}}$$
NTU is a dimensionless size parameter — larger NTU means larger or more effective heat exchanger.
Effectiveness Definition
$$\varepsilon = \frac{Q_{\text{actual}}}{Q_{\max}} = \frac{Q_{\text{actual}}}{C_{\min}\,(T_{h,i} - T_{c,i})}$$
$Q_{\max}$ is the thermodynamic maximum: achieved if the stream with $C_{\min}$ undergoes the full inlet-to-inlet temperature difference.

6. Special Cases of NTU-ε

ConditionEffectiveness $\varepsilon$Notes
Phase change — boiler or condenser ($C = 0$)$\varepsilon = 1 - e^{-\text{NTU}}$Same formula for both parallel and counterflow. Phase-change stream has infinite capacity rate.
Balanced counterflow ($C = 1$)$\varepsilon = \dfrac{\text{NTU}}{1 + \text{NTU}}$Approaches 1 as NTU → ∞ but never reaches it.
Parallel flow, maximum ($C = 1$, NTU → ∞)$\varepsilon_{\max} = 0.5 = 50\%$Fundamental upper limit for parallel flow — outlet temperatures equalise.
Counterflow general ($0 \le C < 1$)$\varepsilon = \dfrac{1 - e^{-\text{NTU}(1-C)}}{1 - C\,e^{-\text{NTU}(1-C)}}$Approaches 1 as NTU → ∞ for any $C < 1$.

The $C = 0$ result ($\varepsilon = 1 - e^{-\text{NTU}}$) is the most frequently tested NTU equation — it applies to any flow arrangement when one stream is changing phase.

Key Points to Remember

Fins & Heat Exchangers Summary

  • Fin parameter $m = \sqrt{hP/kA_c}$ in m⁻¹. Larger $m$ → faster temperature decay along fin.
  • Fin efficiency: $\eta_f = \tanh(mL)/(mL)$. Insulated tip. For convective tip, use $L_c = L + t/2$.
  • Fin effectiveness $\varepsilon_f > 2.0$ to justify adding fins; $\varepsilon_f < 1.0$ means fins hurt.
  • Fouling factor $R_f = 1/U_{\text{dirty}} - 1/U_{\text{clean}}$ in m²·K/W. Always reduces $U$.
  • Corrosive & high-pressure fluids → tube side. Viscous fluids → shell side.
  • Baffles: support tubes AND increase shell-side $h_o$ by inducing cross-flow.
  • Floating head: accommodates differential thermal expansion — used for large $\Delta T$ applications.
  • $\text{LMTD}_{\text{counter}} > \text{LMTD}_{\text{parallel}}$ → counterflow requires less surface area for same duty.
  • $\Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$ → $\text{LMTD} = \Delta T_1$ (becomes arithmetic mean).
  • $\text{NTU} = UA/C_{\min}$. Dimensionless size of heat exchanger.
  • $Q_{\max} = C_{\min}(T_{h,i} - T_{c,i})$ — thermodynamic ceiling.
  • Phase change ($C = 0$): $\varepsilon = 1 - e^{-\text{NTU}}$ for any flow arrangement.
  • Balanced counterflow ($C = 1$): $\varepsilon = \text{NTU}/(1 + \text{NTU})$.
  • Parallel flow absolute maximum effectiveness = 50% (at $C = 1$, NTU → ∞).
