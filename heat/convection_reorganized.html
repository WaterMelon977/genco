1. Dimensionless Parameters

Convective heat transfer is expressed compactly through dimensionless groups. Knowing the physical meaning of each group is essential — exams test definitions and ratios as directly as they test correlations.

NumberFormulaPhysical meaning
Nusselt $Nu$$\dfrac{hL}{k_f}$Convective / conductive heat transfer across fluid layer. Minimum $Nu = 1$ (pure conduction).
Prandtl $Pr$$\dfrac{\nu}{\alpha} = \dfrac{\mu C_p}{k_f}$Momentum diffusivity / thermal diffusivity. Governs relative boundary layer thickness.
Reynolds $Re$$\dfrac{\rho V L}{\mu}$Inertia / viscous forces. Governs laminar-turbulent transition.
Grashof $Gr$$\dfrac{g\beta\Delta T L^3}{\nu^2}$Buoyancy / viscous forces. Primary parameter in free convection.
Rayleigh $Ra$$Gr \cdot Pr$Governs transition to turbulent free convection. Critical $Ra \approx 10^9$ (vertical plate).
Stanton $St$$\dfrac{Nu}{Re \cdot Pr} = \dfrac{h}{\rho V C_p}$Actual heat transfer / maximum possible by fluid stream. Under Reynolds analogy: $St = C_f/2$.
Biot $Bi$$\dfrac{h L_c}{k_{\text{solid}}}$External convective resistance / internal conductive resistance of the solid. Used for transients.
Fourier $Fo$$\dfrac{\alpha t}{L_c^2}$Dimensionless time. Appears in transient solutions together with $Bi$.

Important distinction: $Bi$ uses $k_{\text{solid}}$ and characterises the solid body. $Nu$ uses $k_{\text{fluid}}$ and characterises the fluid-side heat transfer. They have the same form but different meanings.

2. Boundary Layer Theory

When a fluid flows over a surface, the velocity gradient near the wall forms the hydrodynamic boundary layer of thickness $\delta$, and the temperature gradient forms the thermal boundary layer of thickness $\delta_t$. Their relative size is governed by the Prandtl number:

Thermal to Hydrodynamic Boundary Layer Ratio
$$\frac{\delta_t}{\delta} = Pr^{-1/3}$$
$Pr = 1$ → $\delta_t = \delta$ (velocity and temperature profiles coincide).
$Pr \gg 1$ (oils) → $\delta_t \ll \delta$ (thin thermal layer inside thick velocity layer).
$Pr \ll 1$ (liquid metals) → $\delta_t \gg \delta$ (thermal layer much thicker).

Along an isothermal plate, the local heat transfer coefficient $h_x$ decreases downstream as $\delta_t(x)$ grows. Turbulence resets the boundary layer and sharply raises $h$.

3. Forced Convection — Flat Plate & Pipe Flow

3.1 Laminar Flow Over an Isothermal Flat Plate

Local Nusselt Number (Laminar, Flat Plate)
$$Nu_x = 0.332\, Re_x^{1/2}\, Pr^{1/3}$$
Valid for $Pr \geq 0.6$, laminar ($Re_x < 5 \times 10^5$).
Average vs Local Coefficient — Flat Plate (Laminar)
$$\bar{h}_L = 2\, h_{x=L}$$
The plate-averaged coefficient is exactly twice the value of the local coefficient at the trailing edge.

3.2 Turbulent Flow Inside a Pipe — Dittus-Boelter Equation

The most-tested pipe flow correlation for fully developed turbulent flow in smooth tubes:

Dittus-Boelter Equation
$$Nu_D = 0.023\, Re_D^{0.8}\, Pr^n$$
$n = 0.4$ when fluid is being heated ($T_w > T_f$).
$n = 0.3$ when fluid is being cooled ($T_w < T_f$).
Valid for: $Re_D > 10{,}000$, $0.7 \leq Pr \leq 160$, $L/D > 10$.

4. Free (Natural) Convection

Free convection is driven by buoyancy forces arising from density differences due to temperature gradients. There is no externally imposed velocity. The Grashof number plays the role that Reynolds number plays in forced convection.

Grashof Number
$$Gr_L = \frac{g\,\beta\,\Delta T\,L^3}{\nu^2}$$
$\beta$ = volumetric thermal expansion coefficient [K⁻¹]  ·  For ideal gas: $\beta = 1/T$ [K⁻¹].

The general correlation for free convection takes the form $Nu = C\,(Gr\cdot Pr)^n = C\,Ra^n$, where $C$ and $n$ depend on geometry and flow regime.

Velocity profile in free convection over a vertical heated plate: The velocity is zero at the wall (no-slip), rises to a maximum inside the boundary layer, then decays back to zero at the outer edge of the boundary layer. This distinguishes it from forced convection, where the velocity approaches the free-stream value at the edge.

Transition Criterion — Free Convection (Vertical Plate)
$$Ra = Gr \cdot Pr \approx 10^9 \quad \text{(laminar → turbulent)}$$

5. Mixed (Combined) Convection

When both forced flow velocity and buoyancy effects are of comparable magnitude, neither can be neglected. The governing parameter is the ratio $Gr/Re^2$:

Mixed Convection Criterion
$$0.1 \le \frac{Gr}{Re^2} \le 10$$
$Gr/Re^2 \ll 0.1$ → pure forced convection (buoyancy negligible).
$Gr/Re^2 \gg 10$ → pure free convection (forced flow negligible).
$0.1$–$10$ → mixed convection; both effects must be included.

6. Lumped Transient Method (Biot & Fourier Numbers)

See also Chapter 1 §7. The key formulas for transient heat transfer in solids with small Biot number:

Lumped Capacity — Temperature Response
$$\frac{T(t) - T_\infty}{T_i - T_\infty} = e^{-Bi \cdot Fo}$$
Validity: $Bi = hL_c/k_{\text{solid}} < 0.1$.
$Fo = \alpha t / L_c^2$  ·  $L_c = V/A_s$.
Infinite-Fin Temperature Profile
$$\frac{T(x) - T_\infty}{T_b - T_\infty} = e^{-mx}, \qquad m = \sqrt{\frac{hP}{kA_c}}$$
Temperature decays exponentially from base to tip for an infinitely long fin.

Key Points to Remember

Convection Summary

  • $Nu = hL/k_f$ (fluid $k$). Minimum value = 1 (pure conduction limit).
  • $Pr = \nu/\alpha = \mu C_p / k_f$. Governs $\delta_t / \delta = Pr^{-1/3}$.
  • $Pr = 1$ → $\delta_t = \delta$. Liquid metals: $Pr \ll 1$ → $\delta_t \gg \delta$. Oils: $Pr \gg 1$ → $\delta_t \ll \delta$.
  • $Gr = g\beta\Delta T L^3/\nu^2$. Free convection transition at $Ra = Gr \cdot Pr \approx 10^9$.
  • Mixed convection when $0.1 \le Gr/Re^2 \le 10$.
  • $St = Nu/(Re \cdot Pr) = C_f/2$ (Reynolds analogy, forced flow, $Pr=1$).
  • Free convection velocity profile: zero at wall AND zero at outer boundary — unique double zero profile.
  • Flat plate laminar: $\bar{h}_L = 2h_{x=L}$. Local $h_x$ decreases downstream.
  • Dittus-Boelter: $Nu_D = 0.023 Re_D^{0.8} Pr^n$. Heating: $n=0.4$; Cooling: $n=0.3$.
  • Infinite fin: temperature profile is $e^{-mx}$, decaying from base.
  • Lumped valid when $Bi < 0.1$. Response: $e^{-Bi\cdot Fo}$.
  • $Bi$ uses $k_{\text{solid}}$; $Nu$ uses $k_{\text{fluid}}$ — same formula, different materials.
  • $\beta = 1/T$ for ideal gas (in absolute Kelvin).
