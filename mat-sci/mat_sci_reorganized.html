§3 Deep-Dive Main Body

3.1 Structure & Properties of Engineering Materials & Bonding

Engineering materials derive their elastic modulus, melting temperature, thermal expansion coefficient, electrical conductivity, and mechanical ductility from their fundamental interatomic bonding mechanism.

  • Metallic Bonding: Non-directional electrostatic attraction between array of positive ion cores and delocalised electron cloud ("sea of electrons"). High electrical and thermal conductivity (via free electrons), high ductility (planes slide past each other without breaking discrete bonds).
  • Covalent Bonding: Highly directional sharing of valence electron pairs between specific atoms. High bond energy, extreme hardness, high melting point, brittle nature, electrical insulator. Examples: Diamond ($sp^3$ hybridization), Silicon, Silicon Carbide ($\text{SiC}$).
  • Ionic Bonding: Coulombic attraction between oppositely charged ions ($\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$). High lattice energy, high melting point, brittle solid state insulator, conducts in molten or aqueous state. Examples: $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{MgO}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$.
  • Van der Waals Bonding: Secondary weak electrostatic attractions due to temporary or permanent dipoles. Low bond energy ($<10\ \text{kJ/mol}$), low melting points, soft polymers.

3.2 Crystal Structures & Unit Cell Geometry

Metals solidify into crystalline lattices where unit cells repeat in 3D space. The ratio of sphere volume inside the cell to total cell volume is the Atomic Packing Factor (APF).

Theoretical Density Calculation
$$\rho = \frac{N \cdot A}{V_c \cdot N_A}$$

Where $N$ = number of atoms per unit cell, $A$ = atomic mass ($\text{g/mol}$), $V_c$ = volume of unit cell ($a^3$ for cubic), $N_A$ = Avogadro's number ($6.023 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms/mol}$).

3.3 Imperfections in Crystals & Metals

Real crystals are never perfect. Defects are classified by dimensionality into Point (0D), Line (1D), Planar (2D), and Volume (3D) defects.

Defect CategoryDefect NamePhysical Nature & Mechanics
Point Defects (0D)VacancyUnoccupied lattice site. Thermally generated: $n_v = N \exp(-Q_v / k T)$. Enables diffusion.
Point Defects (0D)Interstitial / SubstitutionalForeign or host atom crammed in void or replacing matrix atom. Causes solid solution hardening.
Point Defects (0D)Schottky & FrenkelSchottky: Cation+Anion vacancy pair. Frenkel: Ion displaced to interstitial site. Maintain charge neutrality.
Line Defects (1D)Edge DislocationExtra half-plane of atoms inserted. Burgers vector $\mathbf{b} \perp$ dislocation line $\mathbf{t}$. Slip occurs parallel to $\mathbf{b}$.
Line Defects (1D)Screw DislocationHelical spiral ramp plane. Burgers vector $\mathbf{b} \parallel$ dislocation line $\mathbf{t}$. Slip occurs $\perp$ to $\mathbf{b}$.
Planar Defects (2D)Grain BoundariesNarrow 2D transition zones between differently oriented crystals. Impede dislocation motion.
Planar Defects (2D)Twin BoundariesSpecific plane across which lattice orientation is a mirror reflection. Common in FCC/HCP under deformation.
Volume Defects (3D)Voids & InclusionsPores, oxide inclusions ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{SiO}_2$), slag traps. Act as stress raisers causing fatigue failure.
Hall-Petch Strengthening Mechanism
$$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y \cdot d^{-1/2}$$

Grain boundaries present high-angle lattice disorientations that halt dislocation pile-ups. Finer grain sizes (smaller $d$) increase grain boundary area per unit volume, forcing higher shear stress $\sigma_y$ to continue plastic flow. Grain refinement uniquely improves strength without sacrificing impact toughness!

3.4 Structure of Alloys & Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram

An alloy is a combination of two or more elements with metallic properties. Solid solutions form when solute atoms dissolve in the solvent matrix without changing crystal structure.

💡 Hume-Rothery Rules for Unlimited Substitutional Solid Solubility:
1. Atomic Size Factor: Difference in atomic radii $\Delta r < 15\%$.
2. Crystal Structure: Must have identical crystal structures (e.g. Cu and Ni both FCC → Monel unlimited solubility).
3. Valency: A metal dissolves a metal of higher valency more readily than one of lower valency.
4. Electronegativity: Must be similar. High electronegativity difference forms intermetallic compounds instead.

3.7 Alloy Steels & Alloying Elements

💡 Classification of Alloying Elements:
• Austenite Stabilisers (Lower $A_3$ line): Nickel ($\text{Ni}$), Manganese ($\text{Mn}$), Cobalt ($\text{Co}$), Nitrogen ($\text{N}$). They expand the $\gamma$-field to room temp.
• Ferrite Stabilisers (Raise $A_3$, close $\gamma$-loop): Chromium ($\text{Cr}$), Molybdenum ($\text{Mo}$), Vanadium ($\text{V}$), Tungsten ($\text{W}$), Silicon ($\text{Si}$), Titanium ($\text{Ti}$).
• Strong Carbide Formers: $\text{Ti} > \text{V} > \text{W} > \text{Mo} > \text{Cr} > \text{Mn} > \text{Fe}$. Non-carbide formers: $\text{Ni}, \text{Si}, \text{Cu}, \text{Al}$.

§4 Core Theory Q&A Bank

Q1: Why does FCC Austenite dissolve significantly more carbon than BCC Ferrite despite BCC being less densely packed (APF 0.68 vs 0.74)?
Answer: Although BCC has a lower overall atomic packing factor (0.68), its interstitial void spaces are smaller and asymmetric. In BCC ferrite, the largest interstitial site is the tetrahedral void ($r_{\text{void}} = 0.291 r_{\text{atom}}$), whereas the octahedral void is squeezed ($0.155 r_{\text{atom}}$). In FCC austenite, the octahedral interstitial void is symmetric and significantly larger ($r_{\text{void}} = 0.414 r_{\text{atom}}$). Carbon atoms ($r_{\text{carbon}} = 0.071\ \text{nm}$) fit into FCC octahedral voids with much less lattice distortion, allowing up to 2.14% C solubility compared to only 0.022% C in BCC ferrite.
Q2: Explain the Hall-Petch equation and why grain boundary refinement is uniquely beneficial among strengthening mechanisms.
Answer: The Hall-Petch equation is $\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y d^{-1/2}$. Grain boundaries act as impermeable barriers to dislocation slip because adjacent grains possess different crystallographic orientations. As dislocations pile up against a grain boundary, a stress concentration builds until it triggers slip in the neighbouring grain. Smaller grains (smaller $d$) shorten the pile-up length, requiring higher applied stress $\sigma_y$ to continue deformation. Grain refinement is unique because unlike strain hardening or solid solution hardening (which reduce ductility/impact energy), grain refinement increases BOTH yield strength AND notch impact toughness by creating a tortuous path for crack propagation.
Q3: Differentiate clearly between Austempering and Martempering heat treatment processes.
Answer:
• Austempering: Quenching steel from above $A_3$ into a molten salt bath maintained at $250\text{--}450^\circ\text{C}$ (in the bainite formation range) and holding isothermally until austenite completely transforms into 100% Bainite. It is followed by air cooling. No subsequent tempering is needed. Yields high toughness, strength, and fatigue resistance with zero quench cracking.
• Martempering (Marquenching): Quenching steel into a salt bath just above the $M_s$ temperature ($200\text{--}250^\circ\text{C}$), holding just long enough to equalize temperature throughout the cross-section without forming bainite, then air cooling through the $M_s\text{--}M_f$ range to form Martensite. It MUST be followed by conventional tempering to restore toughness.
Q4: What is the exact function of Cobalt in Tungsten Carbide ($\text{WC-Co}$) cutting tools manufactured by Powder Metallurgy?
Answer: Tungsten Carbide ($\text{WC}$) possesses extreme hardness and wear resistance but is extremely brittle with near-zero fracture toughness. In PM processing, Cobalt ($\text{Co}$) acts as a ductile metallic binder matrix (~6–12% by weight). During liquid-phase sintering at ~1400°C, molten cobalt wets the WC powder grains, cementing the hard WC particles together into a tough composite matrix. Cobalt provides the necessary shock resistance and toughness so the tool can withstand mechanical impact during metal cutting without catastrophic chipping.
Q5: Explain why German Silver is called by that name despite containing no silver metal.
Answer: German Silver (also called Nickel Silver) is a ternary copper alloy consisting of approximately 60% Cu, 20% Ni, and 20% Zn. Nickel completely decolorises the yellowish copper color, giving the alloy a bright, lustrous silvery-white aesthetic appearance identical to sterling silver. It was historically developed in Germany as an economical substitute for genuine silver in tableware, cutlery, musical instruments, and decorative hardware.
Q6: Describe the age hardening (precipitation hardening) process sequence for Duralumin ($\text{Al-4\% Cu}$).
Answer: Age hardening consists of 3 distinct steps:
1. Solution Heat Treatment: Heat alloy to ~500°C in the single-phase $\alpha$ region to dissolve all copper into solid solution.
2. Quenching: Rapidly cool in cold water to room temperature to trap copper in a supersaturated solid solution (SSSS). The alloy is soft and ductile immediately after quenching.
3. Ageing: Hold at room temperature (Natural Ageing) or reheat to 150–200°C (Artificial Ageing). Copper atoms cluster into extremely fine sub-microscopic GP zones and transition precipitates ($\theta'' / \theta'$ $\text{CuAl}_2$) coherent with the lattice. These coherent strain fields pin dislocation movement, causing a massive increase in yield strength.

§5 Consolidation Notes & Exam Traps

⚠️ CRITICAL EXAM TRAPS & GOTCHAS:
1. Babbitt Bearing Microstructure: Hard intermetallic cuboids ($\text{Sb}_3\text{Sn}, \text{Cu}_6\text{Sn}_5$) carry the load while the soft Tin matrix wears away slightly to form oil supply channels. Do NOT confuse the matrix and particle roles!
2. Mass Conservation in PM Compacting: The mass of a green compact equals EXACTLY the mass of the starting powder charge. Compaction increases density ($\text{g/cm}^3$) by squeezing out air gaps, but total mass is strictly conserved.
3. Curie Point of Iron: At 768°C ($A_2$ line), iron undergoes a magnetic transition from ferromagnetic to paramagnetic without any change in crystal structure! It remains BCC $\alpha$-ferrite both below and above 768°C up to 912°C ($A_3$).
4. Stainless Steel Minimum Chromium: Stainless steel MUST contain a minimum of 10.5%–12% Chromium to form a microscopic, self-healing passive chromium oxide ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$) film. 18/8 Stainless (Type 304) is non-magnetic because Nickel stabilises the FCC Austenite phase down to room temp.
