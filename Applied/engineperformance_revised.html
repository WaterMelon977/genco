Engine Performance Testing & Design Considerations

1. Supercharging & Turbocharging

Supercharging increases the mass density of intake air (and charge) entering the cylinder, enabling more fuel to be burned and raising power output without increasing engine displacement.

1.1 Charge Density Ratio

$$\frac{\rho_{\text{boost}}}{\rho_{\text{atm}}} = \frac{P_{\text{boost}}}{P_{\text{atm}}} \cdot \frac{T_{\text{atm}}}{T_{\text{boost}}}$$
  • Pressure ratio increases density; temperature rise from compression partially offsets it
  • Intercooler (aftercooler) cools the compressed charge, restoring density closer to the ideal cold-air value
FeatureMechanical SuperchargerTurbocharger
DriveCrankshaft belt/gear drive — parasitic power lossExhaust turbine — uses waste exhaust energy; no direct crankshaft load
ResponseImmediate (no lag)Turbo lag at low RPM
Typical useRacing engines, roots-type on large SI enginesMost modern diesel and petrol production engines

1.2 Supercharging Effect on Knocking

  • CI (Diesel) engine: supercharging reduces knocking tendency — higher intake pressure and temperature shorten ignition delay, reducing fuel accumulation
  • SI (Petrol) engine: supercharging increases knocking tendency — higher end-gas temperature and pressure promote pre-ignition and detonation

2. Engine Powers & Mechanical Efficiency

$$IP = BP + FP \quad \Rightarrow \quad FP = IP - BP$$
$$\eta_m = \frac{BP}{IP} = 1 - \frac{FP}{IP}$$
PowerDefinitionMeasured by
Indicated Power (IP)Power developed inside combustion chamber by gas pressure on pistonP-V indicator diagram; indicator card
Brake Power (BP)Net useful power available at crankshaft after friction lossesDynamometer (rope brake, eddy current, hydraulic)
Friction Power (FP)Power lost to piston friction, bearing friction, pumping, and accessoriesMotoring method; FP = IP − BP

2.1 Rope-Brake Dynamometer

$$BP = \frac{(W - S)\,\pi D_m\,N}{60\,000} \quad \text{(kW)}$$
  • $W$ — dead weight applied to rope (N); $S$ — spring balance reading (N)
  • $D_m = D + d$ — effective brake drum diameter (m); $D$ = drum diameter; $d$ = rope diameter
  • Net braking force = $W - S$; effective radius = $D_m/2$

2.2 Indicated Power from P-V Diagram

$$IP = \frac{P_{mi}\,L\,A\,N\,k}{120\,000} \quad \text{(kW, 4-stroke)}$$
  • $P_{mi}$ — indicated mean effective pressure (Pa); $L$ — stroke (m); $A = \pi D^2/4$ — bore area (m²)
  • Divide by 120,000 for 4-stroke (factor includes $\times 2$ for stroke conversion)
  • For 2-stroke: denominator is 60,000 (power stroke every revolution)

3. Engine Testing Methods

3.1 Morse Test (Multi-Cylinder Engines — IP of Each Cylinder)

With all cylinders firing, record total brake power $BP_{\text{all}}$. Then cut out one cylinder at a time by shorting its spark plug (SI) or stopping fuel injection (CI), and record the new brake power. Since friction power stays approximately constant, the indicated power of the cut-out cylinder is:

$$IP_i = BP_{\text{all}} - BP_{i\,\text{cut-out}}$$
$$IP_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} IP_i, \qquad \eta_m = \frac{BP_{\text{all}}}{IP_{\text{total}}}$$
  • Assumption: FP remains constant when one cylinder is cut out (valid at constant throttle/speed)
  • Applicable only to multi-cylinder engines; cannot be used on single-cylinder engines

3.2 Willan's Line Method (CI Engines — FP at Zero Load)

Plot fuel consumption rate $\dot{m}_f$ against brake power $BP$ at constant speed. The graph is linear (Willan's line). Extrapolate to $\dot{m}_f = 0$ — the negative intercept on the $BP$ axis gives friction power:

$$FP = \text{|Negative } BP \text{ intercept at } \dot{m}_f = 0\text{|}$$
  • Valid only for CI (diesel) engines — SI engines do not have a reliable linear relationship due to throttling losses
  • Gives $FP$ without stopping the engine (unlike motoring test)

4. Fuel Consumption & Thermal Efficiencies

4.1 Specific Fuel Consumption

$$BSFC = \frac{\dot{m}_f}{BP} \quad \text{(kg/kWh)}$$
$$ISFC = \frac{\dot{m}_f}{IP} \quad \text{(kg/kWh)}$$
  • Lower SFC = better fuel economy; typical BSFC for diesel ≈ 0.20–0.26 kg/kWh; petrol ≈ 0.27–0.35 kg/kWh

4.2 Thermal Efficiencies

$$\eta_{bth} = \frac{BP}{\dot{m}_f \cdot CV} \qquad \text{(brake thermal efficiency)}$$
$$\eta_{ith} = \frac{IP}{\dot{m}_f \cdot CV} \qquad \text{(indicated thermal efficiency)}$$
$$\eta_m = \frac{\eta_{bth}}{\eta_{ith}} = \frac{BP}{IP} \qquad \text{(mechanical efficiency links the two)}$$
  • $CV$ — calorific value (lower heating value) of fuel (kJ/kg)
  • $\dot{m}_f$ in kg/s for consistency with power in kW

5. Volumetric Efficiency & Firing Order

5.1 Volumetric Efficiency

$$\eta_v = \frac{m_{\text{actual}}}{m_{\text{theoretical}}} = \frac{V_{\text{actual suction}}}{V_s}$$
  • Reduced by: intake restriction, hot inlet manifold, residual exhaust gases, late valve opening
  • Improved by: oversquare engine design, low-restriction intake, high valve lift and duration, ram tuning, supercharging
  • Typical naturally aspirated engine: $\eta_v = 75$–90%

5.2 Injection Angle

$$\theta = 6 \cdot N \cdot t \quad \text{(degrees crank angle)}$$
  • $N$ — speed in rpm; $t$ — injection duration in seconds
  • At 3000 rpm, 1 ms of injection = $6 \times 3000 \times 0.001 = 18°$ of crank angle

5.3 Firing Order

Firing order determines the sequence in which cylinders fire. For a 4-cylinder engine it is typically 1-3-4-2 or 1-2-4-3. For a 6-cylinder engine: 1-5-3-6-2-4. The order is chosen to:

  • Balance crankshaft torsional loads and bending moments
  • Minimise vibration and smooth torque delivery
  • Prevent two adjacent cylinders firing consecutively (thermal and structural reasons)

Key Points to Remember

  • Turbocharger driven by exhaust turbine (waste energy) — not crankshaft. Mechanical supercharger driven by crankshaft (parasitic loss).
  • CI supercharging reduces knock; SI supercharging increases knock — opposite effects due to ignition mechanism.
  • IP = BP + FP. Mechanical efficiency $\eta_m = BP/IP$. Typical automotive: 75–90%.
  • Rope-brake dynamometer: $BP = (W-S)\pi D_m N / 60\,000$ kW. Effective drum diameter $D_m = D + d$ (rope diameter included).
  • Morse test: cut cylinders one at a time; $IP_i = BP_{\text{all}} - BP_{i\,\text{cut}}$. Requires multi-cylinder engine; FP assumed constant.
  • Willan's line: fuel vs BP at constant speed in CI engine. FP = negative BP intercept when $\dot{m}_f$ extrapolated to zero.
  • BSFC = $\dot{m}_f/BP$ (kg/kWh). Lower is better. Diesel typically 0.20–0.26; petrol 0.27–0.35.
  • Brake thermal efficiency $\eta_{bth} = BP/(\dot{m}_f \cdot CV)$; indicated thermal efficiency $\eta_{ith} = IP/(\dot{m}_f \cdot CV)$.
  • $\eta_m = \eta_{bth}/\eta_{ith}$: mechanical efficiency links brake and indicated thermal efficiencies.
  • Injection angle (degrees): $\theta = 6Nt$. At high speed, even short injection durations span many crank degrees.
  • 4-cylinder firing order 1-3-4-2 avoids adjacent cylinders firing consecutively; minimises crankshaft stress.
  • Volumetric efficiency typically 75–90% for NA engines. Supercharging can raise effective $\eta_v$ above 100% (based on atmospheric reference).
