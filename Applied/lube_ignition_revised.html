Lubrication, Cooling & Ignition Systems

1. Lubrication Systems in IC Engines

The lubricating oil in an engine reduces friction between moving parts, removes heat from bearing surfaces, seals piston rings against blow-by, and carries away wear debris. The type of lubrication system used depends on engine type, speed, power, and operating attitude.

1.1 Mist Lubrication

  • Oil (2–3% by volume) is premixed directly with petrol in the fuel tank
  • As petrol vaporises in the crankcase, oil droplets form a mist that coats cylinder walls and bearings
  • Used exclusively in 2-stroke petrol engines — the crankcase is used for intake charge scavenging and cannot hold an oil sump
  • Total-loss system: oil burns in the combustion chamber along with fuel

1.2 Splash Lubrication

  • Dippers (scoops) attached to connecting rod big-ends dip into an oil trough in the sump on each revolution, splashing oil onto cylinder walls and wrist pins
  • Suitable for low-speed, low-load engines (lawnmowers, stationary generators)
  • Inadequate for high-speed or heavily loaded engines (insufficient and uncontrolled delivery)

1.3 Pressure (Wet-Sump) Lubrication

$$P_{\text{oil}} = 0.4 \text{ to } 4.0\ \text{kg/cm}^2 \quad (40\text{ to }400\ \text{kPa})$$
  • An oil pump (gear or rotor type) draws oil from the crankcase sump and delivers it under pressure through drilled oil galleries to main bearings, big-end bearings, and camshaft bearings
  • Standard system for most automotive engines (cars, trucks, buses)
  • Oil pressure warning light activates if pressure drops below ~0.5 kg/cm²

1.4 Dry-Sump Lubrication

  • The crankcase is kept dry — one or more scavenge pumps continuously remove oil from the crankcase and return it to an external oil tank
  • A separate pressure pump draws from the external tank and feeds engine bearings
  • Requires at least two pumps: scavenge pump + pressure pump
  • Used in: aircraft engines, racing cars, sports cars, heavy-duty off-road vehicles — anywhere severe tilting, cornering, or acceleration could starve oil from a conventional sump

2. Lubrication Regimes & Stribeck Curve

The Stribeck curve plots the coefficient of friction $\mu$ against the Hersey number $\Lambda = \eta N/P$, and identifies four distinct lubrication regimes.

$$\Lambda = \frac{\eta \cdot N}{P}$$
  • $\eta$ — dynamic viscosity of oil (Pa·s); $N$ — rotational speed (rpm or rad/s); $P$ — load per unit bearing area (N/m²)
Regime$\Lambda$ valueFilm conditionFriction level
BoundaryLow (low speed, high load)No continuous film; metal-to-metal contact at asperitiesHighest friction; wear occurs
MixedIntermediatePartial fluid film; some asperity contactIntermediate; transition region
Hydrodynamic (full film)High (high speed, low load)Full separating fluid wedge; no metal contactLow; viscous shear only
HydrostaticAny (externally pressurised)Full film maintained by external pump regardless of shaft speedLowest at starting; no speed needed to generate film
  • Hydrostatic lubrication has the lowest starting friction — full film exists even at zero speed because pressure is supplied externally
  • Hydrodynamic lubrication: film builds up naturally from shaft rotation (wedge effect); minimum friction at optimum $\Lambda$; friction increases at very high speed due to viscous shear
  • Engine bearings operate mostly in hydrodynamic regime; boundary at start/stop

3. Lubricating Oil Viscosity & SAE Grades

Viscosity is the most important property of a lubricating oil. A thicker film carries higher loads but increases viscous drag. The SAE (Society of Automotive Engineers) viscosity classification assigns grades based on kinematic viscosity measured at 100°C.

$$\text{SAE } 80 > \text{SAE } 70 > \text{SAE } 40 > \text{SAE } 30 \quad \text{(viscosity ranking at }100°\text{C)}$$
  • Higher SAE number = thicker oil = higher viscosity = thicker protective film
  • Monograde oils (SAE 30, SAE 40): single-grade; viscosity changes greatly with temperature
  • Multigrade oils (SAE 10W-40, 15W-50): viscosity modifiers keep oil from thinning too much at high $T$ or thickening too much at low $T$; 'W' denotes winter/cold-start grade
  • Viscosity decreases with temperature (for oils); higher operating temperature → thinner oil → risk of film breakdown

4. Cooling System

Engine cooling removes excess heat from combustion to prevent overheating of components while keeping the engine warm enough for efficient combustion and low friction.

4.1 Thermostat

$$T_{\text{thermostat}} = 80°\text{C to }85°\text{C} \quad \text{(standard automotive opening range)}$$
  • Below thermostat opening temperature: coolant circulates only within the engine block (short circuit); no flow to radiator — engine warms up quickly
  • Above opening temperature: thermostat opens; hot coolant flows to radiator for heat rejection
  • Operating too cold (thermostat stuck open): condensation, corrosion, higher fuel consumption, rough running
  • Operating too hot (thermostat stuck closed): overheating, detonation, seizure risk

4.2 Effect of Cooling-Water Temperature on SI Knocking

Higher coolant temperature → hotter cylinder walls → hotter charge → higher end-gas temperature → SI knocking increases. This is why an overheated engine is more prone to detonation.

  • Note: in CI engines, higher coolant (wall) temperature shortens ignition delay → reduces diesel knock
  • SI and CI effects are opposite for coolant temperature on knocking

4.3 Choke Valve (Cold Starting Aid — SI Engines)

  • Choke valve restricts air flow at the carburettor inlet to create a rich (low AFR) mixture for cold starting
  • Rich mixture needed because fuel vaporisation is poor at low temperatures — excess fuel compensates for poor atomisation
  • Manual choke: driver-operated cable; automatic choke: bimetallic spring opens progressively as engine warms
  • Modern fuel-injected engines: ECU enriches fuel delivery directly — no physical choke valve

5. Ignition Systems

5.1 Battery-Coil (Conventional / Electronic) Ignition

  • Energy source: 12V vehicle battery; coil steps voltage up to 15,000–30,000 V
  • Primary circuit (low voltage) is interrupted by contact breaker points (older) or electronic trigger (modern)
  • Magnetic field collapse in coil primary induces high-voltage pulse in secondary winding → spark at plug
  • Distributor routes high-voltage pulse to correct cylinder in firing order
  • Modern versions: distributorless ignition (DIS), direct ignition (coil-on-plug)
  • Advantage: consistent spark energy at all speeds; adjustable timing
  • Disadvantage: depends on battery; fails if battery discharged

5.2 Magneto Ignition

  • Self-contained unit: permanent magnets rotate to generate high-voltage ignition pulse — completely independent of battery
  • Generates its own electricity; no external power source required
  • Advantage: independent of vehicle electrical system; reliable at high speeds; essential for aircraft and racing engines
  • Disadvantage: weaker spark at low speed/start; heavier; more expensive

Key Points to Remember

  • Mist lubrication: 2–3% oil in petrol; only for 2-stroke petrol engines; total-loss (oil burns with fuel).
  • Dry-sump: always requires two pumps — scavenge pump (empties crankcase) + pressure pump (feeds bearings from external tank).
  • Pressure wet-sump oil pressure: 0.4–4.0 kg/cm² (40–400 kPa). Standard for most automobiles.
  • Stribeck parameter: $\Lambda = \eta N/P$. Increasing $\Lambda$ progresses from boundary → mixed → hydrodynamic regime.
  • Hydrostatic lubrication: lowest starting friction; full film at zero speed (externally pressurised). Hydrodynamic: full film only when shaft rotates (wedge effect).
  • SAE viscosity: SAE 80 > SAE 70 > SAE 40 > SAE 30 (higher number = thicker oil at 100°C).
  • Oil viscosity decreases with temperature. Multigrade oils (e.g. 10W-40) maintain performance across a wide temperature range.
  • Thermostat opens at 80°C–85°C. Below: short-circuit loop (fast warm-up). Above: full radiator loop (cooling).
  • Higher cooling-water temperature → SI knock increases. Higher coolant temperature → CI knock decreases. Opposite effects.
  • Choke valve richens the SI mixture for cold starting by restricting inlet air flow.
  • Battery ignition: uses 12V battery + induction coil → 15,000–30,000 V at plug. Distributor routes to cylinders.
  • Magneto ignition: self-contained; no battery needed; better at high speed; used in aircraft, racing engines.
  • Magneto preferred where independence from vehicle electrical system is essential (aircraft, racing, small engines).
