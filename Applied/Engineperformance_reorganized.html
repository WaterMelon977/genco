SECTION 2: QUICK REFERENCE SHEET

2.1 — All Formulas (with symbols & units)

1. Supercharging & Boost Density

  • Charge Density Ratio:
    $${\boxed{\frac{\rho_{\text{boost}}}{\rho_{\text{atm}}} = \left(\frac{P_{\text{boost}}}{P_{\text{atm}}}\right) \left(\frac{T_{\text{atm}}}{T_{\text{boost}}}\right)}}$$
  • $\rho$: intake charge density $\text{kg/m}^3$
  • $P$: absolute pressure ($\text{kPa}$ or $\text{bar}$)
  • $T$: absolute temperature $\text{K}$

2. Engine Powers & Mechanical Efficiency

  • Power Balance:
    $${\boxed{IP = BP + FP \implies FP = IP - BP}}$$
  • $IP$: Indicated Power developed inside cylinder $\text{kW}$
  • $BP$: Brake Power delivered at crankshaft $\text{kW}$

  • $FP$: Friction Power lost to friction, pumping, and auxiliaries $\text{kW}$

  • Mechanical Efficiency $\eta_m$:

    $${\boxed{\eta_m = \frac{BP}{IP} = \frac{IP - FP}{IP} = 1 - \frac{FP}{IP}}}$$

3. Power & Torque Formulas

  • Indicated Power $IP$ for 4-Stroke Engine:
    $${\boxed{IP = \frac{P_{mi} \cdot L \cdot A \cdot N \cdot K}{120 \times 1000} \quad (\text{kW})}}$$
  • $P_{mi}$: indicated mean effective pressure ($\text{N/m}^2$ or $\text{Pa}$; $1 \text{ bar} = 10^5 \text{ N/m}^2$)
  • $L$: stroke length $\text{m}$
  • $A$: cylinder cross-sectional area $\text{m}^2 = \frac{\pi D^2}{4}$
  • $N$: crankshaft rotational speed $\text{rpm}$

  • $K$: number of cylinders

  • Brake Power $BP$ from Torque:

    $${\boxed{BP = \frac{2 \pi N T}{60 \times 1000} = \frac{N T}{9549} \quad (\text{kW})}}$$

  • $T$: brake torque $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$

  • Rope-Brake Dynamometer Brake Power $BP$:

    $${\boxed{BP = \frac{(W - S) \cdot \pi D_m \cdot N}{60 \times 1000} \quad (\text{kW})}}$$

  • $W$: net dead weight applied $\text{N}$
  • $S$: spring balance reading $\text{N}$
  • $D_m = D + d$: effective brake drum diameter $\text{m}$
  • $D$: brake drum diameter $\text{m}$
  • $d$: rope diameter $\text{m}$

4. Testing Methods (Morse Test & Willan's Line)

  • Morse Test (Multi-Cylinder $IP$ Determination):
    $${\boxed{IP_i = BP_{\text{all}} - BP_{i \text{ cut-out}}}}$$
  • $IP_i$: indicated power of cylinder $i$ $\text{kW}$
  • $BP_{\text{all}}$: total brake power with all cylinders firing $\text{kW}$

  • $BP_{i \text{ cut-out}}$: brake power with cylinder $i$ cut off $\text{kW}$

  • Total Indicated Power $IP_{\text{total}}$:

    $${\boxed{IP_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^K IP_i}}$$
    $${\boxed{\eta_m = \frac{BP_{\text{all}}}{IP_{\text{total}}}}}$$

  • Willan's Line Method (CI Engine $FP$ Determination):

    $${\boxed{FP = \text{Negative intercept on } BP \text{ axis at zero fuel consumption } (\dot{m}_f = 0)}}$$

5. Fuel Consumption & Thermal Efficiencies

  • Brake Specific Fuel Consumption $BSFC$:
    $${\boxed{BSFC = \frac{\dot{m}_f}{BP} \quad (\text{kg/kWh})}}$$
  • $\dot{m}_f$: fuel mass consumption rate $\text{kg/h}$

  • $BP$: brake power $\text{kW}$

  • Indicated Thermal Efficiency $\eta_{\text{ith}}$:

    $${\boxed{\eta_{\text{ith}} = \frac{IP}{\dot{m}_f \cdot CV} = \frac{IP (\text{kW})}{\frac{\dot{m}_f (\text{kg/h})}{3600} \cdot CV (\text{kJ/kg})}}}$$

  • $CV$: lower heating value / calorific value of fuel $\text{kJ/kg}$

  • Brake Thermal Efficiency $\eta_{\text{bth}}$:

    $${\boxed{\eta_{\text{bth}} = \frac{BP}{\dot{m}f \cdot CV} = \eta_m \cdot \eta{\text{ith}}}}$$

6. Volumetric Efficiency & Injection Kinematics

  • Volumetric Efficiency $\eta_{\text{vol}}$:
    $${\boxed{\eta_{\text{vol}} = \frac{\dot{m}{\text{air, actual}}}{\rho{\text{inlet}} \cdot V_s \cdot \frac{N}{120} \cdot K}}}$$

  • Standard range for naturally aspirated SI engines: $71\% \text{ to } 90\%$ (typically $80\% - 85\%$).

  • Injection Crank Angle $\theta$:

    $${\boxed{\theta = 6 \cdot N \cdot t \quad (\text{degrees})}}$$

  • $N$: engine speed $\text{rpm}$
  • $t$: injection duration $\text{seconds}$

2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Supercharging: Forcing air or air-fuel mixture into an engine cylinder at a pressure higher than atmospheric density, increasing mass of inducted charge and boosting power output.
  • Turbocharging: Supercharging where the compressor is driven by a turbine powered by engine exhaust gases.
  • Indicated Power $IP$: Total theoretical power generated inside the engine cylinders by high-pressure combustion gas acting directly on the piston.
  • Brake Power $BP$: Useful mechanical power output measured at the engine crankshaft via a dynamometer.
  • Friction Power $FP$: Mechanical power lost internally due to piston/bearing friction, pumping work, and driving auxiliaries $FP = IP - BP$.
  • Mechanical Efficiency $\eta_m$: Ratio of useful brake power delivered at the crankshaft to total indicated power generated in cylinders $\eta_m = BP/IP$.
  • Morse Test: A diagnostic test performed exclusively on multi-cylinder engines at constant speed to determine individual cylinder $IP$ and total $FP$ without using an indicator diagram.
  • Willan's Line Method: Graphical extrapolation technique plotting fuel consumption rate against $BP$ at constant speed to find Friction Power $FP$ for unthrottled CI engines.
  • Brake Specific Fuel Consumption $BSFC$: Mass of fuel consumed per hour per kilowatt of brake power developed $\text{kg/kWh}$. Measures fuel economy.
  • Rope-Brake Dynamometer: An absorption dynamometer measuring brake power by applying friction torque via a weighted rope wrapped around a brake drum.
  • Firing Order: The specific sequential order in which power strokes occur in multi-cylinder engines to minimize engine vibration and balance crankshaft forces ($1-3-4-2$ for inline 4-cylinder).

2.3 — Standard Values & Physical Constants

  • Inline 4-Cylinder Standard Firing Order: $1 - 3 - 4 - 2$.
  • Inline 6-Cylinder Standard Firing Order: $1 - 5 - 3 - 6 - 2 - 4$.
  • Naturally Aspirated SI Volumetric Efficiency: $\eta_{\text{vol}} = 71\% \text{ to } 90\%$.
  • Crankshaft Angular Velocity Relation: $1 \text{ rpm} = 6^\circ \text{ per second}$.

SECTION 3: MAIN BODY

3.1 Supercharging & Turbocharging Principles

1. Concept Pointers
  • Supercharging increases the mass density of the intake charge entering the cylinder, allowing more fuel to be burned and raising power output for a given cylinder size.
  • Supercharger vs Turbocharger:
  • Mechanical Supercharger: Compressor driven directly by engine crankshaft via belt/gears (draws mechanical power from engine).
  • Turbocharger: Compressor driven by a turbine expanding hot exhaust gases (uses waste exhaust energy).
  • CI vs SI Engine Suitability:
  • CI (Diesel) Engines: SUPERCHARGING IS HIGHLY PREFERRED! Raising intake pressure and temperature SHORTENS ignition delay, reduces knocking tendency, improves combustion, and permits higher power.
  • SI (Petrol) Engines: Supercharging is SEVERELY LIMITED by knocking/detonation! Increasing intake temperature and pressure increases end-gas auto-ignition tendency.
  • Compression raises air temperature, which lowers density; therefore, an Intercooler is added after the supercharger to maximize density gain.
FeatureMechanical SuperchargerTurbocharger
DriveCrankshaft belt/gear drive — parasitic power lossExhaust turbine — uses waste exhaust energy; no direct crankshaft load
ResponseImmediate (no lag)Turbo lag at low RPM
Typical useRacing engines, roots-type on large SI enginesMost modern diesel and petrol production engines
💡 Key Concept: Supercharging Knock Tendency: - CI (Diesel) Engine $\implies$ Supercharging REDUCES knocking! - SI (Petrol) Engine $\implies$ Supercharging INCREASES knocking!
⚠️ Exam Trap: Turbocharger Drive Source: A turbocharger compressor is driven by an EXHAUST TURBINE, NOT by the engine crankshaft! A mechanical supercharger is driven by the crankshaft.
2. Decision Tree
Supercharging Engine Application Check?
├─ Is engine a Compression-Ignition (Diesel) engine?
│  ├─ YES → Supercharging PREFERRED (Reduces ignition delay, reduces knock, boosts power)
│  └─ NO → Spark-Ignition (Petrol) engine
│     └─ Supercharging LIMITED by knocking/detonation (Requires higher Octane fuel)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Supercharging increases intake charge density.
  • [ ] Turbocharger uses exhaust gas turbine drive.
  • [ ] Diesel supercharging reduces knocking.
  • [ ] Petrol supercharging increases knocking risk.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: Air enters a supercharger at $P_1 = 1.0 \text{ bar}$ and $T_1 = 300 \text{ K}$. It is compressed to $P_2 = 1.5 \text{ bar}$ and temperature rises to $T_2 = 330 \text{ K}$. Calculate the ratio of supercharged density to atmospheric density. - Solution:

$${\frac{\rho_{\text{boost}}}{\rho_{\text{atm}}} = \left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right) = \left(\frac{1.5}{1.0}\right) \left(\frac{300}{330}\right) = 1.5 \times 0.9091 = 1.3636}$$
- Key Insight: Density increases by $36.36\%$ (boosted ratio $= 1.364$). Temperature rise slightly offsets pressure ratio gain.

3.2 Engine Powers & Mechanical Efficiency

1. Concept Pointers
  • Indicated Power $IP$: Power generated inside combustion chambers $IP = BP + FP$.
  • Brake Power $BP$: Power output at crankshaft available for useful work.
  • Friction Power $FP$: Mechanical power lost to piston friction, bearing friction, pumping loops, and driving accessories $FP = IP - BP$.
  • Mechanical Efficiency $\eta_m$: $\eta_m = \frac{BP}{IP} = \frac{BP}{BP + FP}$.
  • Friction power $FP$ depends primarily on engine speed $N$ and remains virtually CONSTANT across different engine loads at a fixed speed!
  • $IP > BP$ ALWAYS; Mechanical efficiency $\eta_m$ is ALWAYS less than $100\%$.
PowerDefinitionMeasured by
Indicated Power (IP)Power developed inside combustion chamber by gas pressure on pistonP-V indicator diagram; indicator card
Brake Power (BP)Net useful power available at crankshaft after friction lossesDynamometer (rope brake, eddy current, hydraulic)
Friction Power (FP)Power lost to piston friction, bearing friction, pumping, and accessoriesMotoring method; FP = IP − BP
💡 Key Concept: Constant FP Assumption: At a constant engine speed $N$, Friction Power $FP$ remains essentially CONSTANT regardless of load variation. This key principle forms the basis of both the Morse Test and Willan's Line Method.
⚠️ Exam Trap: Efficiency Ratio Ordering: Mechanical efficiency $\eta_m = \frac{BP}{IP}$. NEVER invert this ratio as $\frac{IP}{BP}$! Since $IP > BP$, $\eta_m$ is always $< 1.0$.
2. Decision Tree
Engine Power Balance Check?
├─ Power Balance Formula → IP = BP + FP
├─ Mechanical Efficiency Formula → eta_m = BP / IP = (IP - FP) / IP
└─ Constant Speed Rule → FP remains constant when load changes at fixed RPM
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $IP = BP + FP$.
  • [ ] $\eta_m = BP / IP$.
  • [ ] $FP$ is constant at fixed RPM.
  • [ ] $IP > BP$ always.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: An engine develops an Indicated Power $IP = 125 \text{ kW}$ and operates at a mechanical efficiency $\eta_m = 80\%$. Calculate its Brake Power $BP$ and Friction Power $FP$. - Solution:

$${BP = \eta_m \times IP = 0.80 \times 125 = 100 \text{ kW}}$$
$${FP = IP - BP = 125 - 100 = 25 \text{ kW}}$$
- Key Insight: $BP = 100 \text{ kW}$, $FP = 25 \text{ kW}$.

3.3 Mean Effective Pressure & Power Calculations

1. Concept Pointers
  • Indicated Mean Effective Pressure $P_{mi}$: Average pressure acting on piston during power stroke that yields net cycle work.
    $${P_{mi} = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{V_s}}$$
  • Power Formulas:
  • $IP = \frac{P_{mi} \cdot L \cdot A \cdot N \cdot K}{120 \times 1000} \text{ kW}$ (for 4-stroke engines, denominator $= 120 \times 1000$).
  • $IP = \frac{P_{mi} \cdot L \cdot A \cdot N \cdot K}{60 \times 1000} \text{ kW}$ (for 2-stroke engines, denominator $= 60 \times 1000$).
  • Rope-Brake Dynamometer:
    $${BP = \frac{(W - S) \cdot \pi \cdot D_m \cdot N}{60000} \quad (\text{kW})}$$
    Where effective brake diameter $D_m = D + d$ $D = \text{drum diameter}, d = \text{rope diameter}$.
💡 Key Concept: 4-Stroke Denominator Rule: For 4-stroke engines, power strokes occur at $\frac{N}{2}$ strokes per minute. Thus, the denominator includes $60 \times 2 = 120$!
⚠️ Exam Trap: Rope Diameter Addition: In rope-brake calculations, ALWAYS add rope diameter $d$ to drum diameter $D$ ($D_m = D + d$). Forgetting $d$ causes a calculation error in effective torque radius.
2. Decision Tree
Engine Power Calculation?
├─ Is engine 4-stroke or 2-stroke?
│  ├─ 4-Stroke IP → IP = (P_m * L * A * N * K) / (120 * 1000) [kW]
│  └─ 2-Stroke IP → IP = (P_m * L * A * N * K) / (60 * 1000) [kW]
└─ Rope-Brake BP → BP = [(W - S) * pi * (D + d) * N] / 60000 [kW]
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] 4-stroke denominator $= 120000$.
  • [ ] 2-stroke denominator $= 60000$.
  • [ ] Rope brake $D_m = D + d$.
  • [ ] $1 \text{ bar} = 10^5 \text{ N/m}^2$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A single-cylinder 4-stroke engine has a bore $D = 0.10 \text{ m}$, stroke $L = 0.12 \text{ m}$, runs at $N = 1500 \text{ rpm}$, and produces $P_{mi} = 6 \text{ bar}$ $6 \times 10^5 \text{ N/m}^2$. Calculate Indicated Power $IP$. - Solution:

$${A = \frac{\pi}{4} D^2 = \frac{\pi}{4} (0.10)^2 = 0.007854 \text{ m}^2}$$
$${IP = \frac{P_{mi} \cdot L \cdot A \cdot N \cdot K}{120000} = \frac{(6 \times 10^5) \times 0.12 \times 0.007854 \times 1500 \times 1}{120000}}$$
$${IP = \frac{848.23 \times 10^3}{120000} = 7.068 \text{ kW} \approx 7.07 \text{ kW}}$$
- Key Insight: Convert pressure to $\text{N/m}^2$ $6 \times 10^5$ and use $120000$ in denominator.

3.4 Testing Methods — Morse Test & Willan's Line Method

1. Concept Pointers
  • Morse Test:
  • Used ONLY for Multi-Cylinder engines (both SI and CI).
  • Engine is run at CONSTANT SPEED $N$.
  • Cutting off spark/fuel to cylinder $1$ drops total brake power from $BP_{\text{all}}$ to $BP_1$.
  • The drop in brake power equals the Indicated Power of cylinder $1$:
    $${IP_1 = BP_{\text{all}} - BP_1}$$
  • Total $IP = IP_1 + IP_2 + \dots + IP_K$.
  • Friction Power $FP = IP_{\text{total}} - BP_{\text{all}}$.
  • CANNOT be performed on a single-cylinder engine!
  • Willan's Line Method:
  • Graphical method used for Single-Cylinder CI (Diesel) engines at CONSTANT SPEED.
  • Plots fuel consumption rate ($\dot{m}_f$ in $\text{kg/h}$) vs Brake Power $BP$.
  • Extrapolating the straight line to zero fuel consumption $\dot{m}_f = 0$ gives a negative intercept on the $BP$ axis equal to Friction Power $FP$.
  • NOT reliable for SI engines because petrol fuel consumption curves are non-linear (curved).
💡 Key Concept: Testing Method Selection: - Multi-Cylinder Engine $IP$ Determination $\implies$ Morse Test - Single-Cylinder CI Engine $FP$ Determination $\implies$ Willan's Line Method
⚠️ Exam Trap: Morse Test Assumption Trap: Morse test assumes Friction Power $FP$ of individual cylinders stays CONSTANT when a cylinder is cut out. This assumption holds ONLY IF engine speed $N$ is brought back to original value by reducing load on the dynamometer!
2. Decision Tree
Testing Method Identification?
├─ Multi-Cylinder Engine IP Test → MORSE TEST (Cut one cylinder at a time at const RPM)
│  └─ Formula → IP_i = BP_all - BP_i_cutout, IP_total = Sum(IP_i)
└─ Single-Cylinder CI Engine FP Test → WILLAN'S LINE METHOD (Extrapolate fuel rate vs BP to zero fuel)
   └─ Result → Negative BP intercept = Friction Power FP
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Morse test for multi-cylinder engines only.
  • [ ] $IP_1 = BP_{\text{all}} - BP_1$.
  • [ ] Willan's line for single-cylinder CI engines.
  • [ ] Willan's line negative $BP$ intercept $= FP$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A 4-cylinder 4-stroke engine running at constant speed produces $BP_{\text{all}} = 30 \text{ kW}$. When cylinders 1, 2, 3, and 4 are cut out individually, the remaining brake powers measured are $21.5 \text{ kW}$, $21.0 \text{ kW}$, $21.8 \text{ kW}$, and $21.7 \text{ kW}$ respectively. Calculate total $IP$, $FP$, and mechanical efficiency $\eta_m$. - Solution:

$${IP_1 = 30 - 21.5 = 8.5 \text{ kW}}$$
$${IP_2 = 30 - 21.0 = 9.0 \text{ kW}}$$
$${IP_3 = 30 - 21.8 = 8.2 \text{ kW}}$$
$${IP_4 = 30 - 21.7 = 8.3 \text{ kW}}$$
$${IP_{\text{total}} = 8.5 + 9.0 + 8.2 + 8.3 = 34.0 \text{ kW}}$$
$${FP = IP_{\text{total}} - BP_{\text{all}} = 34.0 - 30.0 = 4.0 \text{ kW}}$$
$${\eta_m = \frac{BP_{\text{all}}}{IP_{\text{total}}} = \frac{30.0}{34.0} = 0.8824 = 88.24\%}$$
- Key Insight: $IP_{\text{total}} = 34.0 \text{ kW}$, $FP = 4.0 \text{ kW}$, $\eta_m = 88.24\%$.

3.5 Fuel Consumption, Thermal Efficiencies & Volumetric Efficiency

1. Concept Pointers
  • Brake Specific Fuel Consumption $BSFC$:
    $${BSFC = \frac{\dot{m}_f}{BP} \quad (\text{kg/kWh})}$$
    Lower $BSFC$ indicates superior fuel economy. Diesel engines have lower $BSFC$ than petrol engines.
  • Thermal Efficiencies:
  • Indicated Thermal Efficiency $\eta_{\text{ith}} = \frac{IP}{\dot{m}_f \cdot CV}$.
  • Brake Thermal Efficiency $\eta_{\text{bth}} = \frac{BP}{\dot{m}f \cdot CV} = \eta_m \cdot \eta{\text{ith}}$.
  • Volumetric Efficiency $\eta_{\text{vol}}$:
  • Measures mass of air inducted relative to swept volume capacity.
  • Standard range for SI engines: $71\% \text{ to } 90\%$.
  • Supercharging INCREASES $\eta_{\text{vol}}$; higher intake air temperature DECREASES $\eta_{\text{vol}}$ (lower density).
💡 Key Concept: BSFC Unit Conversion: In $BSFC = \dot{m}_f / BP$, fuel flow $\dot{m}_f$ MUST be in $\text{kg/h}$ and $BP$ in $\text{kW}$ to yield standard units of $\text{kg/kWh}$.
2. Decision Tree
Fuel Economy & Efficiency Calculation?
├─ BSFC Formula → BSFC = m_dot_f (kg/h) / BP (kW) [kg/kWh]
├─ Thermal Efficiency → eta_bth = BP / (m_dot_f * CV)
└─ Volumetric Efficiency Trend → Supercharging raises eta_vol, Intake heating lowers eta_vol
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] $BSFC = \dot{m}_f / BP$ $\text{kg/kWh}$.
  • [ ] $\eta_{\text{bth}} = \eta_m \cdot \eta_{\text{ith}}$.
  • [ ] Diesel has lower $BSFC$ than petrol.
  • [ ] Intake heating lowers $\eta_{\text{vol}}$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: An engine produces $BP = 20 \text{ kW}$ while consuming fuel at a rate of $\dot{m}f = 5 \text{ kg/h}$. Fuel calorific value $CV = 42000 \text{ kJ/kg}$. Calculate $BSFC$ and Brake Thermal Efficiency $\eta{\text{bth}}$. - Solution:

$${BSFC = \frac{\dot{m}f}{BP} = \frac{5}{20} = 0.25 \text{ kg/kWh}}$$
$${\dot{m}_f (\text{kg/s}) = \frac{5}{3600} = 0.001389 \text{ kg/s}}$$
$${\eta{\text{bth}} = \frac{BP}{\dot{m}f \cdot CV} = \frac{20}{0.001389 \times 42000} = \frac{20}{58.333} = 0.3428 = 34.28\%}$$
- Key Insight: $BSFC = 0.25 \text{ kg/kWh}$, $\eta{\text{bth}} = 34.28\%$. Convert $\dot{m}_f$ to $\text{kg/s}$ for thermal efficiency.

3.6 Firing Order & Injection Angle Kinematics

1. Concept Pointers
  • Firing Order:
  • The sequence in which cylinders execute their power strokes.
  • Standard Inline 4-Cylinder Firing Order: 1-3-4-2 (or 1-2-4-3). Avoids adjacent cylinder firing to minimize primary force imbalances and crankshaft torsional vibration.
  • Standard Inline 6-Cylinder Firing Order: 1-5-3-6-2-4.
  • Injection Angle Kinematics:
  • Engine rotates at $N \text{ rpm} \implies \omega = N \times \frac{360^\circ}{60 \text{ s}} = 6 N \text{ degrees per second}$.
  • Crank angle rotated during time duration $t$:
    $${\theta = 6 \cdot N \cdot t \quad (\text{degrees})}$$
💡 Key Concept: Crank Angle Rotation Rate: At speed $N \text{ rpm}$, the crankshaft rotates by $6 \times N$ degrees every second!
2. Decision Tree
Firing Order & Kinematic Check?
├─ Inline 4-Cylinder Firing Order → 1-3-4-2 (Balances primary forces & torsional vibration)
├─ Inline 6-Cylinder Firing Order → 1-5-3-6-2-4
└─ Crank Angle Injection Calculation → theta (deg) = 6 * N (rpm) * t (seconds)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] 4-cyl firing order: $1-3-4-2$.
  • [ ] 6-cyl firing order: $1-5-3-6-2-4$.
  • [ ] Rotation rate $= 6N \text{ deg/sec}$.
  • [ ] $\theta = 6Nt$.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 - Problem: A 4-stroke Diesel engine runs at $N = 2500 \text{ rpm}$. The fuel injection duration is $t = 2 \text{ ms}$ $0.002 \text{ s}$. Calculate the crank angle $\theta$ turned during injection. - Solution:

$${\theta = 6 \cdot N \cdot t = 6 \times 2500 \times 0.002 = 15000 \times 0.002 = 30^\circ}$$
- Key Insight: $\theta = 30^\circ$ crank angle. Direct application of $\theta = 6Nt$.

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (Core Conceptual)

Q1: What is supercharging and what is its primary objective?
A: Supercharging is the process of supplying intake air to engine cylinders at a density higher than atmospheric, increasing the mass of oxygen inducted per stroke to burn more fuel and boost engine power output.

Q2: Why is supercharging preferred for Diesel (CI) engines but limited in Petrol (SI) engines?
A: Supercharging increases intake pressure and temperature. In CI engines, this reduces ignition delay and DECREASES knocking tendency. In SI engines, higher pressure/temperature increases end-gas auto-ignition and INCREASES knocking tendency.

Q3: Differentiate between a mechanical supercharger and a turbocharger.
A: A mechanical supercharger is driven by the engine crankshaft via gears or belts (drawing mechanical power). A turbocharger is driven by a turbine expanding waste exhaust gases.

Q4: Define Indicated Power $IP$, Brake Power $BP$, and Friction Power $FP$.
A: $IP$ is power developed inside cylinders by combustion gas. $BP$ is useful power delivered at crankshaft. $FP = IP - BP$ is power lost to mechanical friction, pumping, and accessories.

Q5: State the formula for Mechanical Efficiency $\eta_m$.
A: $\eta_m = \frac{BP}{IP} = \frac{IP - FP}{IP}$.

Q6: Write the Indicated Power $IP$ formula for a 4-stroke multi-cylinder engine.
A: $IP = \frac{P_{mi} \cdot L \cdot A \cdot N \cdot K}{120000} \text{ kW}$, where $P_{mi}$ is in $\text{N/m}^2$.

Q7: How is Brake Power $BP$ measured using a rope-brake dynamometer?
A: $BP = \frac{(W - S) \cdot \pi D_m N}{60000} \text{ kW}$, where $D_m = D + d$ is the effective brake drum diameter.

Q8: Describe the Morse Test and state its primary application.
A: The Morse Test is a method used on multi-cylinder engines to determine individual cylinder $IP$ and total $FP$ by cutting off spark/fuel to one cylinder at a time at constant speed. $IP_i = BP_{\text{all}} - BP_{i \text{ cut-out}}$.

Q9: Why can the Morse Test NOT be performed on a single-cylinder engine?
A: Cutting out the single cylinder stops the engine completely, producing zero brake power and making power drop comparison impossible.

Q10: Describe Willan's Line Method.
A: A graphical method for single-cylinder CI engines at constant speed that plots fuel rate $\dot{m}_f$ vs $BP$. Extrapolating the straight line to zero fuel rate yields a negative $BP$ intercept equal to Friction Power $FP$.

Q11: Why is Willan's Line Method unsuitable for SI engines?
A: Petrol engine fuel consumption curves are non-linear due to throttling and variable air-fuel ratio characteristics.

Q12: Define Brake Specific Fuel Consumption $BSFC$.
A: $BSFC = \frac{\dot{m}_f}{BP} \text{ kg/kWh}$, the mass of fuel consumed per hour per kilowatt of brake power.

Q13: What is the typical volumetric efficiency range for naturally aspirated SI engines?
A: $71\% \text{ to } 90\%$ (typically $80\% - 85\%$).

Q14: What is the standard firing order for an inline 4-cylinder engine and why is it used?
A: $1 - 3 - 4 - 2$. It balances primary inertia forces and minimizes crankshaft torsional vibration.

Q15: State the formula connecting crank angle rotation $\theta$ with engine speed $N$ and time $t$.
A: $\theta = 6 \cdot N \cdot t \text{ degrees}$.

SECTION 5: CONSOLIDATION NOTES & HIGH-YIELD EXAM TRAPS

  • CI vs SI Supercharging: CI supercharging REDUCES knock. SI supercharging INCREASES knock.
  • Turbo Drive: Turbocharger is driven by EXHAUST GAS TURBINE, NOT crankshaft.
  • Rope-Brake Diameter: Effective diameter $D_m = D + d$ (Add rope thickness $d$ to drum $D$).
  • 4-Stroke IP Denominator: Use $120000$ (since power stroke occurs every $2$ revolutions).
  • Morse vs Willan's:
  • Morse Test $\implies$ Multi-Cylinder engines (SI or CI).
  • Willan's Line $\implies$ Single-Cylinder CI engines ONLY.
  • Firing Order: Inline 4-cylinder $= 1-3-4-2$.
