Knocking, Fuel Rating & Combustion Chamber Design

1. SI Engine Knocking (Detonation)

Normal combustion in a petrol engine begins at the spark plug and the flame front propagates across the cylinder at 20–30 m/s. As the flame advances, it compresses and heats the remaining unburned charge — the end-gas. If the end-gas temperature exceeds its auto-ignition temperature before the flame arrives, it ignites spontaneously and explosively. This generates a pressure wave (shock wave) travelling at 1000–2000 m/s — producing the characteristic metallic ping of detonation.

1.1 Detonation vs Pre-Ignition

FeatureDetonation (SI Knock)Pre-Ignition
Timing relative to sparkOccurs after spark plug firesOccurs before spark plug fires
CauseEnd-gas auto-ignition due to high temperature/pressureHot spot (carbon deposit, overheated valve/plug electrode) ignites mixture early
LocationFarthest end-gas from spark plugAt the hot-spot surface; can be anywhere
Wave speedDetonation wave: 1000–2000 m/sNormal flame propagation: 20–30 m/s

$$\tau_{id} \propto \frac{1}{P^n \cdot e^{-E/(RT)}} \qquad \text{(ignition delay of end-gas)}$$
  • Shorter $\tau_{id}$ → more likely to knock. Increased $P$ or $T$ shortens $\tau_{id}$.
  • HUCR (Highest Useful Compression Ratio) = maximum $r$ at which the engine runs without knock on a given fuel

2. CI Engine Knocking (Diesel Knock)

Diesel knock occurs at the start of combustion — opposite to SI knock (which occurs near the end). Fuel is injected into hot compressed air near TDC. There is a delay between injection and ignition called the ignition delay period:

$$\tau_{id} = \tau_{\text{physical}} + \tau_{\text{chemical}}$$
  • $\tau_{\text{physical}}$: time for atomisation, heating, vaporisation, and mixing
  • $\tau_{\text{chemical}}$: time for pre-flame oxidation reactions

If $\tau_{id}$ is long, a large quantity of fuel accumulates before ignition. When ignition finally occurs, all accumulated fuel burns simultaneously — causing a very rapid pressure rise ($dP/d\theta > 4$–5 bar/°CA) and severe diesel knock.

Factor changeEffect on CI knock
Cetane number ↑Knock decreases (shorter $\tau_{id}$, less fuel accumulation)
Compression ratio ↑Knock decreases (higher air temp shortens $\tau_{id}$)
Intake air temperature ↑Knock decreases
Injection timing advancedKnock increases (longer exposure before ignition at lower pressure)
Injection pressure ↑Knock decreases (finer atomisation → shorter physical delay)
Supercharging ↑Knock decreases (higher compression temperature)

3. Fuel Rating Systems

3.1 Octane Number (SI Fuel Rating)

The octane number measures a fuel's resistance to detonation (knocking). It is defined as the percentage by volume of iso-octane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane) in a blend with n-heptane that produces the same knock intensity as the test fuel in a CFR engine.

$$\text{ON} = \%\text{ iso-octane by volume in iso-octane/n-heptane blend matching test knock}$$
$$\text{AKI} = \frac{\text{RON} + \text{MON}}{2} \qquad \text{(pump octane number, US)}$$
  • iso-octane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane): ON = 100 (highest knock resistance)
  • n-heptane: ON = 0 (worst knock resistance)
  • RON (Research Octane Number): mild test conditions (lower engine speed)
  • MON (Motor Octane Number): severe test conditions (higher speed, higher temperature)
  • Sensitivity = RON − MON; positive for most fuels

Hydrocarbon Detonation Tendency (Increasing SI knock tendency)

$$\text{Aromatics} < \text{Naphthenes} < \text{Olefins} < \text{Paraffins (n-alkanes)}$$
  • Straight-chain paraffins (n-alkanes) knock most easily; branched chains and aromatics resist knock
  • Adding lead tetraethyl (TEL), MTBE, or ethanol increases octane number

3.2 Cetane Number (CI Fuel Rating)

The cetane number measures a fuel's ease of ignition (readiness to auto-ignite). It is defined as the percentage of n-cetane (n-hexadecane) in a blend with α-methylnaphthalene (or heptamethylnonane, CN = 15) that matches the test fuel's ignition delay.

  • n-cetane (n-hexadecane): CN = 100 (shortest ignition delay; best CI fuel)
  • α-methylnaphthalene: CN = 0; heptamethylnonane: CN = 15
  • Typical diesel fuel: CN = 45–55
  • Higher CN → shorter ignition delay → less diesel knock

4. Combustion Chamber Design

4.1 SI Engine Combustion Chambers

Design objectives: compact chamber for minimum surface-area-to-volume ratio (reduces heat loss and knock), short flame travel distance from spark plug to end-gas, high turbulence, and adequate cooling of hot spots.

Chamber typeFeaturesKnock tendency
T-head (side-valve)Inlet and exhaust valves on opposite sides; long flame travelVery high — largely obsolete
L-head (side-valve)Both valves on one side; less flame travel than T-headHigh — improved over T-head
F-head (Ricardo)One overhead valve, one side valve; better turbulenceMedium
Hemispherical (Hemi)Spark plug centrally located; short flame travelLow; good performance
Pent-roof (4-valve)Centrally located spark plug; 4 valves maximize flow area; compact squish areaVery low; modern engines

4.2 CI Engine Combustion Chambers

TypeSub-typeFeatures
Direct Injection (DI)Open chamber (flat piston/bowl)Fuel injected directly into main cylinder; high injection pressure needed; more efficient
Toroidal cavity (re-entrant bowl)Piston crown bowl creates strong swirl; modern common-rail diesel
Indirect Injection (IDI)Pre-combustion chamber15–30% of clearance volume in pre-chamber; partial combustion there; glow plug for cold start
Swirl/Turbulence chamber (Ricardo Comet)Spherical pre-chamber; strong swirl promotes mixing; lower injection pressure
  • DI: higher thermal efficiency, lower BSFC; requires high injection pressure (1000–2500 bar for common-rail)
  • IDI: smoother combustion (less knock), lower noise; lower efficiency; requires glow plug for cold start
  • Glow plug: pre-heats IDI chamber for cold starting; not needed in DI (high compression ratio sufficient)

Key Points to Remember

  • SI detonation occurs after spark; pre-ignition occurs before spark (hot-spot initiated).
  • Normal flame speed 20–30 m/s; detonation shock wave 1000–2000 m/s.
  • SI knock increases with: higher $r$, advanced spark, higher intake $T$, higher coolant $T$, supercharging, lean mixture, longer flame path, lower octane fuel.
  • To eliminate SI knock: retard spark timing; reduce $r$; use higher octane fuel.
  • HUCR = highest compression ratio without knocking on a given fuel.
  • CI knock = rapid pressure rise from accumulated fuel burning all at once after long ignition delay.
  • CI knock decreases with: higher CN fuel, higher $r$, higher intake air $T$, higher injection pressure, supercharging, retarded injection (slightly).
  • $\tau_{id} = \tau_{\text{physical}} + \tau_{\text{chemical}}$. Longer delay = more fuel accumulation = harder knock.
  • Octane number: iso-octane = 100, n-heptane = 0. AKI = (RON + MON)/2. Higher ON = more knock-resistant fuel for SI.
  • Cetane number: n-cetane = 100. Higher CN = shorter ignition delay = less diesel knock = better CI fuel.
  • SI knock tendency order (increasing): Aromatics < Naphthenes < Olefins < Paraffins (straight-chain worst).
  • Stoichiometric AFR: petrol 14.7:1; methanol 6.5:1; diesel operates lean (18:1–70:1).
  • Pent-roof/hemispherical chambers: central spark plug = short flame travel = least SI knock.
  • CI DI: higher efficiency, needs high injection pressure. IDI: quieter, lower efficiency, needs glow plug for cold start.
