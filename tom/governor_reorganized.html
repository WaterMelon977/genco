2.2 — Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Governor: An automatic speed-control device that regulates the supply of working fluid (fuel, steam, or water) to a prime mover to maintain its MEAN rotational speed nearly constant across changing external loads.
  • Centrifugal Governor: A governor operating on the principle of balancing centrifugal forces generated by rotating flyballs against an opposing force (gravity dead-weight or spring load). As speed increases, flyballs move outward, lifting the sleeve to close the throttle valve.
  • Inertia Governor: A governor where control action depends on both centrifugal force (proportional to speed $\omega$) and angular inertia force (proportional to angular acceleration $\alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt}$). Offers faster response than centrifugal governors but is harder to balance dynamically.
  • Height of Governor $h$: The vertical distance measured along the governor spindle axis from the plane of rotation of the flyballs to the point of intersection of the upper arms (or their extensions) with the spindle axis.
  • Sensitiveness: The responsiveness of a governor to small speed changes. Quantified as the ratio of speed range $N_1 - N_2$ to mean speed $N_m$. A higher sensitiveness means a larger sleeve lift for a given percentage change in speed.
  • Stability: A governor is STABLE if for every speed within its working range, there is ONE AND ONLY ONE unique equilibrium position for the sleeve and flyballs. For a stable governor, the radius of rotation $r$ INCREASES as speed $N$ INCREASES $\frac{dN}{dr} > 0$.
  • Isochronism: A governor is ISOCHRONOUS if its equilibrium speed is CONSTANT for all radii of rotation within its working range $N_1 = N_2 = N_m$. Range of speed is zero, rendering sensitiveness INFINITE $S \to \infty$. An ideal Watt governor (neglecting friction) can be isochronous, but a Porter governor CANNOT be isochronous.
  • Hunting: A phenomenon of violent, continuous speed oscillation above and below mean speed caused by an OVER-SENSITIVE or laggy governor. The sleeve overreacts, over-closing and over-opening the throttle in a continuous cycle.
  • Governor Effort: The mean force exerted BY the governor ON the sleeve during a specified percentage change in speed (commonly defined for a $1\%$ speed variation).
  • Governor Power: The total work done by the governor on the sleeve during a specified speed change, equal to the product of Governor Effort and Sleeve Lift $\text{Power} = \text{Effort} \times \text{Sleeve Lift}$.
  • Pickering Governor: A spring-governor consisting of three flat leaf springs each carrying a central mass. Used primarily in gramophones and precision speed drives to control speed smoothly without links or pivots.

2.3 — Standard Values & Physical Constants

  • Watt Governor Speed Limit: $60 \text{ to } 80 \text{ rpm}$ (at $N > 100 \text{ rpm}$, height $h < 89.5 \text{ mm}$ and drops too small to detect!).
  • Watt Height at $N = 60 \text{ rpm}$: $h = \frac{895}{3600} \approx 0.2486 \text{ m} = 248.6 \text{ mm}$.
  • Watt Height at $N = 100 \text{ rpm}$: $h = \frac{895}{10000} = 0.0895 \text{ m} = 89.5 \text{ mm}$.
  • Watt Height Change for $100 \to 101 \text{ rpm}$: $\Delta h \approx 1.77 \text{ mm}$ (imperceptible sleeve movement).
  • Isochronous Governor Sensitiveness: $S = \infty$ (Range $= 0$).
  • Standard Effort Benchmark Speed Change: $1\%$ variation $c = 0.01$.

3.1 Centrifugal vs Inertia & Watt Governor Mechanics

1. Concept Pointers
  • Classification Overview:
  • Centrifugal Governors: Rely on centrifugal force of revolving flyballs acting against gravity or spring force. Types: Watt (pendulum), Porter & Proell (dead-weight loaded), Hartnell, Hartung, Wilson-Hartnell & Pickering (spring-loaded).
  • Inertia Governors: Rely on angular inertia torque $\alpha = d\omega/dt$ plus centrifugal force. Response is faster because it reacts directly to acceleration rather than waiting for speed to change.
  • Pickering Governor: Features flat leaf springs supporting central flyballs. As speed increases, centrifugal force bends leaf springs outward, pulling the sleeve down to regulate fluid flow. Used extensively in phonographs and gramophones.
  • Watt Governor (Pendulum Type):
  • Simplest form of centrifugal governor. Consists of two flyballs attached to arms pivoted to a rotating spindle.
  • Derivation of Height $h$: For ball of mass $m$ at radius $r$, rotating at angular velocity $\omega$: Centrifugal force $F_c = m \omega^2 r$. Ball weight $= m g$. Taking moments about upper arm pivot $O$:
    $${F_c \cdot h = m g \cdot r \implies (m \omega^2 r) \cdot h = m g r}$$
    Canceling $m \cdot r$ from both sides gives:
    $${\boxed{h = \frac{g}{\omega^2}}}$$
    Substituting $\omega = \frac{2\pi N}{60}$ and $g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$:
    $${\boxed{h = \frac{9.81}{\left(\frac{2\pi N}{60}\right)^2} = \frac{895}{N^2} \quad (\text{metres})}}$$
  • Speed Limitation of Watt Governor:
  • Height $h \propto \frac{1}{N^2}$. As speed $N$ increases, $h$ decreases rapidly.
  • At $N = 60 \text{ rpm}$, $h = 248.6 \text{ mm}$.
  • At $N = 100 \text{ rpm}$, $h = 89.5 \text{ mm}$.
  • At $N = 200 \text{ rpm}$, $h = 22.4 \text{ mm}$.
  • Rate of change $\frac{dh}{dN} = -\frac{1790}{N^3}$. At high speeds $N > 100 \text{ rpm}$, a change in speed of $1 \text{ rpm}$ produces an imperceptible sleeve lift $\Delta h < 1.7 \text{ mm}$, rendering the Watt governor insensitive and useless!
💡 Key Concept: Watt Speed Limit $N < 80 \text{ rpm}$: The height of a Watt governor is inversely proportional to speed squared $h = 895/N^2$. At high speeds $N > 100 \text{ rpm}$, $h$ becomes extremely small and sleeve movement per rpm drops to fraction of a millimeter, making it completely insensitive.

[!TRAP] Mass Independence of Watt Height: The height $h = 895/N^2$ of a Watt governor is completely INDEPENDENT of ball mass $m$! Adding heavier flyballs does NOT increase governor height or sensitivity in a Watt governor.

4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Watt Governor Height Calculation - Problem: A Watt governor rotates at $60 \text{ rpm}$. Calculate: (a) Height $h$ in meters and millimeters, (b) New height $h'$ if speed increases to $61 \text{ rpm}$, (c) Sleeve lift $\Delta h$. - Solution: 1. Calculate initial height at $N = 60 \text{ rpm}$:

$${h = \frac{895}{N^2} = \frac{895}{60^2} = \frac{895}{3600} \approx 0.24861 \text{ m} = 248.61 \text{ mm}}$$
2. Calculate new height at $N' = 61 \text{ rpm}$:
$${h' = \frac{895}{61^2} = \frac{895}{3721} \approx 0.24053 \text{ m} = 240.53 \text{ mm}}$$
3. Calculate height change $\Delta h$:
$${\Delta h = h - h' = 248.61 - 240.53 = 8.08 \text{ mm}}$$
- Key Insight: At $60 \text{ rpm}$, a $1 \text{ rpm}$ change causes $8.08 \text{ mm}$ height drop, providing good sensitivity.

Worked Example 2: Ineffectiveness of Watt Governor at High Speed - Problem: Repeat Example 1 for a Watt governor operating at $150 \text{ rpm}$ when speed increases by $1 \text{ rpm}$ to $151 \text{ rpm}$. - Solution: 1. Height at $N = 150 \text{ rpm}$:

$${h = \frac{895}{150^2} = \frac{895}{22500} \approx 0.03978 \text{ m} = 39.78 \text{ mm}}$$
2. Height at $N' = 151 \text{ rpm}$:
$${h' = \frac{895}{151^2} = \frac{895}{22801} \approx 0.03925 \text{ m} = 39.25 \text{ mm}}$$
3. Calculate height change $\Delta h$:
$${\Delta h = 39.78 - 39.25 = 0.53 \text{ mm}}$$
- Key Insight: At $150 \text{ rpm}$, a $1 \text{ rpm}$ change produces only $0.53 \text{ mm}$ height change—imperceptible for practical throttle linkage.

3.2 Porter & Proell Dead-Weight Governors & Isochronism Proof

1. Concept Pointers
  • Porter Governor Mechanics:
  • Added central dead-weight of mass $M$ attached to the sleeve.
  • Central load $M$ increases the downward force on sleeve, requiring higher rotational speed $\omega$ for equilibrium.
  • Height Formula (No Friction, Equal Link Angles $\alpha = \beta \implies q = 1$):
    $${\boxed{h = \frac{m + M}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}}}$$
    where $m$ is ball mass $\text{kg}$, $M$ is central sleeve load $\text{kg}$.
  • Link Obliquity Factor $q = \frac{\tan\beta}{\tan\alpha}$: If upper arms make angle $\alpha$ and lower arms make angle $\beta$ with spindle:
    $${\boxed{h = \frac{m + M\left(\frac{1+q}{2}\right)}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}}}$$
  • Sleeve Friction $f$: Friction $f$ at sleeve opposes motion. When sleeve moves UP (speed increasing), friction acts DOWN $M g + f$. When sleeve moves DOWN (speed decreasing), friction acts UP $M g - f$:
    $${\boxed{h = \frac{m \cdot g + (M \cdot g \pm f)\left(\frac{1+q}{2}\right)}{m \cdot g} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}}}$$
  • Proell Governor Mechanics:
  • Flyballs are mounted on extensions of the lower links (beyond sleeve pivot pins).
  • Provides a larger change in governor height for a given speed variation compared to Porter governor.
  • Requires smaller flyball mass $m$ for the same operating speed and sensitivity.
  • Height Relation: $h = \frac{a}{e} \cdot \frac{m+M}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}$, where $a$ is lower link length and $e$ is ball extension arm offset.
  • Proof Why Porter Governor CANNOT Be Isochronous:
  • For isochronism, speed must be constant for all sleeve positions $N_1 = N_2 = N$.
  • From Porter equation: $h = \frac{m + M}{m} \frac{895}{N^2}$.
  • If $N_1 = N_2$, then $h_1 = h_2$.
  • However, as the sleeve moves up/down, link geometry MANDATES that height $h$ MUST CHANGE $h_1 \neq h_2$.
  • A constant $h$ across different radii of rotation is geometrically impossible in a Porter linkage!
  • Therefore, a Porter governor CANNOT BE ISOCHRONOUS.
💡 Key Concept: Porter Isochronism Impossibility: An isochronous governor requires constant equilibrium speed $N$ across all sleeve positions, which implies constant height $h$. However, Porter link geometry requires $h$ to change as radius $r$ changes. Thus $N_1 = N_2$ is geometrically impossible!

[!TRAP] Friction Sign Rule for Sleeve: - Speed Increasing (Sleeve moving UP) $\implies$ Use $+f$ in numerator: $(M g + f)$. - Speed Decreasing (Sleeve moving DOWN) $\implies$ Use $-f$ in numerator: $(M g - f)$.

2. ASCII Decision Tree
Porter & Proell Governor Calculation
│
├── Check Link Angles (alpha and beta)
│   ├── Equal Angles (alpha = beta) ──► q = 1  ──► Factor (1+q)/2 = 1.0
│   └── Unequal Angles             ──► q = tan(beta) / tan(alpha)
│
├── Check Sleeve Friction (f)
│   ├── No Friction (f = 0)  ──► Load term = M * g
│   ├── Speed Increasing (Sleeve UP)   ──► Load term = M * g + f
│   └── Speed Decreasing (Sleeve DOWN) ──► Load term = M * g - f
│
└── Apply Height Formula: h = [ (m*g + (M*g +/- f)*(1+q)/2) / (m*g) ] * (895 / N^2)

  • [ ] Porter height $h = \frac{m+M}{m} \frac{895}{N^2}$ (equal arms, no friction).
  • [ ] Obliquity factor $q = \frac{\tan\beta}{\tan\alpha}$.
  • [ ] Friction $+f$ for speed increase, $-f$ for speed decrease.
  • [ ] Proell flyballs mounted on lower link extensions.
  • [ ] Porter CANNOT be isochronous.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Porter Governor Speed Calculation - Problem: A Porter governor has flyball mass $m = 5 \text{ kg}$ and central sleeve load $M = 30 \text{ kg}$. All arms are $250 \text{ mm}$ long and pivoted on the spindle axis. When radius of rotation is $r = 150 \text{ mm}$, calculate the equilibrium speed $N$ $\text{rpm}$. - Solution: 1. Calculate governor height $h$: In right triangle formed by arm $250 \text{ mm}$, radius $150 \text{ mm}$, and height $h$:

$${h = \sqrt{250^2 - 150^2} = \sqrt{62500 - 22500} = \sqrt{40000} = 200 \text{ mm} = 0.20 \text{ m}}$$
2. Since arms are pivoted on spindle axis and equal length, $q = 1$. 3. Apply Porter height formula $h = \frac{m + M}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}$:
$${0.20 = \frac{5 + 30}{5} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = 7 \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = \frac{6265}{N^2}}$$
4. Solve for $N^2$ and $N$:
$${N^2 = \frac{6265}{0.20} = 31325 \implies N = \sqrt{31325} \approx 177.0 \text{ rpm}}$$
- Key Insight: $N \approx 177 \text{ rpm}$. Without central load $M=0$, a Watt governor at $h=0.20 \text{ m}$ would run at $N = \sqrt{895/0.20} \approx 66.9 \text{ rpm}$. Central load increased operating speed by $2.64\times$!

Worked Example 2: Effect of Sleeve Friction on Speed Limits - Problem: For the Porter governor in Example 1 $m=5\text{ kg}, M=30\text{ kg}, h=0.20\text{ m}$, a friction force $f = 20 \text{ N}$ acts at the sleeve. Calculate the maximum and minimum equilibrium speeds for $h = 0.20 \text{ m}$. - Solution: 1. Calculate gravity loads: $m g = 5 \times 9.81 = 49.05 \text{ N}$, $M g = 30 \times 9.81 = 294.3 \text{ N}$. 2. Maximum speed $N_1$ (sleeve about to move UP, $+f$):

$${h = \frac{m g + (M g + f)}{m g} \cdot \frac{895}{N_1^2} \implies 0.20 = \frac{49.05 + (294.3 + 20)}{49.05} \cdot \frac{895}{N_1^2}}$$
$${0.20 = \frac{363.35}{49.05} \cdot \frac{895}{N_1^2} = 7.4077 \cdot \frac{895}{N_1^2} = \frac{6629.9}{N_1^2}}$$
$${N_1^2 = \frac{6629.9}{0.20} = 33149.6 \implies N_1 \approx 182.07 \text{ rpm}}$$
3. Minimum speed $N_2$ (sleeve about to move DOWN, $-f$):
$${0.20 = \frac{49.05 + (294.3 - 20)}{49.05} \cdot \frac{895}{N_2^2} = \frac{323.35}{49.05} \cdot \frac{895}{N_2^2} = 6.5922 \cdot \frac{895}{N_2^2} = \frac{5900.0}{N_2^2}}$$
$${N_2^2 = \frac{5900.0}{0.20} = 29500 \implies N_2 \approx 171.76 \text{ rpm}}$$
4. Frictional speed range $\Delta N = N_1 - N_2 = 182.07 - 171.76 = 10.31 \text{ rpm}$. - Key Insight: Sleeve friction creates a speed deadband of $10.31 \text{ rpm}$ $171.8 - 182.1 \text{ rpm}$ wherein the sleeve does not move at all!

Worked Example 3: Porter Governor with Unequal Link Angles $q \neq 1$ - Problem: A Porter governor has $m = 4 \text{ kg}$, $M = 24 \text{ kg}$. Upper arms make angle $\alpha = 30^\circ$ with spindle, lower arms make angle $\beta = 45^\circ$. If height $h = 0.18 \text{ m}$, calculate equilibrium speed $N$. - Solution: 1. Calculate link obliquity ratio $q$:

$${q = \frac{\tan\beta}{\tan\alpha} = \frac{\tan 45^\circ}{\tan 30^\circ} = \frac{1.0}{0.57735} \approx 1.732}$$
2. Calculate obliquity factor $\frac{1+q}{2}$:
$${\frac{1 + q}{2} = \frac{1 + 1.732}{2} = \frac{2.732}{2} = 1.366}$$
3. Apply modified Porter height formula:
$${h = \frac{m + M \left(\frac{1+q}{2}\right)}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} \implies 0.18 = \frac{4 + 24(1.366)}{4} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}}$$
$${0.18 = \frac{4 + 32.784}{4} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = \frac{36.784}{4} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = 9.196 \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = \frac{8230.4}{N^2}}$$
4. Solve for $N^2$ and $N$:
$${N^2 = \frac{8230.4}{0.18} \approx 45724.5 \implies N = \sqrt{45724.5} \approx 213.83 \text{ rpm}}$$
- Key Insight: $N \approx 213.8 \text{ rpm}$. Unequal link angles $\beta > \alpha$ increase effective sleeve load and raise operating speed.

Worked Example 4: Proell Governor Height & Speed Calculation - Problem: In a Proell governor, lower arms of length $a = 200 \text{ mm} = 0.2 \text{ m}$ carry flyballs mounted on extension arms of vertical offset $e = 160 \text{ mm} = 0.16 \text{ m}$. Ball mass $m = 3 \text{ kg}$, sleeve mass $M = 20 \text{ kg}$. If height $h = 0.15 \text{ m}$, calculate equilibrium speed $N$. - Solution: 1. Calculate extension ratio $a/e = 0.2 / 0.16 = 1.25$. 2. Apply Proell height relation $h = \frac{a}{e} \cdot \frac{m+M}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}$:

$${0.15 = 1.25 \cdot \frac{3 + 20}{3} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = 1.25 \cdot 7.667 \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = 9.583 \cdot \frac{895}{N^2} = \frac{8577.0}{N^2}}$$
3. Solve for $N^2$ and $N$:
$${N^2 = \frac{8577.0}{0.15} = 57180 \implies N = \sqrt{57180} \approx 239.1 \text{ rpm}}$$
- Key Insight: $N \approx 239.1 \text{ rpm}$. Extension factor $a/e = 1.25$ increases equilibrium speed for the same height.

3.3 Hartnell & Spring-Loaded Governors & Controlling Force Diagrams

1. Concept Pointers
  • Hartnell Governor Mechanics:
  • Spring-loaded centrifugal governor operating at high speeds $200 - 600+ \text{ rpm}$.
  • Flyballs are mounted on vertical arms of two bell-crank levers pivoted to a rotating frame.
  • Horizontal arms carry rollers that engage a sleeve loaded by a helical compression spring.
  • Derivation of Spring Stiffness $k$:
  • Let $a$ = length of vertical arm (carrying ball of mass $m$), $b$ = length of horizontal arm (engaging sleeve).
  • At minimum radius $r_1$: centrifugal force $F_1 = m \omega_1^2 r_1$. Spring force $= S_1$.
  • Taking moments about bell-crank pivot:
    $${F_1 \cdot a = \left(\frac{S_1 + M g}{2}\right) \cdot b \implies S_1 = 2 \left(\frac{a}{b}\right) F_1 - M g}$$
  • At maximum radius $r_2$: centrifugal force $F_2 = m \omega_2^2 r_2$. Spring force $= S_2$.
    $${S_2 = 2 \left(\frac{a}{b}\right) F_2 - M g}$$
  • Net change in spring force $\Delta S = S_2 - S_1 = 2 \left(\frac{a}{b}\right) (F_2 - F_1)$.
  • Sleeve lift $x = (r_2 - r_1) \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$.
  • Spring stiffness $k = \frac{\Delta S}{x}$:
    $${k = \frac{2 \left(\frac{a}{b}\right) (F_2 - F_1)}{(r_2 - r_1) \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)} \implies \boxed{k = 2 \cdot \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{F_2 - F_1}{r_2 - r_1}}}$$
  • Equal Arm Case $a = b$:
    $${\boxed{k = 2 \cdot \frac{F_2 - F_1}{r_2 - r_1}}}$$
  • Controlling Force Line Equations ($F_c$ vs $r$):
  • A plot of inward controlling force $F_c$ against radius of rotation $r$ characterizes spring governor behavior:
    1. Stable Governor: Line equation $F_c = A \cdot r - B$ $B > 0$. Intercepts negative $F_c$ axis. Ratio $F_c / r$ increases as $r$ increases $\implies$ Speed $N$ increases with $r$.
    2. Isochronous Governor: Line equation $F_c = A \cdot r$ $B = 0$. Passes through origin $(0,0)$. Ratio $F_c / r = A = \text{constant} \implies$ Speed $N$ is constant for all $r$.
    3. Unstable Governor: Line equation $F_c = A \cdot r + B$ $B > 0$. Intercepts positive $F_c$ axis. Ratio $F_c / r$ decreases as $r$ increases $\implies$ Speed $N$ decreases as $r$ increases!
💡 Key Concept: Factor of 2 in Hartnell Stiffness: The factor of 2 in $k = 2 (a/b)^2 \frac{F_2 - F_1}{r_2 - r_1}$ arises because TWO flyballs (two bell-crank levers) simultaneously compress the single central spring on the sleeve!

[!TRAP] Stiffness vs Sensitivity: Increasing spring stiffness $k$ in a Hartnell governor DECREASES its sensitiveness! A stiffer spring requires a larger speed change to produce the same sleeve lift.

4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Hartnell Governor Spring Stiffness Calculation - Problem: A Hartnell governor has flyball mass $m = 4 \text{ kg}$ per ball. The vertical and horizontal arms of the bell-crank levers are equal $a = b$. At minimum radius $r_1 = 0.20 \text{ m}$, centrifugal force $F_1 = 900 \text{ N}$. At maximum radius $r_2 = 0.25 \text{ m}$, centrifugal force $F_2 = 1600 \text{ N}$. Calculate: (a) Sleeve lift $x$, (b) Spring stiffness $k$ in $\text{N/cm}$ and $\text{N/m}$. - Solution: 1. Since $a = b$, sleeve lift $x$:

$${x = r_2 - r_1 = 0.25 - 0.20 = 0.05 \text{ m} = 5 \text{ cm}}$$
2. Calculate spring stiffness $k$ (using factor 2 for two balls):
$${k = 2 \cdot \frac{F_2 - F_1}{r_2 - r_1} = 2 \cdot \frac{1600 - 900}{0.25 - 0.20} = 2 \cdot \frac{700}{0.05} = 2 \times 14000 = 28000 \text{ N/m}}$$
3. Convert $k$ to $\text{N/cm}$:
$${k = \frac{28000 \text{ N/m}}{100 \text{ cm/m}} = 280 \text{ N/cm}}$$
- Key Insight: $x = 5 \text{ cm}$, $k = 280 \text{ N/cm}$ $28 \text{ kN/m}$. Forgetting factor 2 would give incorrect $140 \text{ N/cm}$.

Worked Example 2: Hartnell Governor with Unequal Arms $a \neq b$ - Problem: A Hartnell governor has vertical arm $a = 120 \text{ mm} = 0.12 \text{ m}$ and horizontal arm $b = 80 \text{ mm} = 0.08 \text{ m}$. Ball mass $m = 3 \text{ kg}$. $r_1 = 0.15 \text{ m}, N_1 = 300 \text{ rpm}$; $r_2 = 0.20 \text{ m}, N_2 = 330 \text{ rpm}$. Calculate: (a) Sleeve lift $x$, (b) Spring stiffness $k$. - Solution: 1. Calculate $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$:

$${\omega_1 = \frac{2\pi \times 300}{60} = 10\pi \approx 31.416 \text{ rad/s} \implies \omega_1^2 = 986.96}$$
$${\omega_2 = \frac{2\pi \times 330}{60} = 11\pi \approx 34.558 \text{ rad/s} \implies \omega_2^2 = 1194.22}$$
2. Calculate centrifugal forces $F_1$ and $F_2$:
$${F_1 = m \omega_1^2 r_1 = 3 \times 986.96 \times 0.15 = 444.13 \text{ N}}$$
$${F_2 = m \omega_2^2 r_2 = 3 \times 1194.22 \times 0.20 = 716.53 \text{ N}}$$
3. Calculate sleeve lift $x$:
$${x = (r_2 - r_1) \cdot \left(\frac{b}{a}\right) = (0.20 - 0.15) \times \left(\frac{0.08}{0.12}\right) = 0.05 \times \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.0333 \text{ m} = 3.33 \text{ cm}}$$
4. Calculate arm ratio term $(a/b)^2$:
$${\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{0.12}{0.08}\right)^2 = (1.5)^2 = 2.25}$$
5. Calculate spring stiffness $k$:
$${k = 2 \cdot \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{F_2 - F_1}{r_2 - r_1} = 2 \times 2.25 \times \frac{716.53 - 444.13}{0.05} = 4.5 \times \frac{272.40}{0.05} = 4.5 \times 5448 = 24516 \text{ N/m}}$$
$${k = 245.16 \text{ N/cm}}$$
- Key Insight: $x = 3.33 \text{ cm}$, $k \approx 245.2 \text{ N/cm}$.

3.4 Governor Performance Characteristics (Sensitiveness, Stability, Isochronism, Hunting, Friction)

1. Concept Pointers
  • Sensitiveness $S$:
  • Ratio of speed range to mean speed: $\boxed{S = \frac{N_1 - N_2}{N_m} = \frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{\omega_m}}$.
  • High sensitiveness $\implies$ Small speed change produces large sleeve movement.
  • Stability:
  • A governor is stable if for every speed there is ONE unique equilibrium position.
  • Stability Condition: Radius of rotation $r$ INCREASES as speed $N$ INCREASES $\frac{dN}{dr} > 0$.
  • Isochronism:
  • Equilibrium speed is constant for ALL radii of rotation $N_1 = N_2 = N_m$.
  • Range of speed $= 0 \implies$ Sensitiveness $S = \frac{N_1 - N_2}{N_m} = \frac{0}{N_m} = 0$ in numerator $\implies S \to \infty$ (INFINITE sensitiveness).
  • In a controlling force diagram ($F_c$ vs $r$), an isochronous spring-controlled governor has a straight controlling force line passing through the origin $F_c = A \cdot r$.
  • Hunting:
  • Unstable, violent speed oscillations caused by an OVER-SENSITIVE or laggy governor.
  • When engine load increases, speed drops; over-sensitive governor opens throttle too wide, causing engine to overspeed; governor then cuts fuel drastically, causing underspeed. Cycle repeats continuously!
  • Sleeve Friction Deadband:
  • Sleeve friction $f$ opposes movement, creating an insensitive speed range $\Delta N_{\text{frict}}$ where speed changes but sleeve stays frozen:
    $${\Delta N_{\text{frict}} \approx \frac{f}{m g + M g} \cdot N_m}$$
💡 Key Concept: Controlling Force Line Characteristics: - Stable Spring Governor: $F_c = A \cdot r - B$ ($B > 0$, positive intercept on negative $F_c$ axis). - Isochronous Spring Governor: $F_c = A \cdot r$ ($B = 0$, passes through origin). - Unstable Spring Governor: $F_c = A \cdot r + B$ ($B > 0$, positive intercept on positive $F_c$ axis).

[!TRAP] Isochronism vs Hunting: - Isochronism is an IDEAL theoretical property ($S = \infty$, zero speed range). - Hunting is a BAD practical defect resulting from excessive sensitiveness or friction lag!

2. ASCII Decision Tree
Governor Performance Classification
│
├── Does radius r increase as speed N increases? (dN/dr > 0)
│   ├── YES ──► STABLE GOVERNOR (Desirable working state)
│   └── NO  ──► UNSTABLE GOVERNOR (Radius decreases as speed increases!)
│
├── Is speed N constant for ALL radii of rotation? (N1 = N2)
│   └── YES ──► ISOCHRONOUS GOVERNOR (Sensitiveness S = INFINITY)
│               └── Controlling force line passes through origin: Fc = a * r
│
└── Does the governor violently oscillate around mean speed?
    └── YES ──► HUNTING (Caused by over-sensitiveness or sleeve friction lag)
3. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Sensitiveness $S = (N_1 - N_2)/N_m$.
  • [ ] Stable: $r$ increases as $N$ increases.
  • [ ] Isochronous: $N_1 = N_2 \implies S = \infty$.
  • [ ] Isochronous controlling force line: $F_c = a r$ (passes through origin).
  • [ ] Hunting: Violent speed oscillations from over-sensitiveness.
4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Governor Sensitiveness Calculation - Problem: A governor runs at $N_1 = 255 \text{ rpm}$ at no load and $N_2 = 245 \text{ rpm}$ at full load. Calculate: (a) Mean speed $N_m$, (b) Speed range, (c) Sensitiveness $S$ as a percentage. - Solution: 1. Calculate mean speed $N_m$:

$${N_m = \frac{N_1 + N_2}{2} = \frac{255 + 245}{2} = 250 \text{ rpm}}$$
2. Calculate speed range:
$${\text{Range} = N_1 - N_2 = 255 - 245 = 10 \text{ rpm}}$$
3. Calculate sensitiveness $S$:
$${S = \frac{N_1 - N_2}{N_m} = \frac{10}{250} = 0.04 = 4\%}$$
- Key Insight: Sensitiveness $S = 4\%$.

Worked Example 2: Controlling Force Line Intercept & Stability Analysis - Problem: The controlling force of a spring governor is given by $F_c = 1500 \cdot r - 120$ $\text{N}$, where $r$ is in meters. Determine whether the governor is stable, isochronous, or unstable. Also calculate the speeds $N_1$ and $N_2$ at $r_1 = 0.15 \text{ m}$ and $r_2 = 0.25 \text{ m}$ for ball mass $m = 2 \text{ kg}$. - Solution: 1. Inspect line equation $F_c = A \cdot r - B$: Here $A = 1500, B = 120 > 0$. Since $B > 0$ with a negative sign, it intercepts the negative $F_c$ axis $\implies$ The governor is STABLE! 2. Calculate controlling force at $r_1 = 0.15 \text{ m}$:

$${F_1 = 1500(0.15) - 120 = 225 - 120 = 105 \text{ N}}$$
Since $F_1 = m \omega_1^2 r_1 \implies 105 = 2 \times \omega_1^2 \times 0.15 = 0.30 \omega_1^2$:
$${\omega_1^2 = \frac{105}{0.30} = 350 \implies \omega_1 \approx 18.708 \text{ rad/s}}$$
$${N_1 = \frac{60 \times 18.708}{2\pi} \approx 178.6 \text{ rpm}}$$
3. Calculate controlling force at $r_2 = 0.25 \text{ m}$:
$${F_2 = 1500(0.25) - 120 = 375 - 120 = 255 \text{ N}}$$
$255 = 2 \times \omega_2^2 \times 0.25 = 0.50 \omega_2^2 \implies \omega_2^2 = \frac{255}{0.50} = 510 \implies \omega_2 \approx 22.583 \text{ rad/s}$:
$${N_2 = \frac{60 \times 22.583}{2\pi} \approx 215.7 \text{ rpm}}$$
4. Verify stability: As radius increases from $0.15 \text{ m}$ to $0.25 \text{ m}$, speed increases from $178.6 \text{ rpm}$ to $215.7 \text{ rpm}$ $\frac{dN}{dr} > 0$, confirming stability! - Key Insight: $F_c = 1500 r - 120$ is a STABLE governor with $N_1 = 178.6 \text{ rpm}$ and $N_2 = 215.7 \text{ rpm}$.

3.5 Governor Effort & Power Equations

1. Concept Pointers
  • Governor Effort $P_{\text{effort}}$:
  • The mean force exerted BY the governor ON the sleeve during a given percentage change in speed $c = \frac{\Delta N}{N_m}$.
  • For a Porter Governor with ball mass $m$ and central load $M$, for a speed increase fraction $c$:
    $${\boxed{P_{\text{effort}} = \frac{c}{1+c} \cdot (m + M) \cdot g}}$$
  • Standard $1\%$ Effort $c = 0.01$:
    $${P_{1\%} = \frac{0.01}{1.01} \cdot (m + M) \cdot g \approx 0.01 \cdot (m + M) \cdot g}$$
  • Governor Power $P_{\text{power}}$:
  • Work done by governor on sleeve over full lift $x$:
    $${\boxed{P_{\text{power}} = \text{Governor Effort} \times \text{Sleeve Lift} = P_{\text{effort}} \cdot x}}$$
  • Units: Joules $\text{J}$ or $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$.
💡 Key Concept: Governor Power Definition: Governor Power is NOT mechanical rate of work in Watts $\text{W}$! In governor terminology, Power is defined as TOTAL WORK DONE ON THE SLEEVE in Joules $\text{J}$ over its full lift range $\text{Power} = \text{Effort} \times \text{Sleeve Lift}$.
2. Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Effort $P = \frac{c}{1+c}(m+M)g$.
  • [ ] $1\%$ Effort $P_{1\%} \approx 0.01 (m+M)g$.
  • [ ] Power $= \text{Effort} \times \text{Sleeve Lift}$ $\text{Joules}$.
3. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Porter Governor Effort & Power Calculation - Problem: A Porter governor has $m = 5 \text{ kg}$, $M = 35 \text{ kg}$, and full sleeve lift $x = 40 \text{ mm} = 0.04 \text{ m}$. Calculate: (a) Governor Effort for a $1\%$ speed increase $c = 0.01$, (b) Governor Power. - Solution: 1. Calculate total gravity weight $(m+M)g$:

$${(m+M)g = (5 + 35) \times 9.81 = 40 \times 9.81 = 392.4 \text{ N}}$$
2. Calculate $1\%$ Governor Effort $P_{1\%}$:
$${P_{1\%} = \frac{0.01}{1.01} \times 392.4 = \frac{3.924}{1.01} \approx 3.885 \text{ N}}$$
3. Calculate Governor Power:
$${\text{Power} = P_{1\%} \times x = 3.885 \text{ N} \times 0.04 \text{ m} = 0.1554 \text{ J} = 0.1554 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}$$
- Key Insight: Effort $= 3.885 \text{ N}$, Power $= 0.1554 \text{ J}$.

Worked Example 2: Hartnell Governor Effort & Power Calculation - Problem: A Hartnell governor has $a = b$, $m = 4 \text{ kg}$, $r_1 = 0.18 \text{ m}, N_1 = 290 \text{ rpm}, F_1 = 800 \text{ N}$; $r_2 = 0.22 \text{ m}, N_2 = 310 \text{ rpm}, F_2 = 1400 \text{ N}$. Calculate: (a) Sleeve lift $x$, (b) Governor Effort $P$, (c) Governor Power. - Solution: 1. Calculate sleeve lift $x$:

$${x = r_2 - r_1 = 0.22 - 0.18 = 0.04 \text{ m}}$$
2. Mean centrifugal force $F_m = \frac{F_1 + F_2}{2} = \frac{800 + 1400}{2} = 1100 \text{ N}$. 3. Change in force $\Delta F = F_2 - F_1 = 1400 - 800 = 600 \text{ N}$. 4. Governor Effort at sleeve level $P_{\text{effort}} = \Delta F = 600 \text{ N}$. 5. Governor Power $= P_{\text{effort}} \times x = 600 \text{ N} \times 0.04 \text{ m} = 24.0 \text{ J}$. - Key Insight: Power $= 24.0 \text{ J}$.

3.6 Governor vs Flywheel Comprehensive Comparison

1. Comparative Analysis Table
Feature / Aspect Governor Flywheel
Primary Function Regulates mean rotational speed OVER MULTIPLE CYCLES. Smooths out cyclic speed variations WITHIN A SINGLE CYCLE.
Cause of Variation External load changes on engine / prime mover. Internal cyclic torque fluctuations (surplus/deficit energy).
Control Action Active control regulating fuel / working fluid throttle valve. Passive kinetic energy storage $E = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$.
Operation Mode Intermittent (activates only when mean speed strays). Continuous and automatic during every single stroke.
Effect on Mean Speed Controls mean speed across varying load demand. Has NO CONTROL over mean speed if external load changes!
Effect on Cyclic Torque Has NO EFFECT on cyclic torque variations within a stroke. Directly smooths out cyclic torque variations.

SECTION 4: THEORY Q&A (20 Core Conceptual Pairs)

Q1: What is the primary function of a Governor?
A: To regulate the mean rotational speed of a prime mover across changing external loads by adjusting working fluid/fuel supply.

Q2: Write the formula for height $h$ of a Watt governor.
A: $h = \frac{g}{\omega^2} = \frac{895}{N^2}$ (in meters).

Q3: Why is a Watt governor insensitive at high speeds $N > 100 \text{ rpm}$?
A: Because height $h \propto 1/N^2$. At high speeds, $h$ becomes very small and height change per rpm $\frac{dh}{dN} = -\frac{1790}{N^3}$ drops to fractions of a millimeter.

Q4: Does adding heavier flyballs to a Watt governor increase its height or sensitivity?
A: No. Height $h = 895/N^2$ is completely independent of flyball mass $m$.

Q5: Write the height formula for a Porter governor without friction.
A: $h = \frac{m + M}{m} \cdot \frac{895}{N^2}$ (for equal link angles $q=1$).

Q6: How does sleeve friction $f$ affect Porter governor height during speed increase?
A: Sleeve friction opposes upward motion, increasing effective sleeve load to $(M g + f)$, giving $h = \frac{m g + (M g + f)}{m g} \frac{895}{N^2}$.

Q7: Prove why a Porter governor CANNOT be Isochronous.
A: Isochronism requires constant speed $N$ for all radii, which implies constant height $h$. However, Porter link geometry mandates that height $h$ MUST change as radius changes $h = \sqrt{L^2 - r^2}$, making $N_1 = N_2$ geometrically impossible.

Q8: What is the structural difference between a Proell governor and a Porter governor?
A: In a Proell governor, flyballs are mounted on extensions of the lower links beyond the sleeve pins, providing higher sensitivity with smaller ball mass.

Q9: Write the spring stiffness formula $k$ for a Hartnell governor with equal arms $a=b$.
A: $k = 2 \cdot \frac{F_2 - F_1}{r_2 - r_1}$ (factor 2 accounts for two flyballs).

Q10: How does increasing spring stiffness affect Hartnell governor sensitiveness?
A: Increasing spring stiffness DECREASES sensitiveness, requiring a larger speed change for a given sleeve lift.

Q11: Define Governor Sensitiveness $S$.
A: The ratio of speed range to mean speed: $S = \frac{N_1 - N_2}{N_m}$.

Q12: Define a Stable Governor.
A: A governor where for every speed there is ONE unique equilibrium position, and radius of rotation $r$ INCREASES as speed $N$ INCREASES $\frac{dN}{dr} > 0$.

Q13: Define an Isochronous Governor.
A: A governor whose equilibrium speed is constant for all radii of rotation $N_1 = N_2$. Speed range is zero, making sensitiveness infinite $S = \infty$.

Q14: What is the Controlling Force line equation for an Isochronous spring governor?
A: $F_c = A \cdot r$ (a straight line passing through the origin).

Q15: Define Hunting in governors.
A: Violent, continuous speed oscillations above and below mean speed caused by an over-sensitive or friction-laggy governor over-correcting fuel supply.

Q16: Define Governor Effort.
A: The mean force exerted BY the governor ON the sleeve during a specified percentage change in speed (commonly defined for $1\%$ speed variation).

Q17: Define Governor Power.
A: The total work done by the governor on the sleeve over its full lift, equal to $\text{Power} = \text{Effort} \times \text{Sleeve Lift}$ (in Joules).

Q18: What is the main difference between a Flywheel and a Governor?
A: A flywheel controls cyclic speed variations WITHIN one cycle using passive KE storage, while a governor regulates mean speed OVER MULTIPLE CYCLES across external load changes by actively controlling fuel supply.

Q19: What type of governor responds faster: Centrifugal or Inertia?
A: Inertia governor, because it responds directly to angular acceleration $\alpha = d\omega/dt$ in addition to centrifugal force.

Q20: What is the frictional speed deadband of a governor?
A: The speed range $\Delta N_{\text{frict}} \approx \frac{f}{m g + M g} N_m$ within which speed varies due to sleeve friction $f$ without causing any sleeve movement.
