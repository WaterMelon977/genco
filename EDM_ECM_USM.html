This guide covers Micro / Unconventional Finishing Machines: EDM, ECM and USM for AP Genco AE level.

Key focus: working principles, material removal mechanisms, important process parameters, MRR relations, advantages/limitations and typical applications.

These processes are non-traditional (no conventional cutting force) and are frequently tested for principle identification, electrolyte/dielectric role, and comparison questions.

High exam relevance for conceptual MCQs and basic formula recognition.

Part B: Deep Dive by Concept

1. Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM)

Concept Pointers
  • Thermal process: material is removed by melting and vaporization due to high-temperature sparks.
  • Tool and workpiece never touch; a small spark gap is maintained.
  • Dielectric fluid ionizes, creates plasma channel, then collapses → removes molten metal.
  • Tool wear occurs (tool material also erodes).
  • Best for hard, electrically conductive materials (carbides, hardened steels, exotic alloys).
  • Two main types: Die-sinking EDM and Wire-cut EDM (WEDM).
Formulas / Relations

Spark Energy (conceptual)

$$\boxed{E \propto V \times I \times t}$$

(higher energy → higher MRR but poorer surface finish)

Parameter Typical Range / Note
Gap voltage 40–300 V
Current 0.5–400 A
Pulse frequency High (kHz range)
Dielectric Hydrocarbon oil or deionized water

Conditions & Special Cases

  • Workpiece must be electrically conductive.
  • No mechanical cutting force → suitable for fragile or thin sections.
  • Surface has recast layer (heat-affected zone).

Worked Example

Example (conceptual PYQ style)

Given: Which fluid is used as dielectric in EDM?
Answer: Kerosene or deionized water.

Key Insight: Dielectric both insulates and flushes away debris; its ionization is essential for spark formation.

2. Electrochemical Machining (ECM)

Concept Pointers
  • Purely electrochemical process (anodic dissolution).
  • Workpiece is anode, tool is cathode; high current density, low voltage DC.
  • Electrolyte flows at high velocity through the gap to remove reaction products and cool the zone.
  • No tool wear in ideal conditions (tool shape is preserved).
  • Excellent for hard materials, complex 3-D shapes, and burr-free finishing.
  • Material removal rate is independent of workpiece hardness.
Formulas

Faraday’s Law based MRR

$$\boxed{\text{MRR} = \dfrac{A \cdot I}{\rho \cdot F \cdot z}}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
( A ) Atomic weight of metal g/mol
( I ) Current A
$ \rho $ Density of workpiece g/cm³
( F ) Faraday’s constant 96500 C/mol
( z ) Valency of metal —

Simplified

$$\boxed{\text{MRR} \propto I}$$

(current is the dominant controllable parameter)

Conditions & Special Cases

  • Only electrically conductive materials.
  • Electrolyte must be carefully chosen (NaCl, NaNO₃, etc.).
  • No residual stress or heat-affected zone.
Diagram

ECM schematic: tool cathode, workpiece anode, and electrolyte flow

Worked Example

Example (conceptual)

Given: ECM is the reverse of which process?
Answer: Electroplating.

Key Insight: In electroplating metal deposits on cathode; in ECM metal dissolves from anode (workpiece).

3. Ultrasonic Machining (USM)

Concept Pointers
  • Mechanical process using high-frequency vibration + abrasive slurry.
  • Tool vibrates at ultrasonic frequency (≈ 20 kHz) with small amplitude.
  • Abrasive particles in slurry are hammered against the workpiece → micro-chipping / erosion.
  • Suitable for hard and brittle materials (glass, ceramics, carbides, semiconductors) that are non-conductive or difficult to machine otherwise.
  • Tool is usually softer than workpiece (soft steel, brass, etc.).
  • No thermal damage or residual stresses.
Formulas / Relations

Material Removal

$$\boxed{\text{MRR increases with amplitude, frequency, abrasive grit size and concentration}}$$

Parameter Typical Value / Effect
Frequency 18–40 kHz
Amplitude 10–50 µm
Abrasive B₄C, SiC, Al₂O₃
Slurry concentration 30–40 % by volume
Tool material Soft (brass, mild steel)

Conditions & Special Cases

  • Best for brittle, hard, non-conductive materials.
  • Low MRR compared with EDM/ECM.
  • Tool wear is significant (tool is gradually eroded).

Ultrasonic Machining (USM)

A. Key Concept Pointers

  • Non-traditional mechanical process using high-frequency tool vibration and abrasive slurry for material removal.
  • Best suited for hard, brittle, non-conducting materials (glass, ceramics, carbides, quartz, semiconductors).
  • Tool vibrates at 15–30 kHz (typically ~20 kHz) with amplitude 25–100 µm (or 0.01–0.06 mm).
  • Material removal occurs by repeated hammering/impact and fracture from abrasive particles in slurry.
  • Tool is made of ductile/soft material (mild steel, brass); wears slowly; does not contact workpiece directly.
  • Abrasive slurry (boron carbide or silicon carbide + water) is pumped into the working gap.
  • Very low material removal rate (MRR); no heat generation.
  • MRR higher for materials with lower toughness/brittleness; increases with amplitude, frequency (resonant), and grain size (up to amplitude limit).

B. Formulas
No closed-form MRR equation in core PYQs (qualitative trends dominate). Key relations from standard practice matching PYQ trends:

MRR ∝ amplitude of vibration
MRR ∝ frequency (must be resonant for max amplitude at tool tip)
MRR increases with abrasive grain size (optimal when grain size ≈ amplitude)
MRR higher for lower toughness / higher brittleness workpiece materials

C. Visual / Diagram

Source: Mechanical Engineering Course — Clear schematic of USM setup. Key exam takeaway: Note ultrasonic transducer → horn/tool holder → vibrating tool tip; abrasive slurry flow into gap; workpiece erosion by abrasive impact (no direct tool-work contact). Observe working gap and slurry circulation path.

D. Examiner Traps

  • USM best only for non-ferrous or dielectrics — wrong; primarily for brittle materials (conducting or non-conducting).
  • Tool is hard material — wrong; tool must be ductile/soft.
  • High MRR or no abrasives used — wrong; very low MRR; abrasives essential in slurry.
  • Tool vibrates at low frequency — wrong; 15–30 kHz (high frequency).
  • Suitable for ductile materials with high MRR — wrong; lower MRR on ductile/tough materials.

Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM)

A. Key Concept Pointers

  • Electro-thermal process; material removal by melting + evaporation from intense localized spark heat (>10,000°C).
  • Workpiece connected to anode (+); tool connected to cathode (−).
  • Only electrically conducting materials can be machined (irrespective of hardness).
  • Dielectric fluid (kerosene-based oil) fills gap; acts as insulator, coolant, and flushes debris.
  • Servo mechanism maintains constant small spark gap (typically 0.025–0.05 mm).
  • Tool materials: copper, graphite, brass, tellurium copper, tungsten carbide.
  • No mechanical force between tool and workpiece; no tool-work contact.
  • Better surface finish obtained at high frequency + low discharge current.
  • Maximum MRR approximately 5000 mm³/min; wear ratio for tungsten carbide workpiece = 0.5.

B. Formulas
No direct MRR formula in PYQs (qualitative + max value given). Wear ratio (tool wear : workpiece wear) for WC work = 0.5.

C. Visual / Diagram

Source: ElectricalWorkbook — EDM schematic. Key exam takeaway: Observe DC power supply + capacitor circuit; servo-controlled tool feed; dielectric bath; workpiece (job) as anode; tool as cathode; flushing system. Note spark gap and servo role in gap maintenance.

D. Examiner Traps

  • Workpiece connected to cathode — wrong; workpiece is anode (+).
  • Dielectric is salt solution or abrasive slurry — wrong; kerosene-based dielectric fluid.
  • Temperature ~1000°C or 100°C — wrong; order of 10,000°C+.
  • Suitable for non-conducting materials — wrong; only conducting materials.
  • Tool wear zero or very high — wear present but controlled; better finish at high frequency + low current.

Electrochemical Machining (ECM)

A. Key Concept Pointers

  • Electrochemical process; material removal by anodic dissolution (atomic-level electrochemical reaction).
  • Reverse of electroplating process.
  • Workpiece = anode (+); tool = cathode (−).
  • Electrolyte (non-corrosive salt solution: NaCl, Na₂SO₄, NaNO₃, etc.) flows through small inter-electrode gap.
  • No appreciable tool wear (tool life very long); mirror-like surface finish.
  • Tool must be electrical conductor (copper, brass, aluminium, stainless steel, graphite); diamond NOT used.
  • Gap maintained 0.5–0.75 mm (typical).
  • Highest MRR among unconventional processes; suitable for conducting materials.
  • In ECG variant: electrolyte also acts as coolant.

B. Formulas

Material Removal Rate (volume basis) from Faraday’s laws (matches PYQ calculations):

$$\text{MRR} = \frac{I \cdot A}{Z \cdot F \cdot \rho}$$

Where:

  • MRR = volumetric material removal rate (cm³/s or consistent units)
  • I = current (A)
  • A = gram atomic weight (g/mol or g)
  • Z = valency (number of electrons)
  • F = Faraday’s constant = 96500 C
  • ρ = density of workpiece material (g/cm³)

Mass removal rate = I · A / (Z · F)
(Example from PYQ: iron workpiece, A=56 g, Z=2, ρ=7.8 g/cm³, F=96500 C → solve for I given required MRR.)

C. Visual / Diagram

Source: Mechanical Engineering Course — ECM schematic. Key exam takeaway: DC power supply; tool (cathode) and workpiece (anode) with small gap; electrolyte flow; anodic dissolution at workpiece. Note polarity and gap — critical for exam MCQs.

D. Examiner Traps

  • Tool wear is high — wrong; negligible tool wear (reverse of plating).
  • Gap 0.1 mm or 0.25 mm — typical gap 0.5–0.75 mm.
  • Diamond tool usable — wrong; tool must be conductor; diamond not used.
  • MRR lowest among processes — wrong; ECM has highest MRR.
  • Surface finish bad — wrong; produces mirror finish.

Beam Machining Processes (LBM & EBM)

A. Key Concept Pointers (LBM)

  • Thermal process; material removal by melting, vaporization, or ablation.
  • Usable laser wavelength 0.4–0.6 µm; pulsed laser gives highest power density.
  • Presence of heat-affected zone (HAZ); not suitable for heat-sensitive materials (e.g., aluminium laminates).
  • Can drill micro-holes and cut very hard materials (diamond, etc.).
  • High initial cost; not very efficient.

A. Key Concept Pointers (EBM)

  • Material removal by melting and vaporization due to thermal effect of high-energy electron impingement.
  • Requires vacuum chamber (reduces contamination, electron collisions, and x-ray hazard).
  • Not mechanical erosion or chemical etching.
  • Suitable for small parts; highly trained operators needed.

B. Formulas
LBM cutting rate (mm/min) variants appear garbled in PYQs but relate to power, speed, and energy factors (no single standard formula tested frequently). EBM has no quantitative formula in PYQs.

C. Visual / Diagram (EBM example)

Source: Woodrow Scientific — EBM schematic. Key exam takeaway: Electron gun → vacuum chamber with magnetic lenses → focused beam on workpiece. Note vacuum requirement and thermal melting/vaporization mechanism (not mechanical).

D. Examiner Traps

  • LBM has no HAZ — wrong; HAZ is present.
  • EBM does not require vacuum — wrong; vacuum essential.
  • LBM suitable for all heat-sensitive materials — wrong; thermal process, avoid heat-sensitive.
  • Mechanism in EBM is mechanical erosion — wrong; thermal melting + vaporization.

Plasma Arc Machining (PAM) & Abrasive Jet Machining (AJM)

A. Key Concept Pointers (PAM)

  • High-temperature plasma jet (11,000–28,000°C) melts/vaporizes material.
  • Power supply: current 100–600 A, voltage 50–200 V.
  • Suitable for all electrically conducting materials.
  • Incorrect statement in PYQs: temperature ~1000°C (actual much higher).

A. Key Concept Pointers (AJM)

  • Material removal by erosion from high-velocity abrasive jet.
  • MRR vs nozzle-to-work distance: increases → becomes stable → then decreases (velocity drop).
  • Achievable tolerance ~50 µm.
  • Suitable for sensitive/heat-sensitive materials without damage.

B. Formulas
No closed-form equations in PYQs for these (qualitative trends and parameter ranges only).

C. Visual / Diagram (PAM example)

Source: YouTube educational — PAM torch schematic. Key exam takeaway: Electrode (cathode), nozzle (anode), plasma gas, constricted arc/jet impinging on workpiece. Note high temperature and conducting materials only.

D. Examiner Traps

  • PAM temperature ~1000°C — wrong; 11,000–28,000°C.
  • AJM MRR continuously increases with distance — wrong; increases then stable then decreases.
  • PAM cannot machine conducting materials — wrong; designed for conducting materials.

Ultrasonic Machining (USM)

1. Concept Pointers (MCQ-Optimized)
  • Material is removed by repeated hammering of abrasive particles in a slurry driven by a high-frequency vibrating tool (15–30 kHz).
  • Best suited for hard, brittle materials (glass, ceramics, carbides, quartz, semiconductors); poor for ductile materials.
  • Tool is made of ductile/soft material (mild steel, brass) so that it wears preferentially rather than the workpiece.
  • Abrasive slurry (boron carbide or SiC + water) must be present; without abrasives the process does not cut.
  • MRR is low and increases with amplitude, resonant frequency and abrasive grain size (optimum when grain size ≈ amplitude).
  • Trap: USM is not restricted to non-conducting materials; hard steel can also be machined, but brittle materials give higher MRR.
  • Confused pair: USM vs AJM — USM uses a shaped vibrating tool + slurry; AJM uses a free abrasive jet with no solid tool contact.

4. Worked Application Example

Question pattern: “Ultrasonic machining is best suited for which of the following materials?”
Options typically include non-ferrous metals, amorphous solids, brittle materials, dielectrics.

Reasoning:

  1. Process relies on brittle fracture under repeated abrasive impacts.
  2. Ductile materials absorb energy by plastic deformation → low MRR.
  3. Conductivity is irrelevant (tool is mechanical).
  4. Answer: Brittle materials.

Key Insight: The single most tested fact is “brittle materials”; any option emphasising conductivity or ductility is wrong.

Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM)

1. Concept Pointers (MCQ-Optimized)
  • Material is removed by melting and evaporation caused by repeated electrical sparks in a dielectric fluid.
  • Only electrically conducting materials can be machined (hardness is irrelevant).
  • Workpiece is connected as anode (+); tool as cathode (−) for maximum metal removal and minimum tool wear.
  • Dielectric (usually kerosene) acts as insulator, coolant and flushing medium; servo maintains a constant small gap (0.025–0.05 mm).
  • Tool materials: copper, graphite, brass, tellurium copper; diamond is never used.
  • Better surface finish is obtained at high frequency + low discharge current.
  • Trap: EDM does not require vacuum (that is EBM); tool and work never touch so there is no mechanical force.
  • Confused pair: EDM vs ECM — EDM is thermal spark erosion with tool wear; ECM is electrochemical dissolution with virtually zero tool wear.

4. Worked Application Example

Question pattern: “In EDM the workpiece is connected to …” or “To obtain maximum metal removal and minimum tool wear the polarity should be …”

Reasoning:

  1. Standard polarity for maximum MRR is workpiece = anode, tool = cathode.
  2. Reversing polarity increases tool wear dramatically.
  3. Non-conducting materials cannot be machined at all.
  4. Answer: Workpiece = anode (+).

Key Insight: Polarity is the single most frequently tested fact; any option that places the tool as anode is wrong for normal EDM.

Electrochemical Machining (ECM)

1. Concept Pointers (MCQ-Optimized)
  • Material is removed by anodic dissolution (atomic-level electrochemical reaction) — the reverse of electroplating.
  • Workpiece = anode (+), tool = cathode (−); both must be electrical conductors.
  • Electrolyte (NaCl, NaNO₃, Na₂SO₄ aqueous solutions) is pumped through a small gap (0.5–0.75 mm).
  • Tool wear is negligible; the same tool can produce thousands of parts.
  • Produces mirror-finish surfaces; independent of workpiece hardness.
  • Tool materials: copper, brass, stainless steel, aluminium, graphite; diamond is never used.
  • Trap: ECM does not use kerosene (that is EDM dielectric); surface finish is excellent, not poor.
  • Confused pair: ECM vs ECG — ECG is electrochemical grinding (abrasive wheel + electrolyte); pure ECM has no abrasive action.

4. Worked Application Example

Question pattern: “Which of the following processes has virtually no tool wear?” or “Which process is the reverse of electroplating?”

Reasoning:

  1. ECM removes metal by anodic dissolution; the tool (cathode) does not dissolve.
  2. EDM has measurable tool wear; USM has mild tool wear.
  3. Only ECM is the electrochemical reverse of plating.
  4. Answer: Electrochemical Machining (ECM).

Key Insight: “No tool wear” and “reverse of electroplating” are the two most reliable MCQ identifiers for ECM.

Overall Process Selection & Comparison

1. Concept Pointers (MCQ-Optimized)
  • Highest MRR among the three: ECM; lowest: USM.
  • Non-conducting brittle materials → USM (or LBM/AJM).
  • Conducting hard materials requiring complex cavities → EDM or ECM.
  • Zero tool wear + mirror finish → ECM.
  • Sharp internal corners are difficult in ECM (electrolyte flow limitation) and also limited in EDM (tool wear).
  • Vacuum is required only for EBM, not for EDM/ECM/USM.
  • Trap: “Highest capital cost” is usually ECM; “lowest capital cost” is AJM.

4. Worked Application Example

Question pattern: “A square blind hole 6 mm × 6 mm × 25 mm deep is required in a hard die steel. Which process is most appropriate?”

Reasoning:

  1. Blind complex cavity → EDM (die-sinking capability).
  2. Plasma and laser are line-of-sight and less suitable for deep square cavities.
  3. USM can produce the shape but MRR is too low for 25 mm depth.
  4. Answer: Electro-discharge machining (EDM).

Key Insight: Depth + complex cross-section + hard material → EDM is the classic choice in PYQs.
