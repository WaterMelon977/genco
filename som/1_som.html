1. Stress and Strain — Core Definitions

Stress (σ) is the internal resisting force per unit area developed within a body when subjected to an external load.

$$\sigma = \frac{P}{A}$$

$\sigma$ — normal stress (Pa or N/mm²)$P$ — axial load (N)$A$ — cross-sectional area (m² or mm²)

  • Tensile stress (+): load pulls the bar apart — elongation occurs.
  • Compressive stress (−): load pushes the bar — shortening occurs.
  • Shear stress (τ): developed on planes parallel to the applied force.
$$\tau = \frac{V}{A}$$

$\tau$ — average shear stress (Pa)$V$ — shear force (N)$A$ — area on which shear acts (m²)

Strain (ε) is the ratio of change in dimension to the original dimension. It is dimensionless.

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\delta L}{L}, \qquad \varepsilon_{lat} = -\nu\varepsilon, \qquad e_v = \varepsilon_x + \varepsilon_y + \varepsilon_z$$

$\varepsilon$ — longitudinal strain $\delta L$ — change in length (m) $\varepsilon_{lat}$ — lateral strain $\nu$ — Poisson's ratio $e_v$ — volumetric strain

Exam Trap: Strain is dimensionless — it has no units. Poisson's ratio is also dimensionless. Do not confuse shear strain $\gamma$ (in radians) with shear stress $\tau$ (in Pa).

2. Hooke's Law

Within the elastic limit, stress is directly proportional to strain. The constant of proportionality is the Young's Modulus (Modulus of Elasticity).

$$\sigma = E\varepsilon \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} = \frac{PL}{A\delta L}$$

$E$ — Young's Modulus (Pa or GPa)$\delta L = \frac{PL}{AE}$ — extension of bar

Similarly, for shear: $\tau = G\gamma$, where $G$ is the Shear Modulus (Modulus of Rigidity) and $\gamma$ is the shear strain.

2.1 Extension of Standard Bar Types

Uniform cross-section bar

$$\delta L = \frac{PL}{AE}$$

Tapering bar (circular — linear taper from $d_1$ to $d_2$)

$$\delta L = \frac{4PL}{\pi d_1 d_2 E}$$

$d_1, d_2$ — end diameters (mm or m)

Bar of uniform strength

In a bar of uniform strength, the cross-sectional area varies along the length such that the stress $\sigma$ remains constant throughout, including the effect of self-weight. This is different from a bar of uniform section where self-weight causes varying stress.

Key distinction: "Uniform strength" ≠ "Uniform section." In a uniform strength bar, area increases from bottom to top (in a hanging bar) to maintain constant stress despite varying weight.

3.1 Relations between Elastic Constants

$$E = 2G(1 + \nu)$$
$$E = 3K(1 - 2\nu)$$
$$E = \frac{9KG}{3K + G}$$
Special cases of Poisson's ratio:
  • $\nu = 0.5$ → incompressible material (rubber); $K \to \infty$.
  • $\nu = 0$ → no lateral strain; $E = 2G = 3K$.
  • $0 < \nu < 0.5$ for all real isotropic engineering materials.
  • Steel: $\nu \approx 0.25$–$0.30$; Aluminium: $\nu \approx 0.33$.

4. Compound Bars (Introduction) & Section Properties

A compound bar consists of two or more materials placed in parallel and bonded together so that they deform by the same amount. The load is shared in proportion to their axial stiffness $AE$.

$$P = P_1 + P_2 + \cdots \quad\text{(equilibrium)}, \qquad \delta_1 = \delta_2 \quad\text{(compatibility)}$$
$$\sigma_i = E_i \cdot \varepsilon \quad\text{(all materials have same strain } \varepsilon\text{)}$$

5. Stress–Strain Curve of Mild Steel

The mild steel tensile test produces a characteristic curve that is one of the most tested topics in all competitive exams. Know every region and the physical meaning of each transition point.

Region / PointDescriptionKey Fact
O–A (Proportional limit)Linear; Hooke's law strictly obeyedSlope = $E$
A–B (Elastic limit)No permanent set on unloadingSlightly beyond proportional limit
B (Upper yield point)Sudden drop in load; stress falls suddenlyUnique to mild steel — absent in cast iron, HSS, aluminium
C (Lower yield point)Stress remains nearly constant; plastic flow occursUsed as design yield stress
C–D (Strain hardening)Stress rises again with increased strainCrystal structure realignment (dislocations pile up)
D (Ultimate Tensile Strength, UTS)Maximum stress on nominal areaNecking (local constriction) begins here
D–E (Necking)Nominal stress falls but true stress continues to riseCross-sectional area decreases rapidly
E (Fracture / Breaking point)Actual fractureNominal (engineering) stress at fracture < UTS
Proof stressStress at 0.2% permanent (offset) strainUsed for non-ferrous materials with no clear yield point (aluminium, copper)
Upper yield point explained: In mild steel, carbon atoms (interstitial atoms) pin dislocations in place. A higher stress is needed to free them (upper yield point). Once freed, dislocations move easily at a lower stress (lower yield point). This phenomenon is absent in pure metals, cast iron, and high-strength steels.

5.1 Key Definitions from the Curve

PropertyDefinitionRepresents
ResilienceStrain energy stored per unit volume up to elastic limitArea under elastic region of $\sigma$–$\varepsilon$ curve
Modulus of resilience$u_r = \sigma_y^2 / 2E$Max resilience — area under linear region
ToughnessTotal strain energy per unit volume up to fractureArea under the entire $\sigma$–$\varepsilon$ curve
Ductility% elongation or % reduction in area at fractureAbility to undergo plastic deformation
Resilience vs Toughness: Resilience = ability to absorb energy elastically (spring-like). Toughness = total energy to fracture (needs both strength AND ductility). A hard brittle material may have high strength but low toughness.

Key Points to Remember

  • $\sigma = P/A$ (normal stress); $\tau = V/A$ (shear stress). Units: N/mm² = MPa.
  • Hooke's law: $\sigma = E\varepsilon$ valid only within the elastic limit. Extension: $\delta L = PL/AE$.
  • Tapering bar (circular): $\delta L = 4PL/(\pi d_1 d_2 E)$ — use $d_1 \times d_2$, not average diameter.
  • Three elastic constant relations: $E = 2G(1+\nu) = 3K(1-2\nu)$; $E = 9KG/(3K+G)$.
  • $\nu = 0.5$ → incompressible (rubber, $K\to\infty$). Real materials: $0 < \nu < 0.5$.
  • Upper yield point: unique to mild steel due to dislocation pinning by interstitial carbon.
  • Lower yield point (not upper) is used as the design yield stress $\sigma_y$.
  • Proof stress (0.2% offset) is used for aluminium, copper — metals without a distinct yield point.
  • Modulus of resilience = $\sigma_y^2/2E$. Toughness = area under entire $\sigma$–$\varepsilon$ curve.
  • Toughness requires both high strength AND high ductility — mild steel is tougher than glass even if glass is harder.
