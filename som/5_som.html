Torsion of Circular Shafts & Springs
Torsion Formula · Polar Moment · Power Transmission · Helical & Leaf Springs

1. Torsion of Circular Shafts

When a circular shaft is subjected to a twisting couple (torque $T$), shear stresses are induced. The stress distribution is linear — zero at the axis, maximum at the outer surface.

1.1 Torsion Formula (Analogy with Flexure Formula)

$$\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G\theta}{L}$$

$T$ — applied torque (N·m) $J$ — polar moment of inertia (m⁴) $\tau$ — shear stress at radius $r$ (Pa) $r$ — radial distance from axis (m) $G$ — modulus of rigidity / shear modulus (Pa) $\theta$ — angle of twist (radians) $L$ — length of shaft (m)

1.2 Maximum Shear Stress and Angle of Twist

$$\tau_{max} = \frac{T \cdot R}{J} = \frac{16T}{\pi D^3} \quad\text{(solid circular shaft)}$$
$$\theta = \frac{TL}{GJ} \quad\text{(angle of twist, radians)}$$

$R = D/2$ — outer radius (m) $D$ — outer diameter (m) $J = \pi D^4/32$ for solid shaft

Shear stress variation: Shear stress is zero at the axis (centre of solid shaft) and maximum at the outer surface. The material at the centre of a solid shaft is therefore lightly stressed — hence hollow shafts are more material-efficient for torsion.

2. Polar Moment of Inertia & Polar Section Modulus

2.1 Polar Moment of Inertia $J$

Section$J$ (polar moment)$\tau_{max}$
Solid circular shaft (dia $D$)$\pi D^4/32$$16T/\pi D^3$
Hollow circular shaft ($D$, $d$)$\pi(D^4 - d^4)/32$$16TD/\pi(D^4 - d^4)$

2.2 Polar Section Modulus $Z_p$

$$Z_p = \frac{J}{R}, \qquad \tau_{max} = \frac{T}{Z_p}$$
  • Solid shaft: $Z_p = J/R = \pi D^3/16$
  • Hollow shaft: $Z_p = \pi(D^4 - d^4)/(16D)$

2.3 Relation between $I$, $J$, and $I_P$

$$J = I_P = I_x + I_y = 2I \quad\text{(for circular section, where } I_x = I_y = I\text{)}$$

For a circular cross-section: $J = \pi D^4/32$ and $I = \pi D^4/64$, so $J = 2I$. This is the perpendicular axis theorem.

2.4 Hollow Shaft vs Solid Shaft (Same Weight and Material)

For the same cross-sectional area (same weight per unit length and same material), a hollow shaft has a larger polar moment $J$ than a solid shaft, making it stronger and stiffer in torsion.

Standard result: A hollow shaft of the same weight and material is stronger in torsion than a solid shaft. The advantage comes from distributing material at a larger radius (where shear stress and hence moment arm are maximum).

3. Power Transmitted by a Shaft

$$P = \frac{2\pi N T}{60} = T \cdot \omega$$

$P$ — power (W = J/s) $N$ — rotational speed (rpm) $T$ — torque (N·m) $\omega = 2\pi N/60$ — angular velocity (rad/s)

$$T = \frac{60P}{2\pi N} = \frac{P}{\omega}$$

4. Springs

4.1 Close-Coiled Helical Spring (Axial Load)

When a close-coiled helical spring (helix angle $\alpha < 10°$) is subjected to an axial load $W$, it is primarily under torsion. Bending effects are negligible.

$$\tau = \frac{8WD}{\pi d^3} \quad\text{(max shear stress, Wahl's correction neglected)}$$
$$\delta = \frac{64 W R^3 n}{G d^4} = \frac{8 W D^3 n}{G d^4}$$
$$k = \frac{W}{\delta} = \frac{G d^4}{64 R^3 n} = \frac{G d^4}{8 D^3 n}$$

$W$ — axial load (N) $D = 2R$ — mean coil diameter (m) $R$ — mean coil radius (m) $d$ — wire diameter (m) $n$ — number of active coils $G$ — shear modulus (Pa) $k$ — spring stiffness / spring rate (N/m)

Wahl's correction factor: The formula above neglects the curvature effect and direct shear. Wahl's factor $K_W$ accounts for these: $\tau = K_W \cdot 8WD/(\pi d^3)$, where $K_W = (4C-1)/(4C-4) + 0.615/C$ and $C = D/d$ (spring index). In most exam problems, Wahl's correction is not applied unless specified.

4.2 Spring Stiffness Dependency

$$k \propto \frac{d^4}{D^3 n} = \frac{d^4}{R^3 n}$$
  • Stiffness $k$ increases with the 4th power of wire diameter $d$ — doubling $d$ increases $k$ by 16×.
  • Stiffness $k$ decreases with the 3rd power of coil radius $R$ — doubling $R$ decreases $k$ by 8×.
  • Stiffness $k$ decreases linearly with number of coils $n$.

4.3 Springs in Series and Parallel

ConfigurationEquivalent Stiffness $k_{eq}$Condition
Springs in series$\dfrac{1}{k_{eq}} = \dfrac{1}{k_1} + \dfrac{1}{k_2}$Same load $W$; total deflection = sum
Springs in parallel$k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2$Same deflection; load is shared
Analogy with capacitors: Springs in series → stiffnesses add like capacitors in series (reciprocal formula). Springs in parallel → stiffnesses add directly like capacitors in parallel. This is opposite to resistors/inductors.

4.4 Open-Coiled Helical Spring

An open-coiled helical spring has a significant helix angle $\alpha > 10°$. Under axial load, it experiences both:

  • Torsion ($T$): $T = WR\cos\alpha$ — tends to close the coils.
  • Bending ($M$): $M = WR\sin\alpha$ — tends to increase helix angle.

Both torsion and bending must be considered together for open-coiled springs. A close-coiled spring is the special case where $\alpha \approx 0$ and bending is negligible.

4.5 Leaf Spring (Semi-Elliptic)

$$\delta = \frac{3WL^3}{8Enbt^3}$$
$$\sigma_{max} = \frac{3WL}{2nbt^2}$$

$W$ — central transverse load (N) $L$ — half-length (span/2) of spring (m) $n$ — number of leaves $b$ — width of each leaf (m) $t$ — thickness of each leaf (m)

A leaf spring operates primarily in bending (unlike helical springs which operate in torsion). The leaves act as a beam in bending.

Key Points to Remember

  • Torsion formula: $T/J = \tau/r = G\theta/L$. Max shear stress at outer surface; zero at axis.
  • Solid shaft: $J = \pi D^4/32$; $\tau_{max} = 16T/\pi D^3$; $\theta = TL/GJ$.
  • Hollow shaft (same weight as solid) → larger $J$ → stronger and stiffer in torsion.
  • $J = 2I$ for circular section (from perpendicular axis theorem: $J = I_x + I_y = 2I$).
  • Power: $P = 2\pi NT/60 = T\omega$. To find $T$: $T = 60P/(2\pi N)$.
  • Close-coiled helical spring: primarily torsion. $\delta = 64WR^3n/(Gd^4)$; $k = Gd^4/(64R^3n)$.
  • Stiffness $k \propto d^4$ (wire dia) and $k \propto 1/R^3$ (coil radius). Doubling $d$ → $k$ increases 16×.
  • Series springs: $1/k_{eq} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2$ (deflections add). Parallel: $k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2$ (loads add).
  • Open-coiled spring: both torsion AND bending act. Close-coiled: bending negligible ($\alpha < 10°$).
  • Leaf spring: operates in bending; $\delta = 3WL^3/(8Enbt^3)$. Used in vehicle suspension.
  • Strain energy in torsion: $U = T^2L/(2GJ)$; per unit volume: $u = \tau^2/(2G)$.
