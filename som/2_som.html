1. Compound Bars

A compound bar consists of two or more materials in parallel (same deformation, shared load). The governing equations are:

  • Equilibrium: Total load $P = P_1 + P_2 + \cdots$ (sum of forces in each member).
  • Compatibility: $\delta_1 = \delta_2 = \cdots$ (same deformation for all members).
  • Constitutive: $\sigma_i = E_i \varepsilon$ (common strain $\varepsilon$; stress proportional to E).
$$\varepsilon = \frac{P}{A_1 E_1 + A_2 E_2}, \qquad \sigma_i = E_i \cdot \varepsilon = \frac{P \cdot E_i}{A_1 E_1 + A_2 E_2}$$

$P$ — total applied load (N) $A_i$ — area of material $i$ (mm²) $E_i$ — Young's modulus of material $i$ (GPa) $\sigma_i$ — stress in material $i$ (MPa)

Key result: In a compound bar, stresses are proportional to Young's Moduli (not areas). The stiffer material (higher $E$) takes more stress. Equilibrium distributes load; compatibility ensures equal strain.

2. Temperature Stresses

When a bar is prevented from expanding or contracting freely due to a temperature change, thermal stresses develop.

2.1 Fully Restrained Bar

$$\delta_T = \alpha L \Delta T \quad\text{(free thermal expansion)}$$
$$\sigma_T = E \alpha \Delta T \quad\text{(stress when fully restrained)}$$

$\alpha$ — coefficient of linear thermal expansion (°C⁻¹) $\Delta T$ — temperature change (°C) $\sigma_T$ — thermal stress (compressive on heating if restrained)

Sign convention: If a bar is heated and fully restrained, it cannot expand → compressive thermal stress develops. If cooled and restrained, tensile stress develops. If partially restrained, stress is less than $E\alpha\Delta T$.

2.2 Compound Bar — Thermal Compatibility

In a compound bar with two materials at the same temperature change $\Delta T$, one will be in tension and the other in compression. The compatibility equation (assuming $\alpha_1 > \alpha_2$, material 1 tends to expand more):

$$\alpha_1 \Delta T - \frac{\sigma_1}{E_1} = \alpha_2 \Delta T + \frac{\sigma_2}{E_2}$$

$\sigma_1$ — compressive stress in material 1 (material 1 is restrained from expanding) $\sigma_2$ — tensile stress in material 2 (pulled by material 1)

Combined with equilibrium $\sigma_1 A_1 = \sigma_2 A_2$ (if both have same length), the stresses can be found.

3. Stresses on Oblique Planes

For a bar under uniaxial stress $\sigma_x$, consider a plane inclined at angle $\theta$ to the cross-section (or $\theta$ to the axial direction).

3.1 General 2D Stress State

For an element with normal stresses $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and shear stress $\tau_{xy}$, the stresses on a plane inclined at angle $\theta$ from the $x$-axis are:

$$\sigma_\theta = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} + \frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta$$
$$\tau_\theta = -\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\cos 2\theta$$

$\sigma_\theta$ — normal stress on the inclined plane $\tau_\theta$ — shear stress on the inclined plane $\theta$ — angle from the $x$-plane to the inclined plane

3.2 Special Case: Pure Uniaxial Stress ($\sigma_y = 0$, $\tau_{xy} = 0$)

$$\sigma_\theta = \sigma_x \cos^2\theta = \frac{\sigma_x(1+\cos 2\theta)}{2}$$
$$\tau_\theta = \frac{\sigma_x}{2}\sin 2\theta$$
  • Max normal stress = $\sigma_x$ at $\theta = 0°$ (axial plane).
  • Max shear stress = $\sigma_x/2$ at $\theta = 45°$ — this is why ductile materials yield in shear at 45°.
  • At $\theta = 90°$: $\sigma_\theta = 0$, $\tau_\theta = 0$ (free surface perpendicular to load).

4. Principal Stresses and Principal Planes

Principal planes are planes on which shear stress is zero. The normal stresses on these planes are called principal stresses — maximum ($\sigma_1$) and minimum ($\sigma_2$).

$$\sigma_1, \sigma_2 = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$
$$\tau_{max} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_2}{2} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$

$\sigma_1$ — major principal stress (algebraically largest) $\sigma_2$ — minor principal stress (algebraically smallest) $\tau_{max}$ — maximum shear stress (acts at 45° to principal planes)

4.1 Angles of Principal and Max Shear Planes

$$\tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x - \sigma_y} \quad\text{(principal plane angle)}$$
  • Principal planes are at $\theta_p$ and $\theta_p + 90°$.
  • Maximum shear stress planes are at $45°$ to the principal planes.
  • On the maximum shear stress planes, the normal stress = $(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)/2$.
Important: Maximum shear stress planes are NOT stress-free — they carry a normal stress of $(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)/2$ (the average normal stress). Only the principal planes are shear-stress free.

4.2 Principal Strains

The strains corresponding to principal stresses (using generalized Hooke's law for isotropic material):

$$\varepsilon_1 = \frac{\sigma_1 - \nu\sigma_2}{E}, \qquad \varepsilon_2 = \frac{\sigma_2 - \nu\sigma_1}{E}$$

5. Mohr's Stress Circle

Mohr's circle is a graphical method for visualizing the complete state of stress at a point. It is plotted on $(\sigma, \tau)$ axes.

5.1 Construction Rules

  • Centre: $C = \left(\dfrac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2},\ 0\right)$ on the $\sigma$-axis.
  • Radius: $R = \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$
  • Principal stresses: $\sigma_1 = C + R$ and $\sigma_2 = C - R$ (intercepts on $\sigma$-axis).
  • Maximum shear stress: = $R$ = radius of the circle.
  • Angle doubling rule: An angle $\theta$ in the physical element corresponds to $2\theta$ on Mohr's circle.
$$\text{Centre: } C = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2}, \qquad \text{Radius: } R = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$

Key Points to Remember

  • Compound bar: load shared in proportion to $AE$; stress proportional to $E$ (not area). Common strain: $\varepsilon = P/(A_1E_1 + A_2E_2)$.
  • Thermal stress (fully restrained): $\sigma_T = E\alpha\Delta T$ — compressive on heating, tensile on cooling.
  • Partial restraint → smaller stress than $E\alpha\Delta T$. Free bar → no stress at all.
  • On oblique plane at $45°$ under uniaxial stress $\sigma_x$: $\tau_{max} = \sigma_x/2$. This explains why mild steel (ductile) fails in shear at $45°$.
  • Principal planes: shear stress = 0. Principal stresses = max and min normal stresses.
  • $\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 = \sigma_x + \sigma_y$ (sum of normal stresses is invariant — same on any plane).
  • Max shear stress = radius of Mohr's circle = $(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)/2$.
  • Max shear stress planes are at $45°$ to principal planes, NOT stress-free — they carry normal stress $(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)/2$.
  • Mohr's circle angle rule: physical angle $\theta$ → $2\theta$ on circle.
  • Pure shear: Mohr's circle centred at origin; $\sigma_1 = \tau$, $\sigma_2 = -\tau$ — principal stresses are equal and opposite.
