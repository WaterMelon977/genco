1. Deflections of Beams

Beam deflection is governed by the differential equation of the elastic curve:

$$EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = M(x)$$

$EI$ — flexural rigidity (N·m²) $y$ — transverse deflection (m) $M(x)$ — bending moment at section $x$ (N·m)

Key insight: Deflection is inversely proportional to flexural rigidity $EI$. Increasing $I$ (by using I-sections or wider flanges) is the most efficient way to reduce deflection, as $I$ depends on the cube of depth.

1.2 Methods for Finding Deflections

  • Double integration method: Integrate $EI\,d^2y/dx^2 = M$ twice; apply boundary conditions. Suitable for simple loadings.
  • Macaulay's method: Extension of double integration for discontinuous loads using angle brackets $\langle x - a \rangle^n$. Integrates the entire BM equation in one go.
  • Moment-area method (Mohr's theorems):
    • 1st theorem: Change in slope between two points = area of $M/EI$ diagram between them.
    • 2nd theorem: Deflection of point B relative to tangent at A = first moment of $M/EI$ area about B.
  • Conjugate beam method: Replace real beam with a "conjugate beam" carrying $M/EI$ as a fictitious load; slope = SF in conjugate beam; deflection = BM in conjugate beam.

2. Columns and Struts — Euler's Theory

Column: Vertical structural member carrying axial compressive load. Strut: A compression member that may be inclined. Both fail by buckling when the axial load exceeds a critical value.

Crippling Load (Euler's Critical Load, $P_{cr}$): The minimum axial compressive load at which a column tends to buckle laterally (become unstable).

2.1 Euler's Critical Load Formula

$$P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{L_{eff}^2}$$

$E$ — Young's Modulus (Pa) $I$ — least second moment of area (m⁴) — always use minimum $I$ $L_{eff}$ — effective length (m) — depends on end conditions

2.2 End Conditions and Effective Lengths

End Condition$L_{eff}$$P_{cr}$ (Euler)Relative strength

Memory Aid: End conditions in order of increasing strength: Fixed–Free < Pin–Pin < Fixed–Pin < Fixed–Fixed. Ratios: 0.25 : 1 : 2 : 4.

2.3 Slenderness Ratio

$$\lambda = \frac{L_{eff}}{k}, \qquad k = \sqrt{\frac{I}{A}} \text{ (radius of gyration)}$$

$\lambda$ — slenderness ratio (dimensionless) $k$ — minimum radius of gyration (m) — use minimum $k$ (smallest $I/A$) $\lambda_{cr}$ — critical slenderness ratio beyond which Euler's formula is valid

Euler's formula validity: Euler's formula applies only to long slender columns where $\lambda > \lambda_{cr} = \pi\sqrt{E/\sigma_y}$. For short or intermediate columns, Rankine's formula is used. Euler gives an unsafe (over-optimistic) answer for short columns.

3. Rankine's Formula (Combined Formula)

Rankine's formula gives the crippling load for all column lengths — it bridges the gap between short (crushing) and long (buckling) columns:

$$P_{Rankine} = \frac{\sigma_c \cdot A}{1 + a\left(\dfrac{L_{eff}}{k}\right)^2}$$

$\sigma_c$ — compressive yield stress (N/m²) $A$ — cross-sectional area (m²) $a = \sigma_c/(\pi^2 E)$ — Rankine's constant (empirical) $L_{eff}/k$ — slenderness ratio $\lambda$

  • For short columns ($\lambda \approx 0$): $P_{Rankine} \approx \sigma_c \cdot A$ (crushing load governs).
  • For long columns ($\lambda$ large): $P_{Rankine} \approx \pi^2EI/L_{eff}^2$ (Euler's load governs).
  • Rankine's formula is always more conservative (lower) than Euler's formula for the same column.

4. Strain Energy

Strain energy (U) is the energy stored in an elastic body due to deformation. It equals the work done by the external forces during loading (for slow, quasi-static loading).

4.1 Strain Energy Formulas for Different Load Types

Bending$\int M^2\,dx/(2EI)$—
Torsion$T^2L/(2GJ)$$\tau^2/(2G)$ (outer fibre)
$$U = \frac{P^2 L}{2AE} = \frac{\sigma^2 V}{2E} = \frac{\sigma^2 A L}{2E}$$

4.2 Impact Loading

When a load $P$ is dropped from height $h$ onto a bar, the impact stress $\sigma_{impact}$ is found by equating strain energy in the bar to the work done by the falling load:

$$\sigma_{impact} = \frac{P}{A}\left[1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{2hAE}{PL}}\right]$$

4.3 Special Load Cases

CaseStressNote
Gradual (static) load$\sigma_{static} = P/A$Base case
Sudden load ($h = 0$, dropped from zero height)$\sigma_{sudden} = 2P/A = 2\sigma_{static}$Twice the static stress
Impact load (dropped from $h > 0$)$\sigma_{impact} > \sigma_{sudden} > \sigma_{static}$Always: impact > sudden > static
Sudden load doubles the stress: A load applied suddenly (released from rest just touching the bar, $h = 0$) produces twice the stress of the same load applied gradually. This is a very common exam question. For $h > 0$, stress is even higher.

4.4 Proof Resilience and Modulus of Resilience

$$u_r = \frac{\sigma_y^2}{2E} \quad\text{(modulus of resilience — per unit volume)}$$
$$U_{proof} = \frac{\sigma_y^2 \cdot V}{2E} \quad\text{(proof resilience — for entire bar of volume } V\text{)}$$

Key Points to Remember

  • Deflection $\propto 1/EI$. Adding flanges (I-section) dramatically increases $I$ ($\propto d^3$) with little extra mass.
  • SS beam, central load: $\delta = WL^3/(48EI)$; Cantilever, end load: $\delta = WL^3/(3EI)$. Ratio = 1:16 for same $W$, $L$, $EI$.
  • Euler: $P_{cr} = \pi^2EI/L_{eff}^2$. Use minimum $I$ (least radius of gyration).
  • End condition strengths: Fixed–Free (0.25) < Pin–Pin (1) < Fixed–Pin (2) < Fixed–Fixed (4).
  • Fixed–Free is the weakest ($L_{eff} = 2L$); Fixed–Fixed is the strongest ($L_{eff} = L/2$).
  • Euler's formula valid only for long columns ($\lambda > \lambda_{cr}$). For short/intermediate: use Rankine.
  • Rankine approaches Euler for long columns and $\sigma_c A$ for short columns.
  • Slenderness ratio $\lambda = L_{eff}/k$; radius of gyration $k = \sqrt{I/A}$.
  • Strain energy: axial = $P^2L/(2AE)$; bending = $\int M^2/(2EI)\,dx$; torsion = $T^2L/(2GJ)$.
  • Sudden load → stress = 2 × static stress ($h = 0$). Impact ($h > 0$) → even higher. Always: impact > sudden > static.
  • Modulus of resilience = $\sigma_y^2/(2E)$ — high for high-strength, low-modulus materials (e.g., spring steel).
