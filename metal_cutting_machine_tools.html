Metal Cutting and Machine Tools

1. Topic Overview

This guide covers metal cutting fundamentals, tool geometry, chip formation, Merchant theory, Taylor tool-life relation, cutting speed and forces, and tool wear for AP Genco AE-style preparation. The highest-frequency PYQ areas are ASA tool signature, chip thickness ratio, shear angle, Taylor’s equation, cutting speed calculations, force relations, and flank/crater wear identification. The content below consolidates repeated theory, formulas, diagrams, worked examples, and the most useful exam points into one clean revision guide.

2. Quick Reference Sheet

$$\boxed{\text{Lip angle} = 90^\circ - $\text{rake} + \text{clearance}$}$$

$$\boxed{r = \frac{t}{t_c} = \frac{\sin\phi}{\cos$\phi-\alpha$}}$$
$$\boxed{r = \frac{l_c}{l}}$$
Symbol Meaning Unit

(t_c) chip thickness after cutting mm
(l_c) chip length mm
(l) cut length mm

$\alpha$ rake angle degree

Merchant relation

$$\boxed{2\phi + \beta - \alpha = 90^\circ}$$
$$\boxed{\phi = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}}$$
$$\boxed{\tan\phi = \frac{r\cos\alpha}{1-r\sin\alpha}}$$
Symbol Meaning Unit

$$\boxed{VT^n = C}$$
$$\boxed{V_1T_1^n = V_2T_2^n}$$

2.2 Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Orthogonal cutting: Two-dimensional cutting model where the cutting edge is perpendicular to tool travel.
  • Oblique cutting: Three-dimensional cutting model with an inclined cutting edge.
  • Shear angle: Angle between the shear plane and the cutting direction.
  • Continuous chip: Long, ductile chip formed at high speed, low feed, and high rake.
  • Discontinuous chip: Segmented chip formed with brittle materials or harsh cutting conditions.
  • Built-up edge: Chip material that adheres to the tool tip under certain speed and friction conditions.
  • Positive rake: Reduces cutting force but weakens the tool edge.
  • Negative rake: Strengthens the edge and is used for hard or brittle materials.
  • Clearance angle: Prevents flank rubbing between tool and workpiece.
  • Flank wear: Wear on the clearance faces of the tool.
  • Crater wear: Wear on the rake face, away from the cutting edge.
  • Hot hardness: Ability of a cutting tool material to retain hardness at high temperature.
  • Chatter: Self-excited vibration in machining.

2.3 Standard Values / Constants

  • ASA elements: 7 elements in fixed order.
  • Typical clearance angle: about 6� to 12�.
  • Common tool-life exponent ranges: HSS 0.08-0.2, carbide 0.2-0.4, ceramic 0.5-0.7.
  • Flank-wear criterion: VB about 0.3-0.6 mm.
  • Heat distribution trend: chip carries away the most heat.
  • Heat generation: about 80-85 percent occurs in the primary shear zone.

3. Main Body

3.1 Tool Geometry and ASA Signature

1. Concept Pointers
  • ASA signature contains exactly seven elements in a fixed order.
  • Back rake and side rake control cutting action and chip flow.
  • Relief angles prevent rubbing and friction on the flank faces.
  • Cutting-edge angles define the tool point geometry.
  • Nose radius improves surface finish and edge strength.
  • Positive rake reduces cutting force but weakens the cutting edge.
  • Negative rake is preferred for hard, brittle, and interrupted cutting conditions.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{\alpha_b - \alpha_s - \gamma_e - \gamma_s - C_e - C_s - r}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Lip angle} = 90^\circ - $\text{rake} + \text{clearance}$}$$

Conditions and usage

  • Nose radius is usually stated in mm in modern problems.
  • ASA order must be read strictly left to right.
  • The second element is side rake angle.

Worked Example 1

Given: Tool signature 0�-10�-8�-6�-20�-60�-0 mm.
Find: Side rake angle.

3.2 Chip Formation and Chip Thickness Ratio

1. Concept Pointers
  • Continuous chips usually occur with ductile materials, high speed, low feed, and high rake.
  • Discontinuous chips usually occur with brittle materials or severe cutting conditions.
  • Continuous chips with built-up edge form when friction is high and speed is moderate.
  • Higher cutting speed tends to reduce built-up edge.
  • Chip thickness ratio is always less than 1 in ordinary turning.
  • Good surface finish is associated with continuous chips, but handling them is difficult.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{r = \frac{t}{t_c} = \frac{\sin\phi}{\cos$\phi-\alpha$}}$$
$$\boxed{r = \frac{l_c}{l}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • (r < 1) in ordinary turning.
  • Use either thickness form or length form consistently.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Cut length $l = 76$ mm, chip length $l_c = 61$ mm, uncut thickness (t = 0.2) mm.
Find: Chip thickness (t_c).

Solution:

  1. $r = l_c/l = 61/76 \approx 0.803$.
  2. $t_c = t/r = 0.2/0.803 \approx 0.249$ mm.
  3. Answer: 0.25 mm.

Key Insight: Chip ratio can be obtained from lengths or thicknesses; both forms are equivalent.

3.3 Merchant Theory and Shear Angle

1. Concept Pointers
  • Shear angle is the angle between the shear plane and the cutting direction.
  • Merchant theory is based on minimum energy consumption.
  • Larger shear angle means thinner chips and generally lower cutting force.
  • Friction angle and rake angle appear together in the standard Merchant relation.
  • Orthogonal cutting is the usual assumption for the theory.
  • Built-up edge is ignored in the ideal Merchant model.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{2\phi + \beta - \alpha = 90^\circ}$$
$$\boxed{\phi = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}}$$
$$\boxed{\tan\phi = \frac{r\cos\alpha}{1-r\sin\alpha}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • $\phi$ is shear angle, $\beta$ is friction angle, and $\alpha$ is rake angle.
  • The relation is standard in orthogonal cutting problems.
3. Diagram


Force circle showing cutting force, thrust force, shear force, normal force, friction force, and the angles $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\phi$. Source: LearnEngineering.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: $\alpha = 20^\circ$, $\phi = 30^\circ$.
Find: Friction angle $\beta$.

Solution:

  1. Use Merchant relation: $2\phi + \beta - \alpha = 90^\circ$.
  2. (2(30) + \beta - 20 = 90).
  3. $\beta = 50^\circ$.
  4. Answer: 50�.

Key Insight: The sign before rake angle must be handled carefully; that is the most common exam mistake.

3.4 Taylor Tool-Life Equation

1. Concept Pointers
  • Taylor’s equation relates cutting speed and tool life empirically.
  • Smaller (n) means tool life is more sensitive to speed changes.
  • (C) is the speed corresponding to one-minute tool life.
  • Tool life changes sharply when cutting speed changes.
  • The equation is valid when feed and depth of cut are held constant.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{VT^n = C}$$
$$\boxed{V_1T_1^n = V_2T_2^n}$$

Conditions and usage

  • HSS generally has lower (n) than carbide or ceramic.
  • Larger (n) means less sensitivity of tool life to cutting speed.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: (n = 0.5), $V_1 = 18$ m/min, $T_1 = 180$ min, $T_2 = 45$ min.
Find: (V_2).

Solution:

  1. $V_1T_1^n = V_2T_2^n$.
  2. $18\sqrt{180} = V_2\sqrt{45}$.
  3. $V_2 = 36$ m/min.
  4. Answer: 36 m/min.

Key Insight: The inverse power relation is the entire trick. When (n = 0.5), halving speed has a strong effect on life.

3.5 Cutting Speed, Force, Heat, and Power

1. Concept Pointers
  • Cutting speed is based on workpiece diameter and spindle speed.
  • Cutting force (F_c) is the primary force used in power calculation.
  • Coefficient of friction can be derived from measured cutting and thrust forces.
  • Most heat is generated in the primary shear zone.
  • The chip carries away the maximum share of heat.
  • Increasing rake angle generally reduces cutting force and power.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{V = \frac{\pi DN}{1000}}$$
$$\boxed{\mu = \frac{F_c\sin\alpha + F_t\cos\alpha}{F_c\cos\alpha - F_t\sin\alpha}}$$
$$\boxed{V_c = Vr}$$
$$\boxed{F_s = F_c\cos\phi - F_t\sin\phi}$$
$$\boxed{F_n = F_c\sin\phi + F_t\cos\phi}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Power} \approx F_cV}$$

Conditions and usage

  • Use (V) in m/min, (D) in mm, and (N) in rpm.
  • Heat partition trend: chip > tool > workpiece > environment.
3. Diagram

The Merchant force circle in Section 3.3 also serves this section because it contains the same force components.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: $F_c = 3000$ N, $F_t = 1200$ N, $\alpha = 32^\circ$ with $\sin 32^\circ \approx 0.53$ and $\cos 32^\circ \approx 0.85$.
Find: Coefficient of friction $\mu$.

Solution:

  1. Numerator = (3000(0.53) + 1200(0.85) = 2610).
  2. Denominator = (3000(0.85) - 1200(0.53) = 1914).
  3. $\mu \approx 2610/1914 \approx 1.36$.
  4. Answer: about 1.37.

Key Insight: The denominator must be written with the minus sign before the thrust term.

3.6 Tool Wear

1. Concept Pointers
  • Flank wear occurs on the clearance or relief faces.
  • Crater wear occurs on the rake face, away from the tool tip.
  • Crater wear is mainly due to diffusion and high temperature.
  • Built-up edge tends to increase heat and accelerate wear.
  • Flank wear land width is commonly used as a tool-life criterion.
2. Formulas

No closed-form equation is needed for the PYQ-style questions here.

3. Diagram


Standard labelled wear diagram showing flank wear on the clearance face and crater wear on the rake face. Source: LearnMech.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Wear is observed on the face of the tool a short distance from the cutting edge.
Find: Type of wear and main mechanism.

Solution:

  1. Wear on the rake face away from the edge indicates crater wear.
  2. At high temperature, the dominant mechanism is diffusion.
  3. Answer: Crater wear due to diffusion.

Key Insight: The location tells you the type immediately: rake face means crater wear, flank face means flank wear.

4. Consolidation Notes

Note on ASA signature and tool geometry:
The ASA order never changes, so questions usually test identification rather than derivation. Read the seven values in sequence and identify the second element as side rake.
See Quick Reference Section 2.2.

Note on Merchant theory and cutting force:
Merchant’s relation, chip ratio, friction angle, and force circle all belong to the same orthogonal-cutting framework. If one variable is given, the others usually follow from the same relation set.
See Sections 3.3 and 3.5.

Note on tool wear:
Flank wear and crater wear are often asked together, but the difference is mainly location and mechanism. Crater wear starts on the rake face, while flank wear appears on the clearance faces.
See Section 3.6.

Quick MCQ Points

1. Tool Geometry / ASA Signature

  • ASA signature always has exactly 7 elements.
  • Fixed order: Back rake, Side rake, End relief, Side relief, End cutting-edge angle, Side cutting-edge angle, Nose radius.
  • Nose radius unit is usually mm in modern problems.
  • Lip angle is the angle between face and flank.
  • Negative rake is used for ceramic, CBN, hard/brittle materials, and high-speed cutting.
  • Positive rake reduces cutting force but weakens the tool edge.

2. Chip Formation

  • Continuous chips occur with ductile materials, high speed, low feed, and high rake.
  • Discontinuous chips occur with brittle materials.
  • Continuous chips with built-up edge occur at low-to-medium speed with high friction.
  • Chip thickness ratio $r = t/t_c$ is always less than 1.
  • Higher cutting speed reduces built-up edge.
  • Continuous chips give good finish but are hard to handle.

3. Shear Angle and Merchant Theory

  • Shear angle $\phi$ is the angle between the shear plane and the direction of tool travel.
  • Standard Merchant relation: $2\phi + \beta - \alpha = 90^\circ$.
  • Equivalent form: $\phi = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}$.
  • Higher $\phi$ means thinner chip and lower cutting force.
  • From chip ratio: $\tan\phi = \frac{r\cos\alpha}{1-r\sin\alpha}$.

4. Taylor’s Tool Life

  • Equation: ^n = C$.
  • Typical (n) values: HSS 0.08-0.2, carbide 0.2-0.4, ceramic 0.5-0.7.
  • Smaller (n) means tool life is more sensitive to speed change.
  • Doubling cutting speed sharply reduces tool life.
  • (C) is the cutting speed for 1-minute tool life.

5. Cutting Speed, Forces, and Heat

  • $V = \frac{\pi DN}{1000}$ with D in mm and V in m/min.
  • Primary force used for power is tangential force (F_c).
  • Coefficient of friction: $\mu = \frac{F_c\sin\alpha + F_t\cos\alpha}{F_c\cos\alpha - F_t\sin\alpha}$.
  • About 80-85% of heat is generated in the shear zone.
  • Heat carried away order: Chip > Tool > Workpiece > Environment.
  • Higher rake angle reduces cutting power.

6. Tool Wear

  • Flank wear occurs on relief or clearance faces.
  • Crater wear occurs on the rake face, away from the cutting edge.
  • Crater wear main cause is diffusion plus high temperature.
  • Crater starts away from the tip because temperature is maximum there.
  • Built-up edge increases heat and accelerates wear.
  • Flank wear land VB about 0.3-0.6 mm is a common tool-life criterion.

Extra High-Frequency One-Liners

  • Orthogonal cutting means the cutting edge is perpendicular to tool travel.
  • Oblique cutting means the cutting edge is inclined and the cutting is 3-D.
  • Continuous chips with BUE form on ductile materials at low-to-medium speed.
  • For brass, rake angle of an HSS tool is usually 0�.
  • Chatter in machine tools is caused by self-excited vibrations.
