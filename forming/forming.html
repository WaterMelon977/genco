FORMING: Metal Working Processes
Revision Guide for Competitive Exams

High-frequency topics: Blanking/Punching clearance rules, rolling draft, deep drawing blank size, hot vs cold working, forging defects.

1. Process-by-Process Comprehensive Guide

1.1 Metallurgical Fundamentals: Hot vs Cold Working

Hot Working: Deformation occurs above recrystallization temperature $T_{\text{recrys}}$. Metal recrystallizes continuously, allowing larger deformations with lower forces. Produces refined grain structure, eliminates segregation. Disadvantages: lower accuracy, surface oxidation (scale), tooling cost.

Cold Working: Deformation below $T_{\text{recrys}}$. No recrystallization during forming; work-hardening increases strength and hardness. Better dimensional accuracy and surface finish. Higher forces required. Examples: cold rolling sheet steel, wire drawing, coin stamping.

Figure 1.1: Microstructural evolution in Hot Working (continuous recrystallization) vs Cold Working (grain elongation & strain hardening).

Critical Fact: Temperature classification is material-specific, not process-specific. Lead rolled at 20°C is hot working (since $T_{\text{recrys}} \approx 0$°C for lead); steel at 400°C is cold working (since $T_{\text{recrys}} \approx 700$°C for steel).

Hot vs Cold Working High-Yield Defect Matrix

Defect / Phenomenon Process Association Most-Asked Root Cause & Mechanism
Scale Formation Hot Working Oxidation at high temperature when exposed to atmospheric air; produces thick oxide crust.
Decarburization Hot Working Loss of carbon from steel surface at high temperature, reducing surface hardness and fatigue strength.
Surface Cracking Hot/Cold Working Low material ductility at forming temperature or excessive reduction in a single pass.
Residual Stresses Cold Working Locked-in internal elastic stresses resulting from non-uniform plastic deformation without recrystallization.
Work Hardening Cold Working Dislocation pile-up during deformation below $T_{\text{recrys}}$, increasing strength and hardness while reducing ductility.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP (HOT VS COLD WORKING):
• Cold Working: Associated with Residual Stresses & Work Hardening.
• Hot Working: Associated with Scale Formation & Decarburization.

1.2 Rolling Operations & Mechanics

Two-High Mill: Simple arrangement; both rolls rotate in same direction. Used for reversing mills (back and forth passes).

Three-High Mill: Three horizontal rolls; allows continuous rolling in one direction by moving workpiece between different roll pairs.

Four-High Mill: Two work rolls (small, load-bearing) supported by two backup rolls (large). Reduces deflection, enables thinner gauge rolling.

Figure 1.2A: Schematic of Rolling Mill Arrangements (Two-High, Three-High, Four-High, and Cluster Mills).

Maximum Possible Draft: Determined by roll radius and friction coefficient. Draft cannot exceed $\mu^2 R$ (where $\mu$ is friction, $R$ is work roll radius in mm). Exceeding this limit causes workpiece to slip.

Rolling Formulas

$$\text{Maximum Draft: } \Delta h_{\max} = \mu^2 R$$

Where: $\mu$ = coefficient of friction (contact), $R$ = work roll radius (mm). Exceeding $\mu^2 R$ causes slip. Practical maximum usually 35–40% reduction in single pass.

Figure 1.2B: Geometric Relationship of Roll Bite, Contact Angle $\alpha$, and Maximum Draft $\Delta h_{\max}$.
$$\text{Natural / True Strain: } \varepsilon = \ln\left(\frac{h_0}{h_f}\right)$$

For rolling: Strain relates to draft $\Delta h = h_0 - h_f$. Larger drafts demand lower friction or higher roll temperature.

Rolling Defects & Camber Mechanics

Figure 1.2C: Rolling Defects (Wavy edges, Zipper cracks, Alligatoring).

Root Cause of Rolling Defects: Rolling defects mainly occur due to unequal elongation across the strip width, which creates locked-in residual stresses. The single most critical root cause of non-uniform reduction across strip width is improper roll camber (crown).

1. Insufficient Roll Camber (Straight / Under-Cambered Rolls)

Under roll load, rolls deflect (bend). With insufficient camber, the roll gap is smaller at the edges than in the center. Consequently, edges undergo greater reduction and become longer than the center.

Defect Stress State Root Cause / Mechanism
Wavy Edges Edge Compression Edges elongate more than the center; constrained center puts edges into compression, causing buckling (waving).
Centerline Cracking (Zipper Cracks) Center Tension Center is stretched longitudinally by the longer, elongating edges until central tensile fracture occurs.
Warping Uneven Residual Stresses Non-uniform reduction across strip width creates complex 3D residual stress state.
Memory Rule (Insufficient Camber): Edges longer → Wavy edges; Center in tension → Centerline (zipper) cracks.
2. Over-Cambering (Excessive Roll Crown)

When rolls have excessive initial camber (crown), the roll gap is smaller at the center than at the edges. Consequently, the center undergoes greater reduction and becomes longer than the edges.

Defect Stress State Root Cause / Mechanism
Wavy Center (Center Buckle) Center Compression Center elongates more than the edges; constrained edges put center into axial compression, causing buckling (center wave).
Edge Cracking Edge Tension Edges are stretched longitudinally by the longer, elongating center until edge transverse cracking occurs.
Centerline Splitting Severe Center Deformation Excessive center elongation causes localized necking and longitudinal splitting along centerline.
Memory Rule (Over-Cambering): Center longer → Wavy center; Edges in tension → Edge cracks.

1.3 Forging Operations & Upsetting

Upsetting: Compressive force reduces height, increases cross-sectional area. Used for bolt heads, rivet heads, ring enlargement.

Fullering (Drawing Out): Reduces cross-sectional area, increases length. Concentrates flow in one direction; often used as intermediate operation.

Figure 1.3A: Primary Forging Operations — Upsetting (Area ↑, Length ↓) and Fullering (Area ↓, Length ↑).

Drop Forging (Closed-Die): Workpiece forged between two dies; flash extrudes into flash gutter at parting line. Flash acts as pressure buildup mechanism, forcing metal into die cavity corners. Produces superior grain refinement compared to open-die.

Figure 1.3B: Closed-Die Impression Drop Forging showing Flash & Flash Gutter mechanics.

Coining: Cold closed-die process (no flash relief). High pressure forces metal into smallest die details; produces precise surface impressions (coins, medals, jewelry dies).

Forging High-Yield Defect Matrix

Figure 1.3C: Forging Defects (Unfilled section, Die shift, Cold shut).
Defect Type Most-Asked Root Cause & Mechanism Prevention / Remedy
Cold Shut Improper metal flow; two streams of metal meet but fail to fuse together due to surface oxide film or low temp. Increase die radius; optimize die design; ensure adequate forging temperature.
Laps / Folds Excessive metal flow or sharp die corners causing metal to fold over itself without fusing. Increase fillet and corner radii; proper intermediate blank sizing (fullering/edging).
Cracks Excessive forging reduction per blow or forging at dangerously low temperature (below plastic range). Control blow energy; reheat stock when temp drops below forging range.
Flakes (Internal Hairline Cracks) Hydrogen embrittlement / entrapped hydrogen gas during cooling of large alloy steel forgings. Controlled slow cooling after forging; vacuum degassing during steelmaking.
Underfilling / Unfilled Section Insufficient raw material volume, improper die filling, or premature cooling of metal. Accurate stock weight control; increase forging pressure; optimize flash gutter geometry.
Barreling Interface friction at die-workpiece surfaces restricting lateral flow near contacts while center bulges. Effective die lubrication; heated dies (isothermal forging); lower height-to-diameter ratio ($H/D \le 2$).
Flash (Excessive) Improper stock sizing or undersized flash gutter forcing excess material past parting line. Optimize stock weight and die cavity geometry; trim flash post-forging.
Fiber Misalignment Improper grain flow line orientation relative to working loads due to poor die design or open-die working. Closed-die drop forging; properly sequence fullering and edging passes.
🔥 MOST-ASKED FORGING MCQ:
• Cold Shut: Discontinuity formed when two meeting metal streams fail to fuse due to oxide layer.
• Flakes: Internal hairline cracks caused by Hydrogen embrittlement in heavy forgings.

1.4 Extrusion Processes

Direct (Forward) Extrusion: Ram pushes billet through die; billet and ram move in same direction. Simple tooling; high friction at container wall.

Indirect (Backward) Extrusion: Die mounted on ram; moves toward stationary billet. Lower friction; complex tooling. Produces hollow shapes efficiently.

Figure 1.4A: Direct (Forward) vs Indirect (Backward) Metal Extrusion setups.

Hydrostatic Extrusion: Pressurized liquid surrounds billet, eliminating container-wall friction. Superior surface finish, ability to extrude brittle materials, reduced force.

High-Temperature Lubricant (Steel Extrusion): Conventional oils break down $>1000$°C. Molten glass serves as both thermal insulator and lubricant in Sejournet process.

Extrusion High-Yield Defect Matrix

Figure 1.4B: Extrusion Defects (Center burst / Chevron cracking, Pipe defect).
Defect Type Most-Asked Root Cause & Mechanism Prevention / Remedy
Surface Cracking (Fir-Tree Cracks) Excessive extrusion speed or high billet temperature causing hot shortness (localized surface melting/cracking under high friction shear). Reduce extrusion speed; lower billet temperature; optimize die lubrication.
Center Burst (Chevron Cracking) Hydrostatic tensile stresses along central axis of billet in the deformation zone under low die reduction or high die angle. Increase reduction ratio per pass; decrease die semi-angle; use hydrostatic extrusion.
Pipe Defect (Tailpipe / Funneling) Surface oxides, scale, and container-wall impurities drawn into center of billet toward end of direct extrusion stroke. Leave unextruded dummy block (butt end disc); use dummy block slightly smaller than container diameter.
Internal Cracking Improper die angle, non-uniform material flow, or excessive reduction ratio generating internal shear stresses. Optimize die geometry; preheat billet uniformly.
🔥 MOST-ASKED EXTRUSION MCQ:
• Center Burst (Chevron Cracking): Caused by hydrostatic tensile stresses at the central axis.
• Pipe Defect: Surface oxides and impurities drawn into central core near end of stroke.

1.5 Wire Drawing, Swaging & Thread Rolling

Wire Drawing: Workpiece pulled through converging die. Stress state: axial tension + radial compression. Surface finish and diameter control superior to rolling. Used for fine wires.

Figure 1.5A: Wire Drawing Die profile (Approach angle, Bearing land, & Back relief).

Rotary Swaging: Radial impact hammering reduces diameter or forms tapers on rods and tubes. Rapid, produces high surface quality.

Thread Rolling: Cold forming process for ductile materials. No material removal; threads formed by plastic deformation. Higher fatigue strength than cut threads (no stress concentration from tool marks).

Figure 1.5B: Fiber Grain Flow comparison: Continuous grains in Rolled Threads vs Cut Threads.

1.6 Sheet Metal Shearing: Blanking vs Punching

Blanking: Desired part is the slug removed from sheet. Punch size is smaller than desired blank (clearance subtracted from punch). Die size = blank size (exact).

Punching: Desired part is the remaining sheet with hole. Punch size = hole size (exact). Die size is larger than hole (clearance added to die).

Figure 1.6: Die and Punch Clearance Allocation in Blanking vs Punching operations.

Shear Stress Mode: Both blanking and punching fail in pure shear. Maximum shear stress $\tau_{\max}$ occurs at punch-die interface. Total force is product of perimeter × thickness × shear strength.

Shearing Formulas & Clearance Rules

$$\text{Shearing Force (Circular Profile): } F = \pi \cdot d \cdot t \cdot \tau$$

Where: $d$ = diameter, $t$ = thickness, $\tau$ = shear strength. For polygonal profiles: $F = p \cdot t \cdot \tau$ (perimeter $p$).

$$\text{Blanking Punch Diameter} = D_{\text{blank}} - 2C \quad \text{(Clearance subtracted)}$$ $$\text{Blanking Die Diameter} = D_{\text{blank}} \quad \text{(Exact)}$$
$$\text{Punching Punch Diameter} = D_{\text{hole}} \quad \text{(Exact)}$$ $$\text{Punching Die Diameter} = D_{\text{hole}} + 2C \quad \text{(Clearance added)}$$
$$\text{Total Clearance (per Side): } C = 0.0032 \cdot t \cdot \sqrt{\tau}$$

Where: $t$ = sheet thickness (mm), $\tau$ = shear strength (MPa). Allocate half on punch, half on die (or follow design guidelines).

Blanking vs Punching Comparison Matrix

Feature / Parameter Blanking Operation Punching Operation
Desired Product Slug / Disc removed from sheet Remaining sheet with hole
Punch Dimension $\text{Punch Size} = D_{\text{blank}} - 2C$ (Clearance subtracted) $\text{Punch Size} = D_{\text{hole}}$ (Exact hole size)
Die Dimension $\text{Die Size} = D_{\text{blank}}$ (Exact blank size) $\text{Die Size} = D_{\text{hole}} + 2C$ (Clearance added)

Sheared Edge Quality & Defect Matrix

Edge Zone / Defect Description & Key Characteristic Most-Asked Root Cause & Mechanism
Excessive Burr Sharp, raised metal projection along bottom edge of cut. Excessive punch-die clearance or dull cutting edges causing plastic stretching before fracture.
Rollover Zone Curved depression at top edge of cut surface. Initial elastic/plastic deformation preceding punch penetration into sheet.
Burnished Zone (Cut Band) Smooth, shiny vertical portion of sheared edge. Direct shearing action as punch penetrates sheet prior to crack initiation.
Fracture Zone Rough, matte angled surface below burnished zone. Micro-crack propagation meeting between upper punch edge and lower die edge.

🔥 MOST-ASKED SHEARING MCQ:
• Burr Formation: Directly caused by excessive clearance between punch and die.
• Blanking Rule: Die size is exact ($D_{\text{blank}}$); clearance subtracted from punch.
• Punching Rule: Punch size is exact ($D_{\text{hole}}$); clearance added to die.

1.7 Sheet Metal Bending

Bending Stress Distribution: Outer radius in tension; inner radius in compression. Neutral axis does not coincide with geometric centerline; shifts toward inner surface (factor $k = 0.33 \to 0.5$ depending on $t/R$ ratio).

Springback: Elastic recovery upon load removal. Minimized by bottoming (forcing die), over-bending, or stretch bending (tension applied during bend).

Figure 1.7A: Bending Mechanics showing outer tensile & inner compressive stress with Neutral Axis shift.

Sheet Orientation: Material should be bent perpendicular to rolling/grain direction to prevent edge cracking. Rolling direction creates directional weakness.

Bending Formulas

$$\text{Bend Allowance (Neutral Axis Length): } L_b = \alpha (R + k \cdot t)$$

Where: $\alpha$ = bend angle (radians), $R$ = radius of neutral axis (punch radius $r_p$ + $k \cdot t$), $k$ = position factor ($\approx 0.33 \text{ to } 0.5$, depends on $t/R$ ratio), $t$ = thickness.

$$\text{Flat Pattern Length: } L_{\text{flat}} = L_1 + L_2 + L_b$$

Sum of straight sections plus bend allowance for multi-bend layouts.

Bending Defects & Summary

Figure 1.7B: Bending & Springback (Elastic recovery & edge cracking).
Defect Type Process Root Cause / Condition Prevention / Remedy
Springback Bending Elastic recovery after load removal; depends on $E$, $\sigma_y$, $t/R$ ratio, forming method. Over-bend; use bottoming force (complete die contact); stretch bending; surface conditioning (shot peening).

1.8 Deep Drawing

Deep Drawing: Forms flat blank into 3D cup. Flange region sustains radial tension and circumferential compression. Wrinkling in flange prevented by blank-holder force.

Figure 1.8A: Deep Drawing Tooling setup showing Punch, Die, Blank-Holder Force & Cup Formation.

Deep Drawing Formulas

$$\text{Blank Diameter (Simplified): } D = \sqrt{d^2 + 4dh}$$

Where: $d$ = cup diameter, $h$ = cup height. Assumes small corner radius and neglects thickness change. Accounts for surface area conservation (flat disc → cylindrical cup).

$$\text{Drawing Ratio (Severity Check): } \beta = \frac{D}{d}$$

Limits: First draw $\beta \le 2.0$ to $2.2$ (depending on material). Subsequent draws lower ratio to prevent tearing.

Deep Drawing High-Yield Defect Matrix

Figure 1.8B: Deep Drawing Defects (Wrinkling, Flange tearing, Earing).
Defect Type Most-Asked Root Cause & Mechanism Prevention / Remedy
Wrinkling in Flange Low blank-holder pressure allowing thin flange metal to buckle under compressive hoop stresses. Increase blank-holder force; improve flange lubrication control; use draw beads.
Wrinkling in Wall Improper drawing die clearance, excessive punch corner radius, or unconstrained thin sheet wall. Optimize die and punch corner radii; proper punch-die clearance.
Tearing / Flange Fracture Excessive blank-holder pressure or high drawing ratio ($\beta > 2.0$) exceeding tensile strength of cup wall near base. Reduce blank-holder force; increase die corner radius; perform multi-stage drawing with intermediate annealing.
Earing (Wavy Top Edge) Planar anisotropy ($\Delta R \neq 0$) of sheet metal due to directional preferred orientation of grains from prior rolling. Use isotropic sheet metal ($\Delta R = 0$); align blank relative to rolling direction; trim ears post-drawing.
Surface Scratches / Scoring Poor die lubrication, pickup/galling on punch/die surfaces, or rough die radii. Polish die surfaces; use extreme pressure lubricants; apply die surface coatings (TiN/TD).
Thinning (Neck Down) Excessive tensile stress at cup bottom corner radius during punch penetration. Increase punch/die corner radii; optimize draw ratio.
🔥 MOST-ASKED DEEP DRAWING MCQs:
• Earing: Caused by planar anisotropy ($\Delta R$) of sheet metal.
• Wrinkling in Flange: Caused by insufficient (low) blank-holder pressure.
• Tearing at Cup Base: Caused by excessive blank-holder pressure or high draw ratio.

2. Key Points to Remember (Exam Flashcards)

  • Maximum draft in rolling: $\Delta h_{\max} = \mu^2 R$. Exceeding this causes slip; cannot be overcome by speed alone.
  • Blanking punches are undersized; punching dies are oversized. Clearance $C = 0.0032 \cdot t \cdot \sqrt{\tau}$ allocated between them.
  • Hot working requires temp $> T_{\text{recrys}}$ for continuous recrystallization; temperature classification is material-specific, not universal.
  • Cold working increases strength and hardness through work-hardening but limits deformation magnitude; requires intermediate annealing for large reductions.
  • Forging refines grain structure and aligns flow lines parallel to major stress directions; drop forging (closed-die) produces superior properties vs open-die.
  • Upsetting: Area ↑, Length ↓; Fullering: Area ↓, Length ↑. Both are intermediate forging operations.
  • Extrusion through die lubricated by molten glass (Sejournet process) at high temp; hydrostatic extrusion eliminates container friction.
  • Deep drawing blank diameter: $D = \sqrt{d^2 + 4dh}$. First draw ratio $\beta = D/d \le 2.0\text{–}2.2$ to prevent tearing.
  • Neutral axis in bending shifts inward; factor $k$ ranges 0.33–0.5; bend allowance $L_b = \alpha(R + kt)$ accounts for this shift.
  • Springback is elastic recovery; minimized by bottoming (full die contact), over-bending, or stretch bending under tension.
  • Sheet should be bent perpendicular to rolling direction to prevent edge cracking; rolling imparts directional weakness along flow direction.
  • Wrinkling in deep drawing controlled by blank-holder force; flange sustains compressive hoop stress that can buckle thin material.
  • Shear force in blanking/punching: $F = \pi dt\tau$ or $F = pt\tau$ (perimeter). Shear angle reduces peak force but not total energy.
  • Coining is cold closed-die process (no flash) used for precise surface impressions; requires extremely high pressure.
  • Thread rolling is cold-forming, no material removal; produces higher fatigue strength than cut threads due to absence of stress-concentration notches.

3. APGENCO / SSC JE Manufacturing Revision Notes

3.1 Forging Fundamentals

  • Forging Definition: Metal forming process in which metal is shaped by compressive force. Improves strength because grain flow follows the part shape.
  • Drop Forging: Closed-die process where hammer + die strike work at high velocity. Used for mass production of high-strength components.
  • Open-Die Forging: Simpler process; workpiece compressed between flat dies without full shape enclosure.
  • Fullering (Drawing Out): Cross-section decreases, length increases.
  • Upsetting: Opposite of fullering; cross-section increases, length decreases.
  • Common Forging Defects:
    • Cold Shut: Caused by improper die design / sharp corners; meeting metal streams fail to fuse.
    • Die Shift: Caused by misalignment of upper and lower die halves.
    • Scale Pits: Caused by improper cleaning of stock scale prior to forging.
    • Flakes: Caused by entrapped hydrogen / improper cooling of large forgings.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (FORGING):
• Forging Hammers: Energy-restricted machines.
• Forging Presses: Load-restricted machines.
• Grain Flow: Forged parts have aligned directional fiber flow lines; casting does NOT produce directional fiber flow.

3.2 Rolling Mechanics & Formulae

  • Deformation Mode: Rolling relies on compression + shear (compression from rolls, shear from friction at contact interface).
  • Bite Angle ($\alpha$): Increases as draft $\Delta h$ increases.
  • Maximum Possible Draft: $(\Delta h)_{\max} = \mu^2 R$ (depends on roll radius $R$ and friction $\mu$).
  • Separating Force: Smaller roll diameter reduces projected contact length, thereby decreasing roll separating force.
  • Backup Rolls: Used in 4-high and cluster mills to support slender work rolls and minimize roll bending/deflection under load.
  • Wavy Edges: Associated with material anisotropy and uneven roll gap deformation.
  • Rolling Force Relation: $\text{Force} \propto \text{Stress} \times \text{Projected Area}$. Projected contact length increases with roll radius.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (ROLLING):
• Greater draft $\rightarrow$ Greater bite angle.
• Greater roll radius $\rightarrow$ Greater maximum reduction capability.
• Backup rolls $\rightarrow$ Support against bending deflection (not a direct forming force component).

3.3 Sheet Metal Bending

  • Bend Allowance ($BA$): Length of neutral axis in bend region: $$\text{BA} = \theta (R + kt)$$ If neutral axis lies at $0.5t$ from inside surface, then $k = 0.5$.
  • Springback: Elastic recovery upon load removal caused by the release of stored elastic energy (plastic deformation remains permanent).
  • V-Die Bending Force: $$F = \frac{K L t^2 \sigma_u}{w}$$ (where $L$ = bend length, $t$ = sheet thickness, $\sigma_u$ = ultimate tensile strength, $w$ = die opening width).
⚡ QUICK TRAP (BENDING):
• Springback = Elastic Recovery (NOT plastic strain).
• Greater yield strength ($\sigma_y$) $\rightarrow$ Greater springback.

3.4 Deep Drawing

  • Converts flat blank into 3D cup shape by pushing blank into die cavity.
  • Blank Diameter ($D$): Found by surface area conservation: $D = \sqrt{d^2 + 4dh}$.
  • Wrinkling: Most common defect; caused by compressive hoop stress in flange.
  • Blank Holding Force: Applied to suppress flange wrinkling; excessive force leads to tearing near cup base.
  • Wall thickness may undergo slight variation during drawing due to metal flow.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (DEEP DRAWING):
• Wrinkling = Low blank holding force / Compressive hoop stress.
• Tearing = Excessive tensile stress / Excessive blank holding force.

3.5 Press Work, Cutting Operations & Tool Sequencing

  • Blanking: Desired product is the blank (slug removed); clearance provided on punch ($\text{Punch} = \text{Die} - 2C$).
  • Punching / Piercing: Desired feature is the hole; punch size equals exact hole size ($\text{Die} = \text{Punch} + 2C$).
  • Trimming: Press work operation to remove excess flash or flange material post-forming.
  • Countersinking: Performed after drilling to create a conical seat for screw heads.
  • Tapping: Final operation in hole-making sequence; cuts internal threads after hole is prepared.
$$\text{Standard Hole-Making Sequence: } \text{Drilling} \longrightarrow \text{Boring} \longrightarrow \text{Countersinking} \longrightarrow \text{Tapping}$$
⚡ QUICK TRAP (HOLE SEQUENCE):
• Drilling = Initial hole creation | Boring = Size & accuracy improvement.
• Countersinking = Conical seat | Tapping = Internal thread cutting.

3.6 Die Clearance & Press Tool Relief

  • Angular Clearance on Dies: Usually $0.5^\circ \text{ to } 1^\circ$.
  • Function: Serves as a relief angle on die wall to prevent slug jamming, avoid rubbing, and reduce tool wear while maintaining sharp edge support.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (CLEARANCE):
• Linear clearance = Per-side gap between punch and die boundary.
• Angular clearance = Tapered relief angle on die cavity wall.

3.7 Extrusion & High Energy Rate Forming (HERF)

  • Indirect Extrusion: Provides better surface finish and lower force because billet and container have no relative motion (eliminating wall friction).
  • Forward Hot Extrusion: Uses molten glass (Sejournet process) as lubricant and insulator for steel.
  • Collapsible Tubes: Manufactured by impact extrusion (cold backward extrusion).
  • Spur Gears: Can be produced by extrusion due to uniform axial cross-section.
  • High-Velocity Forming Threshold: Forming speeds above $15\text{ m/s}$.
  • Explosive Forming: High Energy Rate Forming (HERF) process best suited for large, complex, low-volume components (aerospace panels, domes, wing skins, nose cones; high-strength or difficult materials).
⚡ QUICK TRAP (HERF & EXTRUSION):
• Indirect Extrusion = Better surface finish + Lower punch force.
• Impact Extrusion = Collapsible tubes (toothpastes, aluminum cans).
• Explosive Forming = Large complex parts, small batch size.

3.8 Tube & Seamless Product Manufacturing

  • Seamless Tube: Produced by piercing (Mannesmann process); billet is converted into a hollow shell over a mandrel.
  • Plug Rolling: Used post-piercing to reduce wall thickness and increase tube diameter.
  • Rotary Swaging: Reduces cross-sectional area of rods/tubes by rapid radial impact hammering (an impact process, not steady pressure).
⚡ QUICK TRAP (TUBE MFG):
• Piercing $\rightarrow$ Seamless tube hollow shell.
• Plug rolling $\rightarrow$ Wall thinning + Diameter expansion.
• Rotary swaging $\rightarrow$ Section reduction via radial impacts.

3.9 Wire Drawing, Thread Rolling & Surface Fatigue Improvement

  • Ideal Wire Drawing Limit: Maximum theoretical reduction in area is $63.2\%$: $$r_{\max} = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$$
  • Thread Rolling: Cold-forming process restricted to ductile materials (chipless, plastic flow). Produces higher fatigue strength than cut threads due to continuous grain flow and absence of tool marks.
  • Shot Peening: Introduces compressive residual stress on surface, significantly improving fatigue life and prolonging tool life post-grinding.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (WIRE & THREADING):
• Thread rolling = Ductile materials only.
• Shot peening = Compressive residual stress + Fatigue life improvement.
• Ideal wire drawing maximum area reduction limit $\approx 63\%$.

3.10 Machining, Finishing & Drill Geometry

  • Honing: High-precision surface finishing process for internal cylindrical surfaces (bores).
  • Broaching: High-accuracy, high-production finishing process; accuracy typically around $10\ \mu\text{m}$.
  • Flat Drill: Considered a zero-rake cutting tool.
  • Twist Drill Rake Angle: Rake angle increases from center to periphery (zero/negative at chisel edge/center, maximum at outer lip).
⚡ QUICK TRAP (TOOL GEOMETRY):
• Honing $\rightarrow$ Bore finishing.
• Broaching $\rightarrow$ High accuracy ($10\ \mu\text{m}$).
• Twist drill rake angle $\rightarrow$ Increases outward from center.

3.11 Gear Manufacturing Methods

  • Gear Forming: Preferred for small batches of gears with low accuracy requirement.
  • Hobbing & Shaping: Generation methods used for high productivity and accuracy.
  • Broaching Gears: Expensive tooling, used for high production rate and good accuracy.
  • Spur Gear Extrusion: Feasible due to constant axial profile.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (GEARS):
• Small batch + Low accuracy $\rightarrow$ Gear forming.
• High productivity + Accuracy $\rightarrow$ Gear hobbing.
• Internal gear cutting $\rightarrow$ Gear shaping / broaching.

3.12 Powder Metallurgy Secondary Operations

  • Sizing: Cold pressing post-sintering to improve dimensional accuracy.
  • Coining: Cold pressing to press fine surface details and increase density.
  • Impregnation: Pores of sintered part are filled with oil (self-lubricating bearings).
  • Infiltration: Pores are filled with a lower-melting-point molten metal.
  • Heat treatment, plating, and painting are common post-sintering finishing steps.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (POWDER METALLURGY):
• Impregnation = Oil filling.
• Infiltration = Molten metal filling.
• Sizing = Dimensional accuracy.
• Coining = Surface details & density.

3.13 Joining, Soldering & Surface Bonding

  • Rubber-to-Metal Bonding: Accomplished by adhesive bonding.
  • Sweating: Process of tinning surfaces, applying flux, and heating so solder flows between joint surfaces by capillary action (soldering-based joining, NOT welding).
⚡ QUICK TRAP (JOINING):
• Sweating = Tinning + Flux + Heating (Capillarity).
• Rubber sheet to metal = Adhesive bonding.

3.14 Material Behaviour, Room-Temp Exceptions & Special Forming

  • Lead and Tin: Can be hot worked at room temperature because their recrystallization temperature is below room temperature ($T_{\text{recrys}} < 20^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Coining, bending, and rolling are all based on plastic deformation.
  • Structural Sections: Rails, angles, channels, and I-beams are produced by hot rolling.
  • Steel Balls: Manufactured by cold heading.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (SPECIAL CASES):
• Lead and tin = Room temp hot working exception.
• Steel bearing balls = Cold heading.
• Structural sections (rails, I-beams) = Hot rolling.

3.15 Common Process Classification Matrix

  • Coining: Classified as a cold forging operation.
  • Trimming: Belongs to press work.
  • Fullering: Classified as a forging operation.
  • Peen Forming: Used for forming shallow contours and straightening thin sheet parts.
  • Shaper Tool Life: Improved after grinding by shot peening.
⚡ QUICK TRAP (CLASSIFICATION):
• Forging = Fullering, Upsetting, Coining.
• Press Work = Trimming, Blanking, Piercing, Punching, Countersinking.
• Peen Forming = Shallow contours + Straightening.
