Welding, Brazing, Soldering & Joining Processes

1. Topic Overview

This guide covers the physics of welding (arc characteristics, power source linear V-I relation, oxy-acetylene flame chemistry, heat input), major welding process principles (arc, gas, resistance, thermit, solid-state), and allied joining processes (brazing, soldering, adhesive bonding). High-frequency PYQ themes include power-source maximum-power calculations, flame temperature and gas ratios, electrode polarity, filler metal composition, resistance welding parameters, and common weld defects. All formulas, definitions, diagrams, and worked examples are consolidated below.

2. Quick Reference Sheet

2.1 All Formulas

Maximum Arc Power Current (Linear V-I Source)

$$\boxed{I_{\text{max}} = \frac{I_{sc}}{2}}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
$I_{\text{max}}$ current for maximum arc power A
$I_{sc}$ short-circuit current of power source A

Linear V-I Power Source Characteristic

$$\boxed{V = V_{oc} - \left$\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right$ I}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
$V$ arc operating voltage V
$V_{oc}$ open-circuit voltage V
$I_{sc}$ short-circuit current A
$I$ arc operating current A

Heat Generated in Resistance Welding

$$\boxed{H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
$H$ heat generated J
$I$ welding current A
$R$ electrical resistance across joint $\Omega$
$t$ current flow duration s
$k$ heat efficiency / loss factor dimensionless

Heat Input per Unit Length

$$\boxed{H_{\text{input}} = \frac{V \cdot I}{v}}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
$H_{\text{input}}$ heat energy supplied per unit length J/mm
$V$ arc voltage V
$I$ arc current A
$v$ welding travel speed mm/s

Actual Heat Input

$$\boxed{H_{\text{actual}} = \eta_{\text{heat}} \cdot \left$\frac{V \cdot I}{v}\right$}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
$\eta_{\text{heat}}$ heat-transfer efficiency dimensionless

Melting Efficiency

$$\boxed{\eta_{\text{melting}} = \frac{H_{\text{required}}}{H_{\text{actual}}}}$$

Symbol Meaning Unit
$\eta_{\text{melting}}$ melting efficiency dimensionless
$H_{\text{required}}$ heat required to melt joint area J/mm

2.2 Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Fusion Welding: Joining processes where base metals are melted at the joint interface (e.g., SMAW, TIG, MIG, SAW, Gas, Resistance).
  • Solid-State Welding: Joining processes where coalescing is produced at temperatures below base metal melting point without bulk liquid phase (e.g., Friction, Forge, Ultrasonic, Explosive).
  • Straight Polarity (DCEN): Direct current arc welding setup where electrode is connected to negative terminal and workpiece to positive terminal.
  • Reverse Polarity (DCEP): Direct current arc welding setup where electrode is connected to positive terminal and workpiece to negative terminal.
  • Arc Blow: Deflection of electric arc from its intended path caused by magnetic fields generated by welding current (more severe in DC).
  • Brazing: Allied joining process where filler metal melts above 450 °C (or 430 °C) but below base-metal solidus; joint filled by capillary action without melting base metals.
  • Soldering: Allied joining process where soft filler metal (tin-lead alloy) melts below 450 °C (typically 180–250 °C); joint filled by capillary action without melting base metals.
  • Thermit Mixture: Chemical reactant mixture of aluminium powder and iron oxide $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ in a 1 : 3 mass ratio used for heavy casting repair and rail joining.
  • Undercut: Weld defect consisting of a groove melted into base metal adjacent to toe or root of weld, caused primarily by excessive welding current.
  • Weld Spatter: Weld defect consisting of metal droplets expelled from arc or weld pool that adhere to base metal surface.

2.3 Standard Values & Constants

  • Oxy-acetylene flame maximum temperature: $\approx 3200^\circ\text{C}$ (located at inner cone tip).
  • Electric arc temperature range: $6000\text{--}7000^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Neutral flame ratio $\text{O}_2 : \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$: $\approx 1 : 1$ (Complete combustion requires total $\approx 2.5$ volumes $\text{O}_2$ per volume $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$).
  • Thermit mixture ratio $\text{Al} : \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$: $1 : 3$.
  • Brazing vs Soldering threshold: $450^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Soldering temperature range: $180\text{--}250^\circ\text{C}$ (tin-lead solder).
  • Resistance welding voltage: Low voltage $1\text{--}10\text{ V}$, extremely high current (thousands of Amperes).
  • Gas cylinder colors: Oxygen cylinder = Black; Acetylene cylinder = Maroon.

3.1 Arc Welding Power-Source Characteristics

1. Concept Pointers
  • A DC welding power source with linear V-I characteristic has open-circuit voltage $V_{oc}$ and short-circuit current $I_{sc}$.
  • Arc power $P = V \cdot I$; substituting linear V-I relation yields a parabolic power curve.
  • Differentiating arc power with respect to current shows that maximum arc power occurs at operating current $I = I_{sc} / 2$.
  • At maximum power, the operating voltage is also equal to $V_{oc} / 2$.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{I_{\text{max}} = \frac{I_{sc}}{2}}$$

$$\boxed{P_{\text{max}} = \frac{V_{oc} \cdot I_{sc}}{4}}$$

3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic for linear V-I characteristic and parabolic power curve.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: A DC welding power source has a linear voltage-current (V-I) characteristic with an open-circuit voltage of 80 V and a short-circuit current of 300 A.
Find: Current setting for maximum arc power.

Solution:
1. For a linear power-source characteristic $V = V_{oc} - \left(\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right)I$.
2. Power $P = V \cdot I = V_{oc} I - \left(\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right)I^2$.
3. To maximize power, set $\frac{dP}{dI} = V_{oc} - 2\left(\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right)I = 0 \implies I = \frac{I_{sc}}{2}$.
4. $I = \frac{300}{2} = 150\text{ A}$.
5. Answer: 150 A.

Key Insight: For any linear V-I power source, maximum arc power always occurs at exactly half the short-circuit current.

3.2 Oxy-Acetylene Gas Welding and Flame Types

1. Concept Pointers
  • Flame temperature reaches $\approx 3200^\circ\text{C}$, with maximum temperature located at the inner cone tip.
  • Neutral flame $\text{O}_2 : \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 \approx 1 : 1$ is used for general steel, cast iron, copper, and aluminium welding.
  • Carburizing / Reducing flame ($\text{O}_2 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$, ratio $\approx 0.9 : 1$) contains excess acetylene and is used for high-carbon steels, hard-facing, and alloy steels.
  • Oxidizing flame ($\text{O}_2 > \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$, ratio $\approx 1.5 : 1$) contains excess oxygen and is used for brass and bronze welding.
  • Oxygen cylinder is painted black; acetylene cylinder is painted maroon.
  • Complete combustion of 1 volume of acetylene requires 2.5 volumes of oxygen in total.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1 & 2.3.

$$\boxed{\text{Neutral: } \text{O}_2 : \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 = 1 : 1 \quad | \quad \text{Carburizing: } \text{O}_2 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 \quad | \quad \text{Oxidizing: } \text{O}_2 > \text{C}_2\text{H}_2}$$

3. Diagram


Reducing (carburizing), neutral, and oxidizing oxy-acetylene flames showing cone structures. Source: Wikimedia Commons.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: In oxy-acetylene gas welding, the proportion of oxygen supplied is less than the proportion of acetylene.
Find: Type of flame produced.

Solution: 1. When oxygen proportion is less than acetylene, acetylene is in excess. 2. Excess acetylene produces an unburnt carbon envelope, making it a carburizing (reducing) flame. 3. Answer: Carburizing flame.

Key Insight: Oxygen excess = Oxidizing; Acetylene excess = Carburizing; 1:1 equal volume = Neutral.

3.3 Arc Welding Processes, Electrodes and Polarity

1. Concept Pointers
  • Arc temperature is of the order of $6000\text{--}7000^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Straight Polarity (DCEN): Electrode is negative, workpiece is positive. Heat distribution: $\approx 67\%$ at workpiece, $\approx 33\%$ at electrode.
  • Reverse Polarity (DCEP): Electrode is positive, workpiece is negative. Preferred for thin sheets and overhead welding.
  • Arc Blow is the magnetic deflection of arc stream from intended path; occurs primarily in DC welding.
  • TIG (GTAW): Uses non-consumable tungsten electrode and inert shielding gas (Argon/Helium).
  • MIG (GMAW): Uses consumable wire electrode fed continuously; metal transfers as fine spray.
  • SMAW: Electrode coating acts as flux and decomposes to generate protective shielding gas.
  • SAW (Submerged Arc Welding): Arc is submerged beneath a granular fusible flux blanket using bare metal electrode.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.2.

$$\boxed{\text{Straight Polarity (DCEN): Electrode } (-) \quad | \quad \text{Reverse Polarity (DCEP): Electrode } (+)}$$

3. Diagram

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: An arc welding operation uses direct current with reverse polarity.
Find: Connection of the electrode holder.

Solution: 1. Reverse polarity is DCEP (Direct Current Electrode Positive). 2. The electrode is connected to the positive terminal of the power source. 3. Answer: Positive terminal.

Key Insight: Straight = Electrode Negative (DCEN); Reverse = Electrode Positive (DCEP).

3.4 Resistance Welding

1. Concept Pointers
  • Resistance welding operates at low voltage $1\text{--}10\text{ V}$ and extremely high current for short time durations.
  • Heat generated follows Joule's Law: $H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t$.
  • Current is regulated by changing primary turns of the step-down welding transformer.
  • Resistance seam welding produces continuous leak-proof joints using rotating disc electrodes without filler metal.
  • Resistance spot welding is a fusion process (produces a molten nugget), NOT a solid-state process.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t}$$

3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic for resistance spot and seam welding setups.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Current $I$ flows for time $t$ across joint resistance $R$ in a spot welding machine.
Find: Expression for heat generated.

Solution: 1. Heat generated in electrical resistance heating is proportional to $I^2 R t$. 2. Accounting for thermal efficiency/loss factor $k$, $H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t$. 3. Answer: $H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t$.

Key Insight: Heat generated varies quadratically with current $I^2$, so small changes in current have the largest effect on heat input.

3.5 Thermit, Explosive and Solid-State Welding Processes

1. Concept Pointers
  • Thermit mixture consists of aluminium powder and iron oxide $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ in a 1 : 3 mass ratio.
  • Thermit reaction is an exothermic chemical reaction: $8\text{Al} + 3\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 \rightarrow 9\text{Fe} + 4\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + \text{Heat}$. Used for rail joining and heavy casting repairs.
  • Solid-state welding processes join materials without bulk melting of base metals: Friction, Forge, Ultrasonic, Explosive.
  • Explosive welding uses a flyer plate driven by high explosives onto a base plate to form a metallurgical bond.
  • Resistance spot welding is NOT solid-state because it melts a liquid nugget.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.3.

$$\boxed{\text{Thermit Ratio } $\text{Al} : \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ = 1 : 3}$$

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Thermit welding process mixture components and their proportion.
Find: Correct metal powder and ratio with iron oxide.

Solution: 1. Thermit is a mixture of aluminium powder and iron oxide. 2. The standard stoichiometric mass ratio is 1 part Aluminium to 3 parts Iron Oxide. 3. Answer: Aluminium and iron oxide in 1 : 3 ratio.

Key Insight: Aluminium acts as the reducing agent because of its high affinity for oxygen.

3.6 Brazing, Soldering and Adhesive Bonding

1. Concept Pointers
  • Brazing filler metal has a melting temperature above 450 °C (or 430 °C) but below the solidus temperature of base metals.
  • Brazing filler alloys: Copper-zinc (spelter), copper-silver; flux used is borax or boric acid.
  • Soldering filler metal (soft solder) is an alloy of tin and lead; melting point is below 450 °C (typically 180–250 °C).
  • In both brazing and soldering, liquid filler metal fills joint clearance purely by capillary action; base metals DO NOT melt.
  • Brazing does NOT cause metallurgical damage by phase transformation to base metals.
  • Adhesive bonding uses non-metallic adhesives (epoxy, cyanoacrylate) that cure without melting substrates.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.3.

$$\boxed{\text{Brazing: Filler Melting Point} > 450^\circ\text{C} \quad | \quad \text{Soldering: Filler Melting Point} < 450^\circ\text{C}}$$

3. Diagram

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Principal constituent materials used in soft soldering.
Find: Correct alloy combination.

Solution: 1. Soft solder is traditionally an alloy of tin (Sn) and lead (Pb). 2. Eutectic solder (63% Sn - 37% Pb) melts sharply at 183 °C. 3. Answer: Tin and lead.

Key Insight: Solder filler melts below 450 °C, while brazing spelter melts above 450 °C.

3.7 Welding Defects, Physics & Heat Input

1. Concept Pointers
  • Inadequate penetration (lack of penetration) forms sharp root stress raisers, leading to crack formation.
  • Undercut is caused by excessive welding current or incorrect electrode angle.
  • Weld spatter is a defect consisting of expelled droplets adhering to workpiece surface.
  • Porosity is caused by trapped gas pockets (mainly hydrogen dissolved in molten pool).
  • Residual stresses in fusion weld zone after cooling are predominantly tensile in weld bead and compressive in adjacent base metal regions.
  • Low-carbon steel $\approx 0.15\%\text{ C}$ exhibits the highest weldability among carbon steels.
  • Aluminium is difficult to weld due to its high thermal conductivity and rapid formation of a tenacious oxide film $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{H_{\text{input}} = \frac{V \cdot I}{v} \quad | \quad H_{\text{actual}} = \eta_{\text{heat}} \cdot \left$\frac{V \cdot I}{v}\right$ \quad | \quad \eta_{\text{melting}} = \frac{H_{\text{required}}}{H_{\text{actual}}}}$$

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Arc welding of steel with voltage 20 V, current 200 A, travel speed 5 mm/s, heat-transfer efficiency 0.8, joint area $20\text{ mm}^2$, heat of melting $10\text{ J/mm}^3$.
Find: Melting efficiency.

Solution: 1. Heat supplied per unit length = $\frac{V \cdot I}{v} = \frac{20 \times 200}{5} = 800\text{ J/mm}$. 2. Actual heat input = $\eta_{\text{heat}} \times 800 = 0.8 \times 800 = 640\text{ J/mm}$. 3. Heat required for melting = Area $\times$ heat of melting = $20 \times 10 = 200\text{ J/mm}$. 4. Melting efficiency $\eta_{\text{melting}} = \frac{H_{\text{required}}}{H_{\text{actual}}} = \frac{200}{640} = 0.3125 \implies 31.25\%$. 5. Answer: 31.25%.

Key Insight: Distinguish heat-transfer efficiency (arc-to-workpiece) from melting efficiency (heat-to-melt vs actual heat input).

4. Consolidation Notes

Note on Resistance Welding vs Solid-State Welding: Resistance spot/seam welding relies on electrical resistance to melt a liquid nugget, so it is a fusion process. In contrast, friction, ultrasonic, forge, and explosive welding do not melt the base metal and are true solid-state processes. See Section 3.4 & 3.5.

Note on Brazing vs Soldering Threshold: Both processes rely on capillary flow without melting base metals. The sole distinguishing criterion tested in MCQs is filler melting temperature: below 450 °C is soldering (tin-lead), above 450 °C is brazing (copper-zinc/silver). See Section 3.6.

Note on Power Source Maximum Power: For linear V-I characteristics, maximum power is obtained at $I_{sc}/2$ and $V_{oc}/2$. Do not confuse short-circuit current with operating current for maximum power output. See Section 3.1.

5. Final Quick-Fire Checklist

  • Memorize linear V-I maximum power current: $I_{\text{max}} = I_{sc} / 2$.
  • Know flame gas ratios: Neutral $1:1$, Carburizing $\text{O}_2 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$, Oxidizing $\text{O}_2 > \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$.
  • Memorize flame temperatures: Oxy-acetylene $\approx 3200^\circ\text{C}$, Electric Arc $6000\text{--}7000^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Remember gas cylinder colors: Oxygen = Black, Acetylene = Maroon.
  • Memorize polarity: Straight (DCEN) = Electrode Negative; Reverse (DCEP) = Electrode Positive.
  • Remember Thermit ratio: Aluminium : Iron Oxide = $1 : 3$.
  • Fix joining temperature boundary: Soldering $< 450^\circ\text{C}$ (Tin-Lead), Brazing $> 450^\circ\text{C}$ (Copper-Zinc).
  • Know primary defect causes: Undercut $\leftarrow$ Excessive current; Cracks $\leftarrow$ Lack of penetration; Porosity $\leftarrow$ Hydrogen.
