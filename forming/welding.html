Welding, Brazing, Soldering & Joining Processes

1. Topic Overview

This guide covers the physics of welding (arc characteristics, power source linear V-I relation, oxy-acetylene flame chemistry, heat input), major welding process principles (arc, gas, resistance, thermit, solid-state), and allied joining processes (brazing, soldering, adhesive bonding). High-frequency PYQ themes include power-source maximum-power calculations, flame temperature and gas ratios, electrode polarity, filler metal composition, resistance welding parameters, and common weld defects. All formulas, definitions, diagrams, and worked examples are consolidated below.

2.2 Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Fusion Welding: Joining processes where base metals are melted at the joint interface (e.g., SMAW, TIG, MIG, SAW, Gas, Resistance).
  • Solid-State Welding: Joining processes where coalescing is produced at temperatures below base metal melting point without bulk liquid phase (e.g., Friction, Forge, Ultrasonic, Explosive).
  • Straight Polarity (DCEN): Direct current arc welding setup where electrode is connected to negative terminal and workpiece to positive terminal.
  • Reverse Polarity (DCEP): Direct current arc welding setup where electrode is connected to positive terminal and workpiece to negative terminal.
  • Arc Blow: Deflection of electric arc from its intended path caused by magnetic fields generated by welding current (more severe in DC).
  • Brazing: Allied joining process where filler metal melts above 450 °C (or 430 °C) but below base-metal solidus; joint filled by capillary action without melting base metals.
  • Soldering: Allied joining process where soft filler metal (tin-lead alloy) melts below 450 °C (typically 180–250 °C); joint filled by capillary action without melting base metals.
  • Thermit Mixture: Chemical reactant mixture of aluminium powder and iron oxide $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ in a 1 : 3 mass ratio used for heavy casting repair and rail joining.
  • Undercut: Weld defect consisting of a groove melted into base metal adjacent to toe or root of weld, caused primarily by excessive welding current.
  • Weld Spatter: Weld defect consisting of metal droplets expelled from arc or weld pool that adhere to base metal surface.

2.3 Standard Values & Constants

  • Oxy-acetylene flame maximum temperature: $\approx 3200^\circ\text{C}$ (located at inner cone tip).
  • Electric arc temperature range: $6000\text{--}7000^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Neutral flame ratio $\text{O}_2 : \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$: $\approx 1 : 1$ (Complete combustion requires total $\approx 2.5$ volumes $\text{O}_2$ per volume $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$).
  • Thermit mixture ratio $\text{Al} : \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$: $1 : 3$.
  • Brazing vs Soldering threshold: $450^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Soldering temperature range: $180\text{--}250^\circ\text{C}$ (tin-lead solder).
  • Resistance welding voltage: Low voltage $1\text{--}10\text{ V}$, extremely high current (thousands of Amperes).
  • Gas cylinder colors: Oxygen cylinder = Black; Acetylene cylinder = Maroon.

3. Main Body

3.1 Arc Welding Power-Source Characteristics

1. Concept Pointers
  • A DC welding power source with linear V-I characteristic has open-circuit voltage $V_{oc}$ and short-circuit current $I_{sc}$.
  • Arc power $P = V \cdot I$; substituting linear V-I relation yields a parabolic power curve.
  • Differentiating arc power with respect to current shows that maximum arc power occurs at operating current $I = I_{sc} / 2$.
  • At maximum power, the operating voltage is also equal to $V_{oc} / 2$.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{I_{\text{max}} = \frac{I_{sc}}{2}}$$

$$\boxed{P_{\text{max}} = \frac{V_{oc} \cdot I_{sc}}{4}}$$

3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic for linear V-I characteristic and parabolic power curve.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: A DC welding power source has a linear voltage-current (V-I) characteristic with an open-circuit voltage of 80 V and a short-circuit current of 300 A.
Find: Current setting for maximum arc power.

Solution:
1. For a linear power-source characteristic $V = V_{oc} - \left(\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right)I$.
2. Power $P = V \cdot I = V_{oc} I - \left(\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right)I^2$.
3. To maximize power, set $\frac{dP}{dI} = V_{oc} - 2\left(\frac{V_{oc}}{I_{sc}}\right)I = 0 \implies I = \frac{I_{sc}}{2}$.
4. $I = \frac{300}{2} = 150\text{ A}$.
5. Answer: 150 A.

Key Insight: For any linear V-I power source, maximum arc power always occurs at exactly half the short-circuit current.

3.2 Oxy-Acetylene Gas Welding and Flame Types

1. Concept Pointers
  • Flame temperature reaches $\approx 3200^\circ\text{C}$, with maximum temperature located at the inner cone tip.
  • Neutral flame $\text{O}_2 : \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 \approx 1 : 1$ is used for general steel, cast iron, copper, and aluminium welding.
  • Carburizing / Reducing flame ($\text{O}_2 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$, ratio $\approx 0.9 : 1$) contains excess acetylene and is used for high-carbon steels, hard-facing, and alloy steels.
  • Oxidizing flame ($\text{O}_2 > \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$, ratio $\approx 1.5 : 1$) contains excess oxygen and is used for brass and bronze welding.
  • Oxygen cylinder is painted black; acetylene cylinder is painted maroon.
  • Complete combustion of 1 volume of acetylene requires 2.5 volumes of oxygen in total.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1 & 2.3.

$$\boxed{\text{Neutral: } \text{O}_2 : \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 = 1 : 1 \quad | \quad \text{Carburizing: } \text{O}_2 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 \quad | \quad \text{Oxidizing: } \text{O}_2 > \text{C}_2\text{H}_2}$$

3. Diagram


Reducing (carburizing), neutral, and oxidizing oxy-acetylene flames showing cone structures. Source: Wikimedia Commons.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: In oxy-acetylene gas welding, the proportion of oxygen supplied is less than the proportion of acetylene.
Find: Type of flame produced.

Solution: 1. When oxygen proportion is less than acetylene, acetylene is in excess. 2. Excess acetylene produces an unburnt carbon envelope, making it a carburizing (reducing) flame. 3. Answer: Carburizing flame.

Key Insight: Oxygen excess = Oxidizing; Acetylene excess = Carburizing; 1:1 equal volume = Neutral.

3.3 Arc Welding Processes, Electrodes and Polarity

1. Concept Pointers
  • Arc temperature is of the order of $6000\text{--}7000^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Straight Polarity (DCEN): Electrode is negative, workpiece is positive. Heat distribution: $\approx 67\%$ at workpiece, $\approx 33\%$ at electrode.
  • Reverse Polarity (DCEP): Electrode is positive, workpiece is negative. Preferred for thin sheets and overhead welding.
  • Arc Blow is the magnetic deflection of arc stream from intended path; occurs primarily in DC welding.
  • TIG (GTAW): Uses non-consumable tungsten electrode and inert shielding gas (Argon/Helium).
  • MIG (GMAW): Uses consumable wire electrode fed continuously; metal transfers as fine spray.
  • SMAW: Electrode coating acts as flux and decomposes to generate protective shielding gas.
  • SAW (Submerged Arc Welding): Arc is submerged beneath a granular fusible flux blanket using bare metal electrode.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.2.

$$\boxed{\text{Straight Polarity (DCEN): Electrode } (-) \quad | \quad \text{Reverse Polarity (DCEP): Electrode } (+)}$$

3. Diagram

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: An arc welding operation uses direct current with reverse polarity.
Find: Connection of the electrode holder.

Solution: 1. Reverse polarity is DCEP (Direct Current Electrode Positive). 2. The electrode is connected to the positive terminal of the power source. 3. Answer: Positive terminal.

Key Insight: Straight = Electrode Negative (DCEN); Reverse = Electrode Positive (DCEP).

3.4 Resistance Welding

1. Concept Pointers
  • Resistance welding operates at low voltage $1\text{--}10\text{ V}$ and extremely high current for short time durations.
  • Heat generated follows Joule's Law: $H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t$.
  • Current is regulated by changing primary turns of the step-down welding transformer.
  • Resistance seam welding produces continuous leak-proof joints using rotating disc electrodes without filler metal.
  • Resistance spot welding is a fusion process (produces a molten nugget), NOT a solid-state process.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t}$$

3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic for resistance spot and seam welding setups.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Current $I$ flows for time $t$ across joint resistance $R$ in a spot welding machine.
Find: Expression for heat generated.

Solution: 1. Heat generated in electrical resistance heating is proportional to $I^2 R t$. 2. Accounting for thermal efficiency/loss factor $k$, $H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t$. 3. Answer: $H = k \cdot I^2 \cdot R \cdot t$.

Key Insight: Heat generated varies quadratically with current $I^2$, so small changes in current have the largest effect on heat input.

3.5 Thermit, Explosive and Solid-State Welding Processes

1. Concept Pointers
  • Thermit mixture consists of aluminium powder and iron oxide $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ in a 1 : 3 mass ratio.
  • Thermit reaction is an exothermic chemical reaction: $8\text{Al} + 3\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 \rightarrow 9\text{Fe} + 4\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + \text{Heat}$. Used for rail joining and heavy casting repairs.
  • Solid-state welding processes join materials without bulk melting of base metals: Friction, Forge, Ultrasonic, Explosive.
  • Explosive welding uses a flyer plate driven by high explosives onto a base plate to form a metallurgical bond.
  • Resistance spot welding is NOT solid-state because it melts a liquid nugget.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.3.

$$\boxed{\text{Thermit Ratio } $\text{Al} : \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ = 1 : 3}$$

3. Diagram

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Thermit welding process mixture components and their proportion.
Find: Correct metal powder and ratio with iron oxide.

Solution: 1. Thermit is a mixture of aluminium powder and iron oxide. 2. The standard stoichiometric mass ratio is 1 part Aluminium to 3 parts Iron Oxide. 3. Answer: Aluminium and iron oxide in 1 : 3 ratio.

Key Insight: Aluminium acts as the reducing agent because of its high affinity for oxygen.

3.6 Brazing, Soldering and Adhesive Bonding

1. Concept Pointers
  • Brazing filler metal has a melting temperature above 450 °C (or 430 °C) but below the solidus temperature of base metals.
  • Brazing filler alloys: Copper-zinc (spelter), copper-silver; flux used is borax or boric acid.
  • Soldering filler metal (soft solder) is an alloy of tin and lead; melting point is below 450 °C (typically 180–250 °C).
  • In both brazing and soldering, liquid filler metal fills joint clearance purely by capillary action; base metals DO NOT melt.
  • Brazing does NOT cause metallurgical damage by phase transformation to base metals.
  • Adhesive bonding uses non-metallic adhesives (epoxy, cyanoacrylate) that cure without melting substrates.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.3.

$$\boxed{\text{Brazing: Filler Melting Point} > 450^\circ\text{C} \quad | \quad \text{Soldering: Filler Melting Point} < 450^\circ\text{C}}$$

3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic for capillary action in brazed lap joints.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Principal constituent materials used in soft soldering.
Find: Correct alloy combination.

Solution: 1. Soft solder is traditionally an alloy of tin (Sn) and lead (Pb). 2. Eutectic solder (63% Sn - 37% Pb) melts sharply at 183 °C. 3. Answer: Tin and lead.

Key Insight: Solder filler melts below 450 °C, while brazing spelter melts above 450 °C.

3.7 Welding Defects, Physics & Heat Input

1. Concept Pointers
  • Inadequate penetration (lack of penetration) forms sharp root stress raisers, leading to crack formation.
  • Undercut is caused by excessive welding current or incorrect electrode angle.
  • Weld spatter is a defect consisting of expelled droplets adhering to workpiece surface.
  • Porosity is caused by trapped gas pockets (mainly hydrogen dissolved in molten pool).
  • Residual stresses in fusion weld zone after cooling are predominantly tensile in weld bead and compressive in adjacent base metal regions.
  • Low-carbon steel $\approx 0.15\%\text{ C}$ exhibits the highest weldability among carbon steels.
  • Aluminium is difficult to weld due to its high thermal conductivity and rapid formation of a tenacious oxide film $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{H_{\text{input}} = \frac{V \cdot I}{v} \quad | \quad H_{\text{actual}} = \eta_{\text{heat}} \cdot \left$\frac{V \cdot I}{v}\right$ \quad | \quad \eta_{\text{melting}} = \frac{H_{\text{required}}}{H_{\text{actual}}}}$$

3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematics for undercut, porosity, and lack of penetration defects.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Arc welding of steel with voltage 20 V, current 200 A, travel speed 5 mm/s, heat-transfer efficiency 0.8, joint area $20\text{ mm}^2$, heat of melting $10\text{ J/mm}^3$.
Find: Melting efficiency.

Solution: 1. Heat supplied per unit length = $\frac{V \cdot I}{v} = \frac{20 \times 200}{5} = 800\text{ J/mm}$. 2. Actual heat input = $\eta_{\text{heat}} \times 800 = 0.8 \times 800 = 640\text{ J/mm}$. 3. Heat required for melting = Area $\times$ heat of melting = $20 \times 10 = 200\text{ J/mm}$. 4. Melting efficiency $\eta_{\text{melting}} = \frac{H_{\text{required}}}{H_{\text{actual}}} = \frac{200}{640} = 0.3125 \implies 31.25\%$. 5. Answer: 31.25%.

Key Insight: Distinguish heat-transfer efficiency (arc-to-workpiece) from melting efficiency (heat-to-melt vs actual heat input).

4. Consolidation Notes

Note on Resistance Welding vs Solid-State Welding: Resistance spot/seam welding relies on electrical resistance to melt a liquid nugget, so it is a fusion process. In contrast, friction, ultrasonic, forge, and explosive welding do not melt the base metal and are true solid-state processes. See Section 3.4 & 3.5.

Note on Brazing vs Soldering Threshold: Both processes rely on capillary flow without melting base metals. The sole distinguishing criterion tested in MCQs is filler melting temperature: below 450 °C is soldering (tin-lead), above 450 °C is brazing (copper-zinc/silver). See Section 3.6.

Note on Power Source Maximum Power: For linear V-I characteristics, maximum power is obtained at $I_{sc}/2$ and $V_{oc}/2$. Do not confuse short-circuit current with operating current for maximum power output. See Section 3.1.

5. Final Quick-Fire Checklist

  • Memorize linear V-I maximum power current: $I_{\text{max}} = I_{sc} / 2$.
  • Know flame gas ratios: Neutral $1:1$, Carburizing $\text{O}_2 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$, Oxidizing $\text{O}_2 > \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$.
  • Memorize flame temperatures: Oxy-acetylene $\approx 3200^\circ\text{C}$, Electric Arc $6000\text{--}7000^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Remember gas cylinder colors: Oxygen = Black, Acetylene = Maroon.
  • Memorize polarity: Straight (DCEN) = Electrode Negative; Reverse (DCEP) = Electrode Positive.
  • Remember Thermit ratio: Aluminium : Iron Oxide = $1 : 3$.
  • Fix joining temperature boundary: Soldering $< 450^\circ\text{C}$ (Tin-Lead), Brazing $> 450^\circ\text{C}$ (Copper-Zinc).
  • Know primary defect causes: Undercut $\leftarrow$ Excessive current; Cracks $\leftarrow$ Lack of penetration; Porosity $\leftarrow$ Hydrogen.

6. Welding, Brazing, Soldering & Adhesive Bonding — SSC JE Revision Notes

6.1 I. Welding Processes

6.1.1 SMAW (Shielded Metal Arc Welding)

Definition: Manual arc welding where a consumable electrode (coated rod) melts and fills the joint. The flux coating provides shielding gas and slag protection.

Key Facts:

  • Heat source: Electrical arc between electrode and base metal
  • Arc temperature: 3000–3500 K
  • Electrode melts and acts as filler material
  • Flux coating provides:
    • Shielding gas ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CO}$)
    • Slag layer (protects from oxidation)
    • Alloying elements ($\text{Mn}$, $\text{Si}$)

Penetration: Good (moderate depth)

Speed: Slow (electrode replacement needed frequently)

Cost: Very low (cheapest process)

Advantages:

  • Works in all positions (overhead, vertical, horizontal)
  • Minimal equipment needed
  • Works outdoors (wind doesn't blow away shielding)
  • Suitable for thick sections
  • Versatile materials (mild steel, stainless, cast iron)

Disadvantages:

  • Slow process
  • Slag removal required between passes
  • Operator skill critical
  • High spatter rate
  • Distortion risk in thin sections

Best For: Manual site work, repairs, diverse materials, structural steel

🎯 Exam Point: Flux provides shielding; slag removal needed after each pass

Electrode Types (AWS):

  • E6013: Low penetration, all-position, good for thin sheet
  • E7018: High strength, low hydrogen, structural steel

6.1.2 MIG/GMAW (Gas Metal Arc Welding)

Definition: Semi-automatic arc welding where a continuous wire electrode melts and fills the joint. External shielding gas provides protection.

Key Facts:

  • Wire fed continuously by motorized drive
  • Arc maintained between wire and base metal
  • Separate shielding gas ($\text{Ar}$, $\text{CO}_2$, or mix)
  • No slag produced

Penetration: Deep

Speed: Fast (continuous wire feed; high productivity)

Cost: Medium (gas + wire)

Shielding Gas Selection:

  • $\text{CO}_2$: Cost-effective, deeper penetration, steel only
  • Argon + 15–20% $\text{CO}_2$: Standard for mild steel, better arc stability
  • Pure Argon: Aluminum, stainless steel

Advantages:

  • High speed (fastest common process)
  • High deposition rate
  • Minimal spatter (with right settings)
  • No slag removal
  • Easy learning curve
  • Good for thin to medium sheet

Disadvantages:

  • Cannot weld outdoors (wind blows away gas)
  • Limited to flat/horizontal positions (gravity pulls wire)
  • High distortion in thin sections
  • Gas bottle required

Best For: Thin to medium sheet metal, production runs, automotive repair

🎯 Exam Point: Wire melts; continuous feed; external gas shielding

Typical Settings:

  • Current: 80–300 A
  • Voltage: 18–28 V
  • Travel speed: 300–600 mm/min

6.1.3 TIG/GTAW (Gas Tungsten Arc Welding)

Definition: Manual arc welding using a non-consumable tungsten electrode. Filler rod is fed manually. Inert gas provides shielding.

Key Facts:

  • Tungsten electrode does NOT melt (melting point $3400^\circ\text{C} \gg$ arc temp)
  • Filler rod supplied separately (optional for autogenous welding)
  • Cleanest weld (no flux, no slag)
  • Highest operator skill required

Arc Temperature: 3000–4500 K

Penetration: Precise, excellent (deepest if high current used)

Speed: Slow (manual filler control)

Cost: Very high (tungsten electrode, argon gas, skill)

Shielding Gas:

  • Pure Argon: Standard for Al, stainless, copper
  • Helium: Deeper penetration, higher cost
  • Ar + He mix: Balance of penetration and speed

Tungsten Electrode Types:

  • Pure W: General purpose
  • Thoriated W: Better arc starting, longer life
  • Ceriated W: Excellent starting, low radioactivity

Advantages:

  • Highest weld quality (precise, contamination-free)
  • Works with dissimilar metals
  • All positions possible
  • Minimal distortion (concentrated heat)
  • Best for thin sections and aluminum

Disadvantages:

  • Slow (manual filler rod + control)
  • High cost
  • High operator skill
  • Portable bottle-mounted gas required
  • Two-handed operation (awkward)

Best For: Precision work, aluminum, stainless steel, aerospace, medical tubes

🎯 Exam Point: Inert gas shielding; filler rod separate; tungsten electrode non-consumable

6.1.4 Resistance Welding (Spot, Seam, Projection)

Definition: Two metal sheets clamped together and melted at contact point by electrical resistance heat (Joule heating: $H = I^2 R t$).

Key Facts:

  • No arc; no filler metal
  • High current passed through contact resistance
  • Joint formed by pressure + heat
  • Electrodes clamp and heat simultaneously

Heat Source: Electrical resistance (Joule effect)

Temperature: 1200–1600 K (lowest among all welding)

Penetration: Localized (only at contact point)

Speed: Very fast (milliseconds per spot)

Cost: Medium (equipment expensive, consumables cheap)

Types:

Type Joint Electrode Uses
Spot Overlap Rounded tip Lap joints, car body
Seam Overlap Rotating wheel Continuous line, pressure vessels
Projection Overlap Embossed pins Battery tabs, sheet assembly

Process Parameters:

  • Current: 1000–10,000 A (very high)
  • Voltage: 2–10 V (very low)
  • Time: 0.1–1 second per spot
  • Pressure: 300–500 N/mm²

Advantages:

  • Extremely fast (mass production)
  • No filler needed
  • Minimal distortion
  • Repeatable and automated
  • Clean (no spatter, no flux)
  • No skilled labor needed

Disadvantages:

  • Lap joint only (overlap required)
  • Weld quality hard to inspect
  • Electrode wear (frequent replacement)
  • Limited to thin sections (<3 mm typically)
  • Surface indentation at weld spot
  • Corrosion risk (exposed lap joint)

Best For: Sheet metal, automotive, appliances, battery assembly

🎯 Exam Point: No filler; pressure + current; very fast; lap joints only

Formula:

$$\boxed{Q = I^2 \times R \times t}$$

where $R$ = resistance at contact point (depends on resistivity, area)

6.1.5 Gas Welding (Oxy-Acetylene)

Definition: Combustion of acetylene gas in oxygen produces a hot flame that melts base metal and filler rod.

Key Facts:

  • Fuel: Acetylene ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$) stored in cylinder with acetone
  • Oxidizer: Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$)
  • Flame temperature: ~3100 K
  • Filler rod melted separately (handheld)

Flame Types:

  • Neutral flame: Equal $\text{O}_2 + \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$ (used for most metals)
  • Oxidizing flame: Excess $\text{O}_2$ (blue, harsh—damages some metals)
  • Reducing/Carburizing flame: Excess $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$ (yellow—prevents oxidation of Al, Mg, Cu)

Penetration: Moderate

Speed: Slow (manual control)

Cost: Very low (cheapest if equipment available)

Advantages:

  • Works outdoors (no gas dispersion issue)
  • All positions
  • Good for thin sections
  • Portable (cylinders carried)
  • Versatile materials (steel, aluminum, copper)
  • Reducing flame prevents oxidation

Disadvantages:

  • Slowest process
  • Requires high operator skill
  • Large HAZ (heat affected zone)
  • Distortion risk
  • Fire/explosion hazard (acetylene)
  • Weak at high currents/speeds

Best For: Repair work, thin sections, small jobs, field work

🎯 Exam Point: Reducing flame prevents oxidation of sensitive metals

Typical Flame:

  • Acetylene regulator: 0.5–2 bar
  • Oxygen regulator: 1–5 bar
  • Adjust for neutral flame (no feather in center)

6.1.6 Submerged Arc Welding (SAW)

Definition: Arc burns under a blanket of granular flux. The flux melts and forms slag, which protects the weld and allows very high currents.

Key Facts:

  • Arc completely submerged under flux
  • Filler wire fed continuously
  • No visible arc (safe for eyes)
  • Automatic or semi-automatic

Arc Temperature: Very high (concentrated, high current)

Penetration: Deepest among manual/semi-automatic processes

Speed: Fast (high current possible)

Cost: Medium (flux + equipment)

Flux Role:

  • Protects molten metal from oxidation
  • Acts as thermal blanket (allows high current without spatter)
  • Adds alloying elements
  • Slag layer easily removed

Advantages:

  • Deepest penetration (ideal for thick plate)
  • Very high deposition rate
  • No spatter (submerged)
  • Clean weld (slag protection)
  • Easy automation
  • Suitable for high-current welding

Disadvantages:

  • Flat position only (flux not retained in other positions)
  • Thick flux cleanup
  • High heat input (more distortion)
  • Not portable (equipment stationary)

Best For: Heavy structural steel, ship building, pressure vessels, thick plates (>6 mm)

🎯 Exam Point: Flux submerges arc; no spatter; deepest penetration; flat position only

6.1.7 Laser/Electron Beam Welding

Definition: Concentrated beam of photons (laser) or electrons melts a narrow, deep joint with minimal HAZ.

Key Facts:

  • Energy concentrated in tiny spot (~0.1 mm)
  • Very high power density ($\text{MW/cm}^2$)
  • Minimal thermal distortion
  • Precise depth control

Arc Temperature: Very high (localized)

Penetration: Precise, very deep (aspect ratio 4:1 typical)

Speed: Fast

Cost: Very high (equipment >₹50 lakh; skilled technician)

Types:

  • $\text{CO}_2$ Laser: Industrial standard, air-cooled
  • YAG Laser: Fiber delivery, flexible
  • Electron Beam: Vacuum required, deepest penetration

Advantages:

  • Minimal HAZ (less material degradation)
  • Precise depth
  • High speed
  • Dissimilar metals possible
  • Automation-ready
  • No consumables (laser)

Disadvantages:

  • Very high capital cost
  • Requires precise fixturing
  • Operator skill (setup critical)
  • Beam absorption varies by material (Al reflects well)
  • Vacuum needed (electron beam only)
  • Gap tolerance tight (<0.5 mm)

Best For: Precision aerospace, automotive, medical devices, dissimilar metal joints

🎯 Exam Point: Concentrated beam; minimal HAZ; very precise; expensive

6.2 II. Brazing

Definition: Joining process where a filler metal (lower melting point than base) melts and flows into the joint by capillary action. Base metal does NOT melt.

Key Characteristic: Filler melts (~450–900°C) $\rightarrow$ Base metal stays solid $\rightarrow$ Metallurgical bond forms

Brazing Temp Range: 450–900°C

Filler Metals:

  • Copper-Zinc (Brass): 40–50% Zn, melting point ~900°C, most common
  • Silver Solder: Ag + Cu + Zn, melting point ~700–800°C, highest strength, expensive
  • Copper: Pure copper filler for copper-to-copper brazing

Joint Clearance: 0.05–0.1 mm (critical—too tight or loose fails)

Flux Requirement: YES (removes oxide from base surface; enables wetting)

Brazing Types:

Type Heat Source Temp Equipment Production
Torch Brazing Oxy-fuel torch 800–900°C Simple torch Manual, small parts
Furnace Brazing Electric furnace 700–900°C Furnace, controlled Batch, precision
Induction Brazing Induced current Variable Coil + generator Repeatable, fast
Dip Brazing Molten salt/metal bath Low Bath vessel Large area parts
Laser Brazing Laser beam Localized Laser system Precision, micro

Key Advantages over Welding:

  1. Lower temperature: No base metal distortion
  2. Dissimilar materials: Bonds Al to Cu, steel to Al (impossible in welding)
  3. Complex shapes: Filler flows into gaps
  4. Thin sections: Less heat damage
  5. Less skill: Simpler than welding
  6. Batch production: Multiple joints heated together

Key Disadvantages:

  1. Lower strength: Typically 60–70% of base metal strength
  2. Joint clearance critical: Too loose $\rightarrow$ weak, too tight $\rightarrow$ won't flow
  3. Cost: Filler expensive (especially silver solder)
  4. Flux cleanup: Corrosive flux must be removed
  5. Temperature sensitive: Some alloys degrade if overheated

Best For: Jewelry, precision instruments, dissimilar metal joints, aerospace components

🎯 Exam Points:
  • Base metal does NOT melt—filler bonds both surfaces
  • Capillary action draws filler into joint
  • Advantages: no distortion, dissimilar materials, lower cost than welding
  • Filler strength ~60–70% of base metal

6.3 III. Soldering

Definition: Joining process where a low-melting-point filler (solder) melts and forms a bond without melting the base metal. Primarily for electrical connections.

Soldering Temp Range: <450°C (soft: 200–300°C; hard: up to 450°C)

Filler Metals:

  • Soft Solder (most common):
    • Tin-Lead (Sn-Pb): 60% Sn + 40% Pb, melting point ~190°C
    • 63-37 Sn-Pb (eutectic), melting point ~183°C (flows smoothest)
    • Lead-free (96.5% Sn + 3.5% Ag): melting point ~221°C
    • Why Pb-free: Environmental regulations (RoHS)
  • Hard Solder: Copper or silver filler, 300–450°C melting point (not common in SSC JE)

Flux Requirement: YES (removes oxide; enables wetting)

  • Types: Rosin flux (non-corrosive), Zinc chloride flux (corrosive—cleanup required)

Soldering Types:

Type Method Temp Application
Iron Soldering Hot iron tip 300–350°C Manual PCB, wiring
Wave Soldering Molten solder bath 250–260°C Production PCBs (automated)
Reflow Soldering Oven heating 250–280°C Surface-mount components (SMD)
Dip Soldering Immersion in bath Controlled Large areas, connectors

Key Advantages:

  1. Very low temperature: No component damage (capacitors, ICs)
  2. Good electrical conductivity: Essential for circuits
  3. Fast: Quick joining, automation possible
  4. Low cost: Cheapest joining method
  5. Reversible: Easy to desolder and rework

Key Disadvantages:

  1. Weak mechanical strength: NOT for structural loads
  2. Temperature sensitive: Joint weak at high operating temp (>100°C)
  3. Corrosion risk: Flux residue if not cleaned
  4. Joint quality varies: Operator-dependent (cold solder joints fail)
  5. Creep: Solder deforms over years (reliability issue)

Best For: Electronics, PCB assembly, electrical wiring, plumbing

Soldering Quality (Defects):

  • Cold Solder Joint: Incomplete melting $\rightarrow$ high resistance $\rightarrow$ joint fails
  • Dry Joint: Solder ball not bonded $\rightarrow$ electrical open circuit
  • Voids/Bubbles: Flux vapor trapped $\rightarrow$ weak joint
🎯 Exam Points:
  • Solder melts, base metal does NOT
  • Temperature <450°C (soft solder ~200–300°C)
  • Primary use: electronics, not structural
  • Eutectic 63-37 Sn-Pb flows best (183°C melting)
  • Lead-free solder 96.5Sn-3.5Ag melts at ~221°C (higher than Pb-Sn)

6.4 IV. Adhesive Bonding

Definition: Joining surfaces using polymeric adhesive that hardens via chemical reaction, moisture absorption, or heat. No melting of base or filler.

Mechanism: Mechanical interlocking + van der Waals/hydrogen bonding

Temperature: Room temperature to mild heating (no arc, no high temp)

Joint Types: Primarily lap joints (overlapped surfaces)

Adhesive Types:

Adhesive Composition Cure Method Strength Best For
Epoxy Resin + Hardener (2-part) Chemical (exothermic) Very high Metals, composites, ceramics, aerospace
Cyanoacrylate Single component Moisture-activated Moderate Plastics, rubber, instant bonding
Polyurethane Polyol + Isocyanate (2-part) Moisture-cured (or heat) High Metals, wood, flexible joints, automotive
Acrylic Monomer + Peroxide (2-part) Self-initiating High Painted surfaces, metals
Silicone Polymer backbone (1 or 2-part) Moisture or heat Flexible (low shear) Seals, vibration isolation, thermal control

Surface Preparation (CRITICAL):

Proper surface prep is more important than adhesive type.

  1. Degrease: Remove oils, wax (solvent wash or alkaline bath)
  2. Mechanical abrasion: Roughen surface (sandpaper, grit blasting)
  3. Chemical treatment: Remove oxides (acid etch for Al, chromate conversion)
  4. Cleanliness: Avoid dust, moisture at application
  5. Timing: Use adhesive within recommended time after prep

Why critical? Oxide layer prevents molecular contact $\rightarrow$ poor wetting $\rightarrow$ bond fails

Joint Design:

Lap Joint Shear Stress:

$$\boxed{\tau = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Overlap Length} \times \text{Width}} = \frac{F}{L \times W}}$$

Stronger joints: Longer overlap, wider width, multiple adhesive layers

Typical overlap: 25–50 mm (more overlap = stronger, but diminishing returns)

Stress Distribution: Concentrated at edges (peel stress risk)

Multi-layer joints: Use film adhesive between layers for better stress distribution

Key Advantages:

  1. No thermal distortion: Low temperature (room temp)
  2. Dissimilar materials: Bonds plastics, composites, metals, ceramics
  3. Low cost: Cheaper than welding/brazing equipment
  4. Large area bonding: Adhesive spreads over entire overlap
  5. Weight savings: No fasteners (rivets, bolts)
  6. Vibration damping: Adhesive layer absorbs vibration
  7. Corrosion resistance: No metal-to-metal contact $\rightarrow$ no galvanic corrosion

Key Disadvantages:

  1. Strength limited: Lower than welding (~30–60 MPa typical vs. 250+ MPa welding)
  2. Temperature sensitive: Strength drops at high temp (above 80–150°C depending on adhesive)
  3. Moisture degradation: Many adhesives weaken if exposed to water
  4. Cure time: Slow (hours to days) vs. instant mechanical fastening
  5. Gap filling poor: Requires thin, uniform bondline (typically <0.5 mm)
  6. Inspection difficult: Joint strength hidden (NDT needed)
  7. Environmental factors: UV, thermal cycling degrade bond over time
  8. Skill needed: Surface prep is tedious and critical

Failure Modes:

  1. Adhesive Failure: Glue breaks internally (cohesive failure of adhesive)
  2. Adhesive-Substrate Failure: Glue peels from surface (poor wetting or prep)
  3. Substrate Failure: Base material tears (joint actually strong)
  4. Peel Failure: Stress concentrated at edges (bending moment)

Best For:

  • Aerospace structures (composites, weight-critical)
  • Automotive (lightweight, noise damping)
  • Electronics (low-temp bonding)
  • Medical devices (no contamination risk)
  • Composite assembly (Carbon-fiber, glass-fiber laminates)
  • Plastic/rubber seals and gaskets
🎯 Exam Points:
  • No melting of base or filler—adhesive hardens via chemical/moisture
  • Surface preparation is CRITICAL (oxides prevent bonding)
  • Primary limitation: lower strength, temperature sensitivity, moisture
  • Epoxy strongest (~30–50 MPa shear); suitable for metals and composites
  • Lap joint shear stress = Load / (Overlap $\times$ Width)
  • Adhesive failure vs. substrate failure vs. peel failure

6.6 VI. Highest-Frequency SSC JE MCQs (Revision Focus)

Setup your flashcard deck with these:

  1. "Why is TIG preferred for aluminum?" $\rightarrow$ Inert gas prevents oxidation; no flux inclusion
  2. "Spot welding uses ___?" $\rightarrow$ Pressure + high current; no filler
  3. "SAW disadvantage ___?" $\rightarrow$ Flat position only; high distortion
  4. "Oxy-acetylene reducing flame prevents ___?" $\rightarrow$ Oxidation of Al, Mg, Cu
  5. "Brazing joint clearance ___?" $\rightarrow$ 0.05–0.1 mm (capillary action)
  6. "Solder 63-37 melts at ___?" $\rightarrow$ ~183°C (eutectic point)
  7. "Adhesive bonding best for ___?" $\rightarrow$ Composites, plastics, dissimilar metals
  8. "Cold solder joint causes ___?" $\rightarrow$ High resistance, intermittent electrical failure
  9. "SMAW electrode E7018 means ___?" $\rightarrow$ 70,000 psi, all-position, low hydrogen
  10. "MIG outside in wind fails because ___?" $\rightarrow$ Gas blown away; no shielding
