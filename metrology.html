Metrology & Measurement

1. Topic Overview

This guide covers Metrology & Measurement for AP Genco AE under standards of measurement, limits, fits, tolerances, linear measurement, angular measurement, comparators, and surface roughness. The most frequent PYQ areas are limits/fits/tolerances, Taylor’s principle, gauges, slip gauges, sine bar, LVDT, comparators, and accuracy versus precision. The formulas, definitions, and worked examples below are consolidated for fast revision with minimal repetition.

2. Quick Reference Sheet

2.1 All Formulas

Fundamental tolerance unit

$$\boxed{i = 0.45\sqrt[3]{D} + 0.001D}$$
Symbol Meaning Unit
(i) fundamental tolerance unit $\mu m$
(D) geometric mean diameter of the size range mm

Common IT grades

$$\boxed{\text{IT6} \approx 10i,\quad \text{IT7} = 16i,\quad \text{IT8} = 25i}$$

Fit relations

$$\boxed{\text{Minimum clearance} = \text{LLH} - \text{HLS}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Maximum clearance} = \text{HLH} - \text{LLS}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Minimum interference} = \text{HLS} - \text{LLH}}$$
Symbol Meaning
LLH lower limit of hole
HLH higher limit of hole
HLS higher limit of shaft
LLS lower limit of shaft

Sine bar relation

$$\boxed{\sin\theta = \frac{h}{L}}$$
$$\boxed{h = L\sin\theta}$$
Symbol Meaning Unit
$\theta$ angle to be measured or set degree or radian
(h) slip-gauge stack height mm
(L) centre distance between sine-bar rollers mm

Least count formulas

$$\boxed{\text{LC (vernier)} = 1\,\text{MSD} - 1\,\text{VSD}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{LC (micrometer)} = \frac{\text{Pitch of screw}}{\text{Number of thimble divisions}}}$$
Symbol Meaning
MSD main scale division
VSD vernier scale division

Surface roughness

$$\boxed{R_a = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L |y|\,dx}$$
Symbol Meaning
(R_a) arithmetic average roughness
(L) sampling length
(y) ordinate from mean line

Best-size wire for effective diameter of thread

$$\boxed{d = \frac{P}{2}\sec\frac{\alpha}{2}}$$
Symbol Meaning Unit
(d) best-size wire diameter mm
(P) thread pitch mm
$\alpha$ thread semi-angle degree

Gauge tolerance practice

$$\boxed{\text{Gauge tolerance} \approx 10\%\ \text{of workpiece tolerance}}$$

2.2 Key Definitions & Master Concepts

  • Basic size: Nominal size from which limits are derived.
  • Tolerance: Total permissible variation in size, equal to upper limit minus lower limit.
  • Allowance: Intentional difference between mating parts, equal to minimum clearance or maximum interference.
  • Fundamental deviation: Distance from the basic size to the tolerance zone.
  • Hole-basis system: System in which the hole is kept at basic size and its lower deviation is zero.
  • Shaft-basis system: System in which the shaft is kept at basic size and its upper deviation is zero.
  • Clearance fit: Fit in which the hole zone lies entirely above the shaft zone, so clearance is always present.
  • Interference fit: Fit in which the shaft zone lies entirely above the hole zone, so interference is always present.
  • Transition fit: Fit in which tolerance zones overlap, so the joint may have either clearance or interference.
  • Taylor’s principle: GO gauge checks all related dimensions simultaneously at maximum material condition, while NO-GO gauge checks one dimension at a time at least material condition.
  • Wringing: Joining slip gauges by molecular attraction and pressure, without adhesive.
  • Comparator: Instrument that compares a dimension against a standard rather than giving absolute measurement.
  • LVDT: Electrical transducer that measures linear displacement through change in mutual inductance.
  • Accuracy: Closeness of a measured value to the true value.
  • Precision: Repeatability or consistency of repeated measurements.
  • Ra: Arithmetic average of absolute deviations from the mean line.
  • Autocollimator: Optical instrument for measuring small angular deviations.
  • Optical flat: Flatness-checking device using interference fringes with monochromatic light.

2.3 Standard Values / Constants

  • IT grades: IT01 to IT18 are used in tolerance systems.
  • Common practical grades: IT6, IT7, and IT8 are frequently asked in PYQs.
  • Slip gauge sets: 83-piece, 112-piece, and 122-piece sets are common.
  • Common vernier least count: 0.02 mm.
  • Common micrometer least count: 0.01 mm.
  • Sine bar centre distances: 100 mm, 200 mm, and 300 mm are commonly used.
  • Wear allowance: Usually provided only on the GO gauge.
  • Surface roughness symbols: Grade marks such as fine-finish symbols are frequently asked in identification questions.

3. Main Body

3.1 Limits, Fits, Tolerances and Allowance

1. Concept Pointers
  • Tolerance is the permissible variation of a single dimension; allowance is the intentional difference between mating parts.
  • Basic size is the nominal size, and all upper/lower limits are measured from this reference.
  • Fundamental deviation positions the tolerance zone relative to the basic size.
  • Hole-basis systems are preferred in practice because the hole is kept at basic size.
  • Shaft-basis systems keep the shaft at basic size and are used when that arrangement is more convenient.
  • Clearance fit always gives a gap, interference fit always gives an overlap, and transition fit may give either.
  • Higher IT grade number means a larger tolerance zone, not a tighter one.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{i = 0.45\sqrt[3]{D} + 0.001D}$$
$$\boxed{\text{IT6} \approx 10i,\quad \text{IT7} = 16i,\quad \text{IT8} = 25i}$$

Conditions and usage

  • (D) is the geometric mean diameter of the size range.
  • IT grades define tolerance magnitude for the chosen basic size.
  • The sign and position of fundamental deviation decide whether the hole or shaft zone lies above or below the basic size.
3. Diagram


Tolerance zones of hole and shaft. Clearance fit means the hole zone stays above the shaft zone, interference fit means the shaft zone stays above the hole zone, and transition fit means the zones overlap. Source: Engineering Arena.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Hole = (40^{+0.06}{+0.02}) mm, Shaft = (40^{-0.06}{-0.08}) mm.
Find: Minimum size of the hole.

Solution:

  1. For a hole, the minimum size is the lower limit.
  2. Lower limit of the hole = (40 + 0.02 = 40.02) mm.
  3. Answer: 40.02 mm.

Key Insight: For a hole, the lower limit gives the minimum size; for a shaft, the upper limit gives the maximum material condition. A common trap is reversing these limits.

3.2 Taylor’s Principle and Limit Gauges

1. Concept Pointers
  • Taylor’s principle states that the GO gauge checks all related dimensions simultaneously, while the NO-GO gauge checks only one feature at a time.
  • GO gauge corresponds to maximum material condition, and NO-GO gauge corresponds to least material condition.
  • Wear allowance is provided only on the GO gauge.
  • Plug gauges are used for internal dimensions, while snap or ring gauges are used for external dimensions.
  • Feeler gauges are used for small clearances and gaps.
  • Thread gauges are available in GO/NO-GO forms for threads.
2. Formulas

No single numerical formula governs Taylor’s principle, but the design practice is summarized below.

$$\boxed{\text{Gauge tolerance} \approx 10\%\ \text{of workpiece tolerance}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • GO plug gauges are designed to check the full form of the hole.
  • NO-GO gauges are shorter or otherwise arranged so they do not test the full form simultaneously.
  • Progressive gauges may combine GO and NO-GO on the same gauge body.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Hole limits are 25.00 to 25.02 mm.
Find: GO and NO-GO plug gauge sizes.

Solution:

  1. GO gauge corresponds to the maximum material condition of the hole, so it is made to the lower limit.
  2. NO-GO gauge corresponds to the least material condition of the hole, so it is made to the upper limit.
  3. Answer: GO = 25.00 mm, NO-GO = 25.02 mm.

Key Insight: GO must enter the part, and NO-GO must not. Wear allowance is relevant only for the GO gauge, which is a frequent PYQ trap.

3.3 Linear Measurement Instruments

1. Concept Pointers
  • Slip gauges are end standards used as reference blocks for precision measurement.
  • Wringing joins slip gauges by molecular attraction and pressure, not by adhesive.
  • Vernier calipers are used for external, internal, and depth measurements.
  • Micrometers provide finer resolution and use a ratchet stop for uniform measuring force.
  • Comparators measure deviation from a standard, not an absolute dimension.
  • LVDT converts linear displacement into an electrical output through mutual induction.
  • Optical flats use interference fringes to check flatness.
  • Autocollimators are used for very small angular deviations rather than linear length measurement.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{\text{LC (vernier)} = 1\,\text{MSD} - 1\,\text{VSD}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{LC (micrometer)} = \frac{\text{Pitch of screw}}{\text{Number of thimble divisions}}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • Lower least count means higher resolving power.
  • Ratchet stop avoids excessive measuring pressure on the job.
  • Slip-gauge wringing requires clean, dry surfaces.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: Main-scale reading = 5.5 mm, thimble reading = 28 divisions, least count = 0.01 mm.
Find: Total micrometer reading.

Solution:

  1. Thimble contribution = $28 \times 0.01 = 0.28$ mm.
  2. Total reading = (5.5 + 0.28 = 5.78) mm.
  3. Answer: 5.78 mm.

Key Insight: The reading is always main-scale reading plus thimble reading multiplied by least count. The ratchet is there to standardize pressure and reduce error.

3.4 Angular Measurement - Sine Bar

1. Concept Pointers
  • A sine bar is a precision instrument used to set or measure angles indirectly.
  • It consists of a hardened bar with two precision rollers at a known centre distance.
  • Slip gauges are placed under one roller to generate the required height.
  • The measurement is based on the sine relation between height and centre distance.
  • Sine bars are widely used for taper checks and angle setting.
  • Autocollimators are used for measuring very small angular deviations with high sensitivity.
  • Clinometers are used for inclination or angular measurement, not linear measurement.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{\sin\theta = \frac{h}{L}}$$
$$\boxed{h = L\sin\theta}$$

Conditions and usage

  • The two rollers must be equal in diameter and parallel.
  • The bar is most reliable when used within a practical angle range, commonly around the middle of its measuring range.
  • The height (h) is obtained by slip-gauge stacking.
3. Diagram


Sine bar on a surface plate with slip gauges under one roller creating height (h). Source: ElectricalWorkbook.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: $\theta = 30^\circ$, $L = 100$ mm.
Find: Required slip-gauge height (h).

Solution:

  1. Use $h = L\sin\theta$.
  2. $h = 100 \times \sin 30^\circ = 100 \times 0.5 = 50$ mm.
  3. Answer: 50 mm.

Key Insight: The sine bar does not measure angle directly; it converts an angular problem into a height problem using slip gauges. For 30 degrees, the height is exactly half the bar length.

3.5 Surface Roughness and Other Precision Instruments

1. Concept Pointers
  • Surface roughness parameters commonly asked in exams are Ra, Rz, and Rq.
  • Ra is the arithmetic average roughness, and higher Ra means a rougher surface.
  • Roughness symbols indicate the required finish grade on drawings.
  • Stylus instruments such as Talysurf are used for roughness measurement.
  • Optical flats are used for flatness checks by interference fringes.
  • Strain gauges work by change in electrical resistance due to strain and are usually connected in a Wheatstone bridge.
  • LVDT is a displacement transducer and may be used inside other measurement systems.
  • Accuracy and precision are different ideas and should not be confused.
2. Formulas

For quick lookup, see Quick Reference Section 2.1.

$$\boxed{R_a = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L |y|\,dx}$$
$$\boxed{d = \frac{P}{2}\sec\frac{\alpha}{2}}$$

Conditions and usage

  • Ra is the average absolute deviation from the mean line over the sampling length.
  • The best-size wire formula is used in effective diameter calculations for threads.
  • Higher surface roughness value means poorer finish, which is a frequent conceptual trap.
3. Diagram

No suitable diagram found; refer to standard textbook schematic.

4. Worked Example

Worked Example 1

Given: A surface has higher Ra than another surface.
Find: Which surface is smoother?

Solution:

  1. Lower Ra means smaller average deviation from the mean line.
  2. Therefore the surface with lower Ra is smoother.
  3. Answer: The surface with lower Ra is smoother.

Key Insight: Students often reverse the trend and assume higher Ra means smoother. The correct relation is the opposite: higher Ra means rougher surface.

4. Consolidation Notes

Note on slip gauges and sine bar:
Slip gauges are not just a separate linear-measurement tool; they are also the standard height-setting blocks used in sine-bar angle measurement. See Section 3.3 for slip gauges and Section 3.4 for the angle-setting application.

Note on LVDT:
LVDT is introduced as a linear displacement transducer, but it also appears in broader comparator and transducer questions. Its core idea remains displacement-to-electrical conversion through mutual induction. See Quick Reference Section 2.2 and Section 3.3.

Note on comparator usage:
A comparator does not replace an absolute measuring instrument. It indicates deviation from a standard, which is why it is usually paired with slip gauges or another master reference. See Section 3.3.

Note on accuracy and precision:
These terms are often mixed up in PYQs. Accuracy is closeness to the true value, while precision is consistency of repeated readings. See Quick Reference Section 2.2.

5. Final Quick-Fire Checklist

  • Memorize the tolerance unit formula: $i = 0.45\sqrt[3]{D} + 0.001D$.
  • Remember the fit types: clearance, interference, and transition.
  • Know Taylor’s principle: GO checks full form, NO-GO checks one feature at a time.
  • Fix the sine-bar relation: $\sin\theta = h/L$.
  • Recall common least counts: vernier 0.02 mm and micrometer 0.01 mm.
  • Keep the roughness rule straight: higher Ra means rougher surface.
  • Remember that comparator means comparison with a standard, not absolute measurement.
